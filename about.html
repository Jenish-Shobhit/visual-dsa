Why this guide exists

Understanding is a sequence of questions.

A diagram can make a step visible. A prediction, an awkward input, and a proof make the idea yours.

01 / The method

See it, then change it.

Visual DSA begins before the usual algorithm vocabulary. The first figure has only an instruction pointer, a remembered value, and an output. From there the path earns decisions, loops, functions, references, arrays, invariants, and cost. When a later lesson says “queue” or “recursive call,” you have already watched the underlying behavior happen.

  1. Predict. Before pressing Next, say what you think the state will be.
  2. Trace. Step forward or backward. The caption says what was read, written, or ruled out.
  3. Change the input. Try empty, repeated, sorted, reversed, and missing cases. A good idea should survive them.
  4. Explain why. Name what stays true, why the process stops, and how its work grows.

The three practice problems in each guided lesson deliberately have no solutions on the page. They are a chance to transfer the idea to a new case before you look elsewhere.

02 / Two depths

One path, then room to go deep.

The 17 guided lessons form the beginner spine. Each stays focused on one major idea and includes an interactive trace. The 24 deep studies go further: amortization, lower bounds, balanced trees, range queries, weighted paths, greedy proofs, flow, matching, tries, hardness, approximation, and randomness.

The long studies have worked derivations, figures, and problem sets. Start them after the guided path, or use one as a reference when you want to understand a topic beyond its first mechanism. A separate graph route explorer lets you compare BFS and Dijkstra on the same weighted network.

An animation is a beginning. The goal is to know what the next frame must be, and why.

03 / The project

Open, static, and independent.

The guide is plain HTML, CSS, and JavaScript. It works on GitHub Pages without an account or build step. The browser keeps a local list of lesson filenames you have visited to help you find your place; no learner input is sent to a server.

The long studies adapt the author's local DSA lecture notes. Charles Petzold’s Code and Arpaci-Dusseau and Arpaci-Dusseau’s Operating Systems: Three Easy Pieces helped shape the early explanations. Published book text and figures were not copied. The source and model notes explain the teaching simplifications, and the repository README explains how to run and contribute.
