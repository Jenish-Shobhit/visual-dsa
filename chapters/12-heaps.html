Heaps.

Keep the highest-priority value at the top by repairing one path at a time.

About 7 minutes · Use ← → in the figure

You have tasks with priorities [8, 5, 7, 2, 4, 6]. The highest number should be easy to get. A max heap keeps a simple rule: each parent is at least as large as its children. Here, 8 is at the top.

index:  0  1  2  3  4  5
value:  8  5  7  2  4  6

The heap is a complete binary tree, filled level by level from left to right. Its array form avoids storing explicit links. For an item at index i, its parent is at floor((i - 1) / 2); its children are at 2i + 1 and 2i + 2.

Insert 9 at the next free position, index 6. Its parent at index 2 holds 7, so swap them. Its new parent at index 0 holds 8, so swap again. Now 9 is at the top. Step through the figure and check the parent rule after each move.

Only the top is guaranteed.

A heap is not a fully sorted array. Siblings 5 and 8 can be in either order while the parent rule holds. Reading the top takes O(1). Inserting or removing the top may move a value along the tree's height, O(log n) steps.

To remove the maximum, replace the root with the last value, remove the last slot, then sift that value down by swapping with its larger child until the rule returns. A priority queue uses this to serve the most urgent item next. Heap sort uses repeated removals to order all values.
