What does “shortest” mean?

One graph. Two correct algorithms. Two different answers, because they measure different things.

← All labsLesson: BFS & DFS

Suppose you want to travel from A to F. A direct road reaches F in one edge, but its weight is 14. A → B → C → F takes three edges whose weights add to 6.

Fewest edgesA → F1 edge · total weight 14
Least total weightA → B → C → F3 edges · total weight 6

Breadth-first search (BFS) explores one edge farther at a time. It finds the path with the fewest edges. Dijkstra's algorithm expands the cheapest known total weight next. It finds the least-weight path when edge weights are non-negative. Change the endpoints, then step through both on the same graph.

Why the answers differ.

BFS uses a queue. A newly discovered node enters at the back, so all one-edge routes are considered before two-edge routes. The first route it finds to a node has the fewest edges. It treats a weight-14 road and a weight-2 road as one edge each.

Dijkstra replaces that queue rule with a priority rule: choose the unsettled node with the lowest known total weight. When the chosen node becomes final, non-negative edge weights ensure a later detour cannot make it cheaper. The orange direct edge initially offers F at 14; the route through B and C improves that estimate to 6.

The model uses an undirected graph with positive weights. Negative edges break Dijkstra's finalization argument. If every edge has equal weight, the two methods agree on the cost ordering and BFS is enough.
