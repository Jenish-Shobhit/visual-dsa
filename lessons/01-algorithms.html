What is an algorithm?

A list of steps so exact that a machine which understands nothing can follow it, and get the right answer every time.

  • Beginner
  • No prerequisites

Be the computer

Seven cards lie face down. Each hides a number. Your job: find the largest one. Click a card to turn it over.

Did you turn over every card? You had to. A card still face down might hide something bigger, so there is no safe shortcut.

Look at what you did. You kept one number in mind, the largest so far. You compared each new card with it. You swapped only when the new card was bigger. That habit is precise enough for a machine to follow.

Definition

An algorithm is a finite list of precise steps that turns an input (here, seven cards) into an output (the largest number). Anyone, or anything, that follows the steps exactly gets the same answer.

Precise enough for a machine

A recipe looks like an algorithm: steps, in order. But a recipe is written for a cook, who fills every gap with experience. A machine has no experience, so every step must say exactly what to do.

Press play and watch a robot cook read two versions of the same recipe.

Analogy: a recipe for someone who knows nothing

Write each step so that a stranger who has never cooked, and who takes every word literally, still gets pancakes. That stranger is a computer.

Where the analogy breaks

Even the exact recipe depends on the world: flour and pans vary. Algorithms work on symbols such as numbers, where “exactly” is truly possible: 9 > 5 is true every single time.

Your turn. Here are the card steps from the first figure, in plain English. One step would leave a machine stuck.

  1. Turn over the first card and hold it.
  2. Turn over the next card.
  3. If it is bigger than the card you hold, hold it instead.
  4. Keep going for a while.
  5. Say the number on the card you hold.

Input, steps, output

Every algorithm has the same shape. An input goes in, the steps run, an output comes out. The steps never change. The input does.

Pick an input and watch it go through the machine. Try the negative numbers, and the single card.

Key idea

An algorithm solves a whole problem, not one example. The problem is “given any list of at least one number, say the largest”. Each list you feed it is one input. The algorithm must work for all of them.

State: where you are, what you know

While an algorithm runs, two things change: which step comes next, and what you know so far. Together they are the state.

On the left are the six steps. The arrow is the next-step pointer: it marks the step running now. On the right is everything else you are keeping track of. Step through with → and watch one thing change at a time.

Definition

The state is everything you would need to write down to pause and carry on later: which step is next, which card you are on, and the best card so far.

That is all “running an algorithm” means: a state, changing one step at a time, until a step says stop. A computer does exactly this, billions of times a second.

Three building blocks

Look again at the six steps. Some simply follow each other. Some ask a question and pick a path. One sends you back to repeat. Those three moves are all you need.

Find the largest card uses all three. Click a building block to light up where it appears.

Only three

In 1966 Corrado Böhm and Giuseppe Jacopini proved that sequence, choice and repetition are enough to write any algorithm at all. Any program can be rewritten using only these three moves.

The same steps as a flowchart

A flowchart draws the steps as boxes and the order as arrows. A diamond asks a yes-or-no question and has two ways out. Follow the glowing token: it always travels to the step that runs next.

Right every time, and it stops

An algorithm must give the right output for every valid input, not only the ones you tried. And it must finish. One wrong answer, or one run that never ends, and it is not a correct algorithm.

This test bench runs a version of find-the-largest on six tricky inputs. Switch to a buggy version and see which tests catch it.

The “stops too early” bug passes five of six tests. Tests can prove an algorithm wrong, but never right. To know it is right, you need a reason that covers every input.

Why the correct version is right

After every step, this stays true: best is the largest of the cards turned over so far. It is true after the first card, and each comparison keeps it true. When no cards are left, “so far” means “all of them”.

Why it stops: each trip around the loop turns over one more card, so the count of face-down cards goes down by one every time. Seven cards mean at most six more trips. A loop that forgets to move on never runs out of work.

Same answer, different work

Two correct algorithms can do very different amounts of work. Play a game: a secret number hides somewhere from 1 to 100. After each guess you hear “higher”, “lower” or “got it”.

Player one counts up: 1, 2, 3 and so on. Player two always guesses the middle of what is left, so every answer throws away half. Choose a secret and race them.

One race can be lucky. This chart plays every secret from 1 to 100. Counting up needs as many guesses as the secret itself. Halving never needs more than 7. Click a column to race that secret.

Now let the range grow. Halving needs at most 10 guesses for 1 to 1,000 and at most 20 for a million. Counting up can need as many guesses as there are numbers.

A 2,300-year-old algorithm

Algorithms are far older than computers. Around 300 BCE, Euclid described one for the greatest common divisor (GCD) of two numbers: the largest number that divides both exactly. The GCD of 21 and 12 is 3.

It has a picture. Take a w × h rectangle and cut off the biggest square you can. Do it again to the piece that is left. When the leftover piece is itself a square, its side is the GCD.

Why does it work? A square tiles a w × h rectangle exactly when its side divides both w and h. Cutting an h × h square off leaves a (w − h) × h piece, and the same squares tile it. So the answer never changes while the rectangle shrinks, and the rectangle cannot shrink forever.

Faster Euclid

Try 17 × 1: sixteen thin cuts. Cutting all the same-size squares at once is the same as taking a remainder (17 mod 1 = 0), and that version never needs more than five steps per digit of the smaller number.

History

      

        
c. 825the word

        
Muhammad ibn Musa al-KhwarizmiBaghdad, on calculating with Hindu numerals

        

          
The word “algorithm” comes from al-Khwarizmi. His book taught arithmetic with Hindu numerals as step-by-step recipes anyone could follow, and its Latin translation opens “Dixit Algorizmi”: thus spoke al-Khwarizmi.

          
The oldest algorithm still in daily use is older still: Euclid’s, from about 300 BCE. In 1843 Ada Lovelace published a table of steps for Charles Babbage’s unbuilt Analytical Engine. In operation 4 the divisor and dividend are the wrong way round (v5 ÷ v4 should be v4 ÷ v5): often called the first computer bug.
