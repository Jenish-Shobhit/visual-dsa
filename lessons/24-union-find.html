Union-find

Keep track of which things belong together while groups keep merging. Each group is a tree that points at its root, and two tricks make the question “are these two connected?” cost almost nothing.

  • 16 min
  • Intermediate
  • Needs: arrays, trees

The problem

You run a social network. People become friends one at a time, and all day long the app asks: “Are Ana and Ivy connected through friends of friends?” Two people are in the same group when a chain of friendships links them, however long.

Build some groups yourself. Click one person, then another, to make them friends. Watch what a new friendship does to the groups, and ask the question at the bottom.

A search through the friendship list can answer the question, but it costs a walk through every friendship each time. With millions of people and thousands of questions per second, that is too slow. You do not need the route, only a yes or no.

The dynamic connectivity problem

Keep a set of items in groups. Support two operations forever, in any order: union merges two groups, and find says which group an item is in. Union-find answers both in almost constant time, and it never needs to know how two items are connected.

Build the intuition

Give every group a captain. Everyone remembers one person: their boss, who is either the captain or someone closer to the captain. To find your captain, ask your boss, then ask your boss’s boss, and so on until you meet someone who is their own boss.

Two people are in the same group exactly when they name the same captain. To merge two groups, one captain simply starts reporting to the other. Nobody else has to be told.

Analogy: chains of command

Merging groups is cheap because only one person changes their boss. Asking “who is my captain?” costs one question per level of the chain, so the whole game is to keep chains short.

Where the analogy breaks

In a company, a boss knows their reports. Here nobody does: every element stores only its own parent. That is why the structure is tiny, and why you cannot list a group’s members without scanning everything.

A forest in an array

Draw the “boss” of each element as an arrow and you get a forest: one tree per group, with the captain, called the root, at the top. The computer stores all of it in a single array: parent[i] is the boss of element i.

Definition

An element is a root when parent[i] = i: it points to itself, so nobody is above it. The small curl on a node shows exactly that. Every element starts as a root of a group of one.

Step through a few unions. The tree and the array change together: one union changes exactly one number in the array.

Find: climb to the root

find(x) answers “which group is x in?” by returning the group’s root. Start at x and hop from each element to its parent until you reach an element that points to itself. Every hop follows one pointer, and the number of hops is the cost.

“Are a and b connected?” becomes find(a) === find(b). Two elements share a group exactly when their climbs end at the same root.

Union: link the two roots

union(a, b) first finds the two roots, then makes one root the parent of the other. That is one pointer written, however large the groups are. If both finds return the same root, the elements are already together and nothing changes.

Notice what gets linked: the roots 3 and 6, not the elements 0 and 6 you were given. That detail is the most common bug in a first implementation.

The trap: tall chains

The plain rule “hang the first root under the second” looks harmless. But feed it union(0,1), union(1,2), union(2,3) and so on, and every new root goes on top of the old tree. The result is a chain, and the first find pays for every link.

Cost of the naive rule

A chain of n elements makes a find cost n − 1 hops. Run n operations and you have a quadratic algorithm, no better than searching the friendship list. Two small ideas fix this, and they work independently.

Fix 1: union by rank

The problem was hanging a tall tree under a short one, which makes everything in the tall tree one level deeper. Do the opposite: hang the shorter tree under the taller root. Store a number called rank on each root, an upper bound on its tree’s height, so you can compare without measuring.

Only when two trees of equal rank meet does the result grow: it becomes one level taller, so its rank goes up by one. The small badge on a root is its rank.

Why the height stays small

A root of rank r always has at least 2r elements below and including it: rank 1 needs two equal singletons, rank 2 needs two rank-1 trees of at least 2 each, and so on. With n elements, 2r ≤ n, so every rank, and every find, is at most log2 n.

That bound is tight. Merge equal trees in rounds, pairs then quadruples then eights, and you build the tallest tree union by rank allows: height log2 16 = 4 for sixteen elements.

Fix 2: path compression

A find that climbs a long path has just learned the root for every node on it. Use that: after reaching the root, walk the path again and point each node straight at the root. The next find from any of them is one hop.

Pick a variant. Full compression re-points every node on the path. Path halving re-points every other node to its grandparent in a single pass, which is about as good and needs no second walk.

Key idea

Compression does not change who is connected to whom, only how directly. It pays for itself: the walk you already made doubles as the flattening. Long paths are rare, and each one gets destroyed the first time somebody walks it.

The last row is the headline: O(m·α(n)) for m operations, where α is the inverse Ackermann function. It grows so slowly that α(n) is at most 4 for any number of elements you could ever store. Robert Tarjan proved in 1975 that this is exactly the right bound, and in 1989 Fredman and Saks proved that no such structure can beat it.

Rank is not height

After path compression the real height of a tree can be much smaller than its rank. Rank is only an upper bound, and you never lower it. Do not try to recompute it: the analysis depends on rank staying a bound that only grows.

Where does the inverse Ackermann function come from?

The Ackermann function grows faster than any tower of powers. Here is one standard version, Ak(1): 2, 3, 7, 2047, and then A4(1) = A3(2047), a tower of powers of 2 about two thousand levels high. Even one step of that tower, A2(2047) = 22059 − 1, already has 620 digits. The inverse α(n) is the smallest k with Ak(1) ≥ n. So α(n) is 3 for n from 8 to 2047, and 4 for every n from 2048 up to A4(1). That is far beyond the roughly 1080 atoms in the observable universe. Tarjan’s proof uses a potential function on ranks to show that compression “spends” each unit of slack only α(n) times.

Application: percolation

Pour water on a porous rock. It reaches the bottom only if open sites form an unbroken path from top to bottom. Open random sites one at a time and ask, after each: does water get through? The percolation question is a connectivity question, and union-find answers it after every single opening.

Trick: add two virtual nodes, TOP above the first row and BOTTOM below the last, and union each open site in the first or last row with them. The grid percolates exactly when TOP and BOTTOM have the same root.

Backwash

Once the grid percolates, BOTTOM is connected to every open site in the last row. If “is this site full of water?” asked the same structure, every dry bottom site would suddenly look wet. Use a second structure without the BOTTOM node to answer that question; the picture above does.

Repeat the experiment many times on a bigger grid. The chance of percolating jumps from almost 0 to almost 1 in a narrow band around 59 % open sites, a real phase transition, discovered with exactly this structure.

Application: finding a cycle

Is an undirected graph a forest, or does some edge close a loop? Take the edges one at a time. If both ends of the next edge already have the same root, they were connected before, so this edge closes a cycle. Otherwise it joins two groups: union them and move on.

Key idea

An edge is redundant exactly when its ends are already connected. That one test is the whole of Kruskal’s algorithm for minimum spanning trees (lesson 30): sort the edges, and keep an edge only when union succeeds.

Variations and patterns

Whenever a problem says “connected”, “same group”, “merge” or “does this edge make a cycle”, reach for union-find. Three more places it hides:

To connect every city as cheaply as possible, sort the roads by cost and add each one unless union says it would close a loop. With union-find the loop test is nearly free, so the sort dominates.


        
Image segmentation and “number of islands” do the same thing: union every pair of touching cells with the same value, then each root names a region. It works even when cells arrive one at a time, which flood fill cannot do cheaply.

        
Give each account a number. Walk through all e-mails, and union an account with the account that first owned the same address. Each final root is one person.

History

      

        
1964first published

        
Bernard Galler & Michael Fischer“An improved equivalence algorithm”

        

          
Union-find was invented to compile FORTRAN. Its EQUIVALENCE statement lets several names share one storage location, and in 1964 Galler and Fischer tracked the groups of names as trees of parent pointers. In 1975 Robert Tarjan proved that with union by rank and path compression each operation costs O(α(n)), a function that stays at 4 or below for any input you could ever store.

        

        
Source: B. A. Galler and M. J. Fischer, Communications of the ACM 7(5), 1964; R. E. Tarjan, “Efficiency of a good but not linear set union algorithm”, Journal of the ACM 22(2), 1975.
