Graphs & representations

Dots and lines describe maps, friendships and dependencies. How you store them, as a table or as short lists, decides which questions are fast.

  • 16 min
  • Beginner
  • Needs arrays and linked lists

The problem: seven bridges

In the 1730s the people of Königsberg liked to ask a puzzle over Sunday walks: can you cross each of the city’s seven bridges exactly once? Nobody found a route. Nobody could prove there was none, either.

Step through the figure. Watch what happens to the map when only the connections are kept.

Key idea

A graph keeps only what is connected to what. Shapes, sizes and distances are thrown away. That is why one idea covers road maps, friendships, the web and course prerequisites.

Vertices and edges

A graph has two ingredients. The vertices (or nodes) are the things. The edges are the links between pairs of things.

Definition

A graph is G = (V, E): a set V of vertices and a set E of edges, each edge joining two vertices. We write V for the number of vertices and E for the number of edges.

Switch the costume below. The dots and lines stay the same kind of object, but the meaning changes, and so do the details: whether an edge has a direction, and whether it carries a number.

Two properties describe every edge. It is undirected if it works both ways (a friendship) or directed if it has a from and a to (a prerequisite). It is weighted if it carries a number such as a distance or a cost.

Words for graphs

Four words carry most of graph theory. The degree of a vertex is how many edges touch it; its neighbours are the vertices at the other ends. A path is a chain of edges from one vertex to another. A cycle is a path that ends where it started.

Try each one on the graph below. On a directed graph you count arrows in and arrows out separately, and a path may only follow arrows forwards.

The handshake lemma

Add up the degrees of every vertex and you always get 2E: each edge has two ends, and each end adds one to some vertex. So the sum is even, which means a graph never has an odd number of odd-degree vertices. That is exactly the fact Euler used in Königsberg.

A connected component is a piece of the graph in which every vertex can reach every other. The graph above has two. A graph that is one single component is connected.

Storing a graph in memory

A computer cannot look at a drawing. It needs numbers in memory. There are three classic ways, and you can see all of them grow as you draw.

The adjacency matrix is a V × V table: cell (u, v) holds 1 if there is an edge from u to v. The adjacency list keeps, for each vertex, a short list of its neighbours. The edge list is just the pairs.

Draw your own graph: click empty space to add a vertex, drag from one vertex to another to add an edge. Click an edge or a vertex to see where it lives in each structure.

Undirected means stored twice

Select an edge and two matrix cells light up, and the edge appears in two lists. The table mirrors itself across its diagonal. Switch on Directed and each edge fills just one cell, so the mirror disappears.

Notice the counters below the figure. The matrix always needs V² cells, even when you draw nothing. The list needs V heads plus one entry per edge end. Hold that thought: it decides everything that follows.

The lab: matrix versus list

Ask the same question of both structures and count what each one has to read. Pick a graph (the dense one has nearly every edge), choose a question, and press play. The blue ring is the matrix cell being read; the amber chips are list entries.

Try the Dense preset and ask List u’s neighbours, then the Sparse preset. On the sparse graph the matrix reads mostly zeros; on the dense one it wastes almost nothing. Then ask Is u–v an edge? and compare: the matrix always answers in one read.

What it costs

The lab already hints at the trade-off. Memory shows it most clearly. A matrix stores every possible pair: V² cells. A list stores only real edges: about V + E cells.

Drag the sliders. With a few friends per vertex the list stays tiny while the matrix explodes. Only when nearly every pair is linked do the two curves meet.

Why this decides real systems

A network of a billion people with 150 friends each needs about 1.5 × 10¹¹ list entries. As a matrix it would need 10¹⁸ cells. One of those fits on a cluster; the other fits nowhere.

Here deg(u) is the degree of the vertex you ask about, at most V − 1. “Worst case” means the entry is last in its list or absent. In a sparse graph, where E is about V, the list wins on memory and on “visit every edge”. The matrix wins on “is u–v an edge?”.

Why V + E, and not just E?

Every vertex needs a list head, even a vertex with no edges, so that adds V. Every undirected edge appears in two lists, so the entries add up to 2E. In big-O terms both are the same as V + E, but drawing them shows why an undirected graph with 5 edges has 10 entries.

Why is a dense graph no worse in a matrix?

A graph with E close to V² fills most of the table, so the matrix wastes little. The list then holds about V² entries too, plus pointer overhead. With V small you also get the one-read edge check, so the matrix can be the better choice.

Matrix or list? A decision diagram

You now have enough to choose. Answer the questions yourself, or press a scenario and watch the diagram decide.

Most of the time you land on the last box. Adjacency lists are the default for BFS, DFS and shortest paths, which walk a vertex’s neighbours over and over. The next lessons all assume them.

Graphs you never store

Some graphs are too big, or too regular, to write down. A maze on a grid has thousands of cells, yet you never need a list of edges. A cell’s neighbours are the four cells around it, unless there is a wall.

Click a cell to ask for its neighbours, or press Wander and watch a walker move from vertex to vertex. Paint walls and the graph changes, but nothing is stored: one small function is the graph.


      
The same trick describes puzzle games and chess: each position is a vertex, each legal move an edge, and the graph is far too big to build. Algorithms only ever ask “what are the neighbours of this one?”, which is why every graph algorithm is written against that single question.

    


    
    

      
A gallery of special graphs

      
Some shapes turn up so often that they have names. Pick one: the same six vertices glide into a new arrangement, and the chips below count vertices, edges, density and cycles.

      
      
How do you test whether a graph is bipartite? Give every vertex one of two colours so that no edge joins two vertices of the same colour. A queue does it: colour the start, then give each neighbour the opposite colour. If you ever meet an edge whose ends match, there is an odd cycle, and no two-colouring exists.

      
      

        
Odd cycles are the only obstacle

        
A graph is bipartite exactly when it has no cycle of odd length. Even rings alternate colours perfectly; a triangle cannot, because the third vertex is a neighbour of both colours. Trees never have cycles, so every tree is bipartite.

      

    


    
    

      
Variations and patterns

      
The same two structures stretch to cover every kind of edge. Change what a matrix cell holds, and the picture changes with it.

      

        

          
          
          
          
        

        
Store the weight instead of a 1. Pick a marker for “no edge” that no real weight can equal: infinity works for distances, and null works when weights can be zero or negative. In a list each entry becomes a pair (neighbour, weight).

        
A directed edge fills one cell only, so M[u][v] and M[v][u] are independent. Row u lists where u points; column u lists who points at u. A list stores out-neighbours, and finding in-neighbours means scanning everything, or keeping a second list.

        
Königsberg had parallel edges: two bridges joined the same pair of banks. A matrix stores a count; a list repeats the neighbour. A self-loop is an edge from a vertex to itself: cell M[v][v], and it adds 2 to that vertex’s degree.

        
Replace every neighbour list by a hash set. Now “is u–v an edge?” is expected constant time, like the matrix, while memory stays V + E. The price is a little overhead per entry and no guaranteed order. This is the usual choice when both questions matter.

History

      

        
1735presented

        
Leonhard EulerSt Petersburg Academy

        

          
Graph theory began with a walking puzzle. On 26 August 1735 Euler showed the St Petersburg Academy that no stroll through Königsberg crosses each of its seven bridges exactly once. He reduced the city to four land masses and the bridges between them, and noticed that a walk using every bridge once needs zero or two land masses touching an odd number of bridges. In Königsberg all four did.

          
The word “graph” for these diagrams came much later, from J. J. Sylvester in 1878, and the first textbook, by Dénes Kőnig, appeared in 1936.

        

        
Source: L. Euler, “Solutio problematis ad geometriam situs pertinentis”, Commentarii Academiae Scientiarum Petropolitanae 8 (printed 1741).
