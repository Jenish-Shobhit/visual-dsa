BFS & DFS

Two ways to explore a graph. A queue spreads out in waves and finds the route with the fewest edges; a stack dives as deep as it can and exposes the graph’s shape.

  • 20 min
  • Intermediate
  • Needs: graphs, queues, stacks

The problem

Here is a small friendship network. A line means two people have shaken hands. The question sounds easy: how many handshakes separate you from each person? A friend is one handshake away, a friend of a friend two.

The drawing misleads you on purpose: Hal sits right under You, yet he is four handshakes away. Distance in a graph means edges on the shortest route, and nothing about the picture tells you that. You need a search that is systematic.

The same question hides everywhere. A maze asks for the fewest moves to the exit. A web crawler asks which pages are within two clicks. A paint bucket asks which pixels touch the one you clicked.

Build the intuition

Drop a stone in a pond. The ripple reaches everything one step away before anything two steps away. Breadth-first search (BFS) explores a graph like that ripple, ring by ring.

Now explore a cave with a ball of thread. You follow one tunnel as far as it goes, and at a dead end you wind the thread back to the last fork with a tunnel you have not tried. Depth-first search (DFS) explores like that.

Where the analogies break

A graph can loop back on itself, so both searches must remember every vertex they have already seen. Without that memory the ripple would echo forever and the explorer would walk in circles.

Watch both searches start at A on the same graph, at the same speed. Each step, each search visits one more vertex.

One loop, two containers

Both searches keep two things. The frontier holds vertices you have discovered but not finished exploring. The seen set remembers every vertex ever discovered, so nothing is processed twice.

Definitions

Every vertex is in one of three states: unseen (not discovered yet), frontier (discovered, waiting to be explored) or visited (explored). Some books call them white, grey and black.

Both searches repeat one move: pick a vertex from the frontier and look at its neighbours. The only difference is which vertex they pick. BFS picks the oldest discovery, so its frontier is a queue. DFS picks the newest, so its frontier is a stack.

Switch the container below and watch how the same graph is explored in a different order.

Key idea

The container decides the order, and the order decides what you learn. First in, first out gives distances. Last in, first out gives depth, and with it the graph’s structure.

BFS: waves and distances

The queue hands vertices out in the order they were found. So BFS finishes every vertex at distance 1 before it touches distance 2, and all of distance 2 before distance 3. The distance of a vertex is the number of edges on the shortest route from the start.

When BFS discovers a vertex it writes down two things: its distance (one more than the vertex it came from) and its parent, the vertex it came from. Step through, then watch the graph rearrange itself by distance.

Why the first route found is a shortest one

The queue only ever holds two neighbouring distances, d and d + 1, in that order. Suppose a vertex v had a shorter route than the one BFS found. The vertex just before v on that route is closer to the start, so it left the queue earlier and would have discovered v first. Contradiction.

Following parent pointers from any vertex back to the start spells out a shortest route in reverse. Together the parent edges form the BFS tree: one shortest route from the start to every reachable vertex.

One line decides whether BFS is fast: you must mark a vertex as seen when you add it to the queue, not when you take it out. Try both on a graph where everyone knows everyone.

DFS: dive and back up

DFS takes the first unseen neighbour and keeps going. When a vertex has no unseen neighbours left, DFS backs up to the most recent vertex that still has one. Written with recursion, the call stack does the backing up for you: each running call is one vertex on the current route.

Definition

A clock ticks once each time DFS enters or leaves a vertex. The discovery time d is the tick when the call starts; the finish time f is the tick when it returns. Below each vertex you see them as d/f.

The clock records the tree

Look at the bars: two of them never partly overlap. Either one sits completely inside the other, or they do not touch. A bar inside another means a descendant: DFS entered and left it while the outer call was still running.

Deep graphs break recursion

A graph shaped like a long line makes recursive DFS nest one call per vertex. Around ten thousand calls deep, many languages run out of stack. For big inputs, keep your own stack in a loop (see the variations below).

Grids are graphs in disguise

A grid is a graph you never have to draw. Every open cell is a vertex, and it has an edge to each open cell directly above, below, left or right of it. Walls are simply missing vertices.

So BFS on a grid is a flood: from the start it fills cells in diamond-shaped rings, and the first time it reaches the goal it has found a shortest route. Drag on the grid to draw or erase walls, drag S and T to move them, then compare BFS with DFS.

Key idea

Flood fill, “number of islands”, the paint bucket in an image editor and the fewest moves in a maze are all one BFS (or DFS) on a grid. Only the question you ask at the end changes.

What the walks reveal

One search only reaches the start’s own piece of the graph. Restart it from every vertex that is still unseen and you find the connected components: the separate islands of the graph.

BFS can also two-colour a graph: the start is side A, its neighbours side B, theirs side A again. If an edge ever joins two vertices on the same side, the graph has an odd cycle and is not bipartite.

DFS has a sharper tool. In a directed graph, an edge that points to a vertex still on the stack points back to an ancestor: a back edge. A back edge exists exactly when the graph has a cycle.

What it costs

      
Count two operations: taking a vertex out of the frontier, and checking one entry of an adjacency list. Each reachable vertex leaves the frontier once. Each adjacency list is scanned once, and together the lists hold every edge twice (once from each end) in an undirected graph.

Space is where the two searches differ. The frontier of BFS holds a whole layer; the stack of DFS holds a whole route. Which one is bigger depends on the graph’s shape.

      
      

        Why is the matrix version quadratic even on a sparse graph?
        
To find the neighbours of one vertex in a matrix you must read its whole row, V cells, most of them zero. Doing that for each of V vertices costs V² reads, whether the graph has 10 edges or 10,000.

      

      

        Why O(V + E) and not O(E)?
        
A graph can have far fewer edges than vertices; with no edges at all, a full traversal that restarts at every vertex still does V units of work. So both terms stay.

History

      

        
1882DFS in print

        
Charles Trémaux, Konrad Zuse, Edward F. Mooremaze rule, 1882 · BFS, 1945 · BFS rediscovered, 1957

        

          
Depth-first search began as a 19th-century maze trick. Charles Trémaux marked each passage as he walked it and retreated along his own marks when he got stuck; Édouard Lucas published the rule in 1882.

          
Breadth-first search was written down by Konrad Zuse in 1945, in a thesis on his Plankalkül language that stayed unpublished until 1972. So Edward Moore of Bell Labs reinvented it in 1957 to find the shortest path through a maze, and C. Y. Lee used it in 1961 to route wires on circuit boards. In the early 1970s Robert Tarjan showed how much structure one linear-time DFS reveals.

        

        
Sources: É. Lucas, Récréations mathématiques (1882); E. F. Moore, “The Shortest Path Through a Maze” (1959); C. Y. Lee (1961); R. Tarjan, “Depth-first search and linear graph algorithms” (1972). See docs/research/HISTORY.md.
