Dijkstra & A*

Find the cheapest route when roads have different lengths. Always settle the closest unsettled place, keep the candidates in a priority queue, and, with A*, let a good guess pull the search toward the goal.

  • Intermediate
  • Needs: BFS, heaps

The problem

Your phone finds the fastest way home. The map is a graph, and every road carries a number: the minutes it takes to drive. BFS from the last lesson counts roads, not minutes. Watch it choose.

Definitions

A weighted graph gives every edge a number, its weight (a cost, length or time). The weight of a path is the sum of its edge weights, and the shortest path is the path with the smallest total weight, however many edges it uses.

The same question shows up everywhere: the fastest drive, the packet route with the lowest delay, the cheapest way through a game map.

Build the intuition

Picture every road as a fuse. Light a fire at Start and let it burn along each fuse at one unit of weight per minute. A town catches fire the moment the first flame reaches it. So a town’s ignition time is exactly its shortest distance from Start.

Where the analogy breaks

Fire needs no plan: it burns every fuse at once. A program has no clock and looks at one edge at a time. It must work out which town would ignite next without waiting, and that is the job of Dijkstra’s algorithm and its priority queue.

Relaxation: the one move

Every vertex keeps a number, dist: the shortest route found so far. It starts at ∞ for everyone except the start. It is always an upper bound: the true distance is never more than dist, and the number only ever goes down.

Relaxing an edge u → v of weight w asks one question: is going through u better than what v has? Try the edge with your own numbers.

Key idea

Every shortest-path algorithm is just relaxation in a chosen order. Dijkstra’s trick is the order: relax the edges out of the closest unsettled vertex first.

Dijkstra: settle the closest

Dijkstra splits the vertices in two. The settled ones have a distance that is final. The rest are waiting, and some have a tentative dist. Each round it picks the waiting vertex with the smallest dist, settles it, and relaxes its outgoing edges.

Why is that pick safe? Look at the cut between settled and waiting vertices.

The proof in one sentence

Any other route to the closest waiting vertex must first leave the settled set through some other waiting vertex, which is already at least as far, and the rest of the route adds a weight of 0 or more. So nothing can beat the closest one. This is exactly where “no negative weights” is needed.

Now run it by hand on a small map. Beside the graph sits the priority queue: entries (distance, vertex) sorted smallest first. Each vertex carries its distance as a badge, and the little via label names the neighbour it got that distance from.

Each vertex remembers its parent, the neighbour that gave it its best distance. The parent edges form a tree, the shortest-path tree, and following parents back from any vertex spells out its shortest route in reverse.

One detail keeps the queue simple. A binary heap cannot cheaply lower an entry that is already inside it. So when a shorter route is found, Dijkstra pushes a new entry and leaves the old one behind. The leftover is stale: when it finally reaches the front, its distance is larger than the vertex’s current one, and it is thrown away. This is called lazy deletion.

When a weight is negative

Dijkstra’s proof needs every weight to be 0 or more. Give it one negative edge and the greedy choice fails. Here B → A has weight −3. Watch Dijkstra settle A at 1, and then meet an edge that would have made it −1.

The fix is not to patch Dijkstra. With negative edges use Bellman-Ford, which relaxes every edge V − 1 times and needs no greedy choice. That is the next lesson.

A*: aim at the goal

Dijkstra spreads in every direction, like a ripple, even away from the goal. On a map you know roughly where the goal is. A* uses that. For each waiting cell it adds a guess h of the cost still to go, and expands the cell with the smallest f = g + h, where g is the cost so far.

The guess used on grids is the Manhattan distance: how many steps you would need with no walls. Here is a search paused with its frontier labelled g + h.

Three searches use the same loop and differ only in the priority. Dijkstra orders by g, A* by g + h, and greedy best-first by h alone: fast, but it ignores the cost already paid.

Paint walls and mud (a cell that costs 5 instead of 1), move S and T by dragging them, and compare. The table under the grid reruns all three searches on your map.

Admissible and consistent

A guess h is admissible if it never overestimates the true remaining cost. It is consistent if it never drops by more than the cost of a step: h(u) ≤ w(u, v) + h(v). With a consistent guess, A* expands every cell at most once and its first answer is optimal.

The guess is a dial. h = 0 gives Dijkstra. Manhattan distance is admissible on a grid where a step costs at least 1, so A* stays optimal and expands far fewer cells. A guess that is too big pulls the search toward the goal so hard that it may accept a dearer route. Watch the three race on the same map.

Turn the dial yourself. The weight scales h in f = g + weight × h. At 0 you get Dijkstra, at 1 A*, and far beyond that greedy best-first.

Which shortest-path algorithm?

Dijkstra is one tool in a family. Answer the questions in order to see which one fits your graph.

What it costs

With a binary heap, each edge relaxation may push one entry, so at most E entries enter the queue. Each push and each pop costs O(log V), and every vertex is expanded once.

A* is about vertices expanded, not big-O. On a grid with a start and a goal, count how many cells each search expands as the grid grows.

Why do stale entries not ruin the bound?

Each successful relaxation pushes exactly one entry, and there are at most E of those (one per edge), plus the first entry for the start. So the queue never sees more than E + 1 entries in total, and each costs O(log(E + 1)) = O(log V) to push and to pop, because E ≤ V².

Why does A* ever expand more than Dijkstra?

With a consistent, admissible h it never expands a cell that Dijkstra would skip, so it does at most as much work (ties aside). A poor guess, such as h = 0, saves nothing. An inconsistent guess can force A* to reopen cells and spend extra time.

Variations and patterns

The same relax-and-settle loop answers more than one question.

When you need one destination, stop as soon as it leaves the queue. Its distance is final at that moment, so everything after is wasted work. This is the found step in the lab.


        
Run one search from the start and one backwards from the goal, and stop when they meet. Each search only has to reach about half the distance, and the area it covers grows with the square of the radius, so the work drops sharply.

        
When every weight is 0 or 1, a double-ended queue replaces the heap. Push a weight-0 neighbour on the front and a weight-1 neighbour on the back: the deque stays sorted by distance and the whole search runs in O(V + E).

        
Start with several vertices in the queue, all at distance 0. One run gives every vertex its distance to the nearest source: the closest fire station, the closest charging point.

History

      

        
1956a café in Amsterdam

        
Edsger Dijkstra; Peter Hart, Nils Nilsson, Bertram RaphaelDijkstra, 1956 · A*, 1968

        

          
Edsger Dijkstra designed his algorithm in about twenty minutes in 1956, sitting on a café terrace in Amsterdam with his fiancée, without pencil or paper. He wanted a demonstration for the new ARMAC computer that anyone could follow: the shortest route between Dutch cities. He published it in 1959, together with a minimum-spanning-tree method, in a three-page note.

          
A* came in 1968, from Hart, Nilsson and Raphael at SRI, to plan paths for Shakey, a robot that decided its own moves. In 2025 a team showed that Dijkstra’s sorting barrier for directed graphs can be broken, running in time O(m log2/3 n).

        

        
Sources: E. W. Dijkstra, “A Note on Two Problems in Connexion with Graphs”, Numerische Mathematik 1 (1959); P. Hart, N. Nilsson, B. Raphael, IEEE Trans. SSC 4(2) (1968); R. Duan et al., STOC 2025. See docs/research/HISTORY.md.
