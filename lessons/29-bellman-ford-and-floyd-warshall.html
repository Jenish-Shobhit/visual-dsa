Bellman-Ford & Floyd-Warshall

Relax every edge again and again and negative weights stop being a problem. Loop over stopovers, and you get the distance between every pair of vertices at once.

  • 20 min
  • Intermediate

The problem: a discount greedy cannot see

Most route finding pretends every road costs something. Real networks are messier. A toll refund, a trade that pays you, a flight that is cheap only because of a costly connection: an edge can have a negative weight.

That breaks the greedy habit behind Dijkstra’s algorithm: finish the closest place, and never touch its number again. Step through what goes wrong.

This lesson has two answers. Bellman-Ford never finishes a vertex early: it keeps lowering distances until nothing can be lowered, and it notices when that will never happen. Floyd-Warshall answers a bigger question, the cheapest route between every pair, with three nested loops.

One move: relax an edge

An edge u → v with weight w is an offer: if you can reach u for d[u], you can reach v for d[u] + w. Compare the offer with the number you already hold for v, and keep the smaller one.

Definition

To relax the edge u → v is to set d[v] = min(d[v], d[u] + w). The edge is relaxable when d[u] + w < d[v].

Drag the sliders. The last one goes all the way up to ∞, a vertex nobody has reached yet.

Every number in this lesson is an upper bound: the cost of a route you have really found. Relaxing can only lower it, and it can never drop below the truth. Bellman-Ford is nothing more than “relax everything, until nothing changes”.

Rounds: paths with at most k edges

Do it in rounds. One round relaxes every edge once. After round 1 the numbers are the cheapest routes that use at most one edge. After round 2, at most two. After round k, at most k.

Analogy: news travelling town to town

Each round, every town tells its neighbours the cheapest price it has heard about. After k rounds, news has travelled k roads. The analogy breaks in one place: news never gets cheaper by going round a loop. A negative loop does exactly that.

Why V − 1 rounds are enough

A cheapest path never visits a vertex twice: a repeat means a loop, and a loop that does not lower the cost can simply be cut out. A path through V different vertices has at most V − 1 edges, and round k settles every path of k edges. So after V − 1 rounds every cheapest path is found.

Look at the last two columns. Round 4 still changed Z, and here V − 1 is 4. Round 5 would change nothing. Keep that in mind: it is how Bellman-Ford knows the answer is final.

Two exits, two meanings. “Nothing relaxed” inside the loop is early exit: the answer is final and you saved rounds. “Something still relaxes in round V” is a negative cycle: there is no answer to return.

When there is no answer: negative cycles

Definition

A negative cycle is a loop of edges whose weights add up to less than zero. Walk around it once and you are cheaper than when you started, so walk around again, and again. The cheapest route to anything the cycle can reach does not exist.

Change the total weight of the loop A → B → C → A and watch two distances round after round. With a negative loop they never settle. With a loop that costs 0 or more they stop after a few rounds.

Two ways to be surprised

An undirected edge with a negative weight is a negative cycle all by itself: walk u → v → u and you gain twice. And Bellman-Ford only reports cycles the source can reach. A negative loop in a far corner is invisible to it (the lab has a “cycle out of reach” preset). To search the whole graph, start every distance at 0, as if a hidden vertex pointed at everything.

Negative cycles are money: currency arbitrage

Exchange rates multiply along a chain of trades: 100 USD becomes 100 × 0.92 EUR, then × 0.86 GBP, then × 1.28 USD. If the product of the rates around a loop exceeds 1, you end with more than you began: arbitrage.

Bellman-Ford adds weights; it does not multiply. The bridge is the logarithm. Give each trade the weight −ln(rate). Then a product above 1 becomes a sum below 0, so profit is exactly a negative cycle. Move the sliders until the sum flips sign.

The three fixed “return” quotes include a 1 % spread, like a real exchange, so a fair market shows no profit. Real arbitrage windows last milliseconds, but the detection method is exactly this.

All pairs at once: Floyd-Warshall

Bellman-Ford answers “from S to everywhere”. Running it from every vertex answers “from everywhere to everywhere”, at V times the cost. Floyd-Warshall takes a different road: keep a matrix, d[i][j] = cheapest known cost from i to j, and improve the whole matrix in V passes.

The idea: allow one more stopover at a time

Number the vertices. A stopover is a vertex a route passes through between its start and its end. Let the entry d[i][j] after pass k be the cheapest route from i to j whose stopovers all come from the first k vertices. Before pass 1 no stopover is allowed: only direct edges. After pass V, everything is allowed, and that is the true answer.

Pick a pair and step through the passes. Watch the cost drop only when the newest stopover helps.

The update rule

Going from pass k − 1 to pass k asks one question about each pair: is it cheaper to go through k? A route through k splits into a piece from i to k and a piece from k to j, and both pieces only need the earlier stopovers. So d[i][j] = min(d[i][j], d[i][k] + d[k][j]), and only row k and column k are ever read.

That is the whole algorithm: for each k, for each i, for each j, apply the rule. Alongside the distances it keeps a next[i][j] matrix, the first step of the best route, updated whenever a distance is. Here is everything at once. Getting the route back: next hops

The matrix tells you how much a trip costs, not which way to go. That is what next[i][j] is for: the first step of a cheapest route from i to j. When d[i][j] improves by going through k, the route starts the way the route to k starts, so next[i][j] = next[i][k]. To read a route, hop: go to next[i][j], then look up next from there, until you arrive.

The negative diagonal

Every d[i][i] starts at 0: the empty route from a vertex to itself. If it ever turns negative, there is a route from i back to i that costs less than nothing: a negative cycle. Floyd-Warshall needs no extra check, just a look at the diagonal. When one is negative, the other entries are not real distances and next hops can loop, so do not trust them. Which is cheapest depends on the graph. Sparse graphs (E about 3V) favour many Dijkstras or Johnson. Dense graphs (E about V²/2) favour Floyd-Warshall. Bellman-Ford from every vertex loses either way. Drag the slider and flip between sparse and dense.

Why V³ and never less?

Three nested loops over V values each. The loops do not look at the data: even a graph with no edges runs all V³ checks. Floyd-Warshall has no best case, but it also has no bad case, and its inner loop is one addition and one comparison.

Why can Floyd-Warshall update the matrix in place?

During pass k, only row k and column k are read. Without a negative cycle they do not change in that pass, because d[k][j] would have to beat d[k][k] + d[k][j] with d[k][k] = 0. So one V × V matrix is enough: no need for a fresh copy per pass. It is also why the space is O(V²), not O(V³).

Bellman-Ford: are V − 1 rounds ever really needed?

Yes, when the edges happen to be listed against the direction of the path. A chain of V vertices with its edges listed backwards needs a full round for every edge. That is what the lab’s “unlucky order” preset shows, and the same chain listed forwards finishes in two rounds.

Variations and patterns

Three ideas that grow out of the same two algorithms.

On a sparse graph V Dijkstras beat V³, but Dijkstra hates negative edges. Johnson’s algorithm removes them first: one Bellman-Ford gives every vertex a potential h(v), and each edge is reweighted to w + h(u) − h(v), which is never negative. Every route between the same two vertices shifts by the same amount, so cheapest routes stay cheapest.

Change the question from “how far?” to “can I get there at all?”. Warshall’s transitive closure uses the same three loops with “or” and “and” in place of “min” and “+”: reach[i][j] ||= reach[i][k] && reach[k][j]. Still O(V³), and the result answers every “is there a route?” question in O(1).

Knowing a negative cycle exists is one thing; showing it is better. When round V relaxes an edge into v, step back through parent V times. That is guaranteed to land on the cycle, because a walk of V steps has to repeat a vertex. Then follow parents until you come back to where you were.

History

1958published

Shimbel, Ford, Bellman, Moore · Floyd, Warshallrounds of relaxation, 1954–1959 · the matrix passes, 1962

Bellman-Ford was found at least four times in five years, by Alfonso Shimbel, Lester Ford, Richard Bellman and Edward Moore. Bellman’s 1958 paper “On a Routing Problem” gave it the name most people use. A distributed version later routed packets across the ARPANET, and its descendants still run inside network routers.

Floyd-Warshall has several parents too. Bernard Roy described it in 1959. In January 1962 Stephen Warshall published the boolean version, transitive closure, and in June Robert Floyd published the shortest-path version as “Algorithm 97”. Warshall reportedly bet a colleague a bottle of rum on who could first settle whether his method always works. He proved it overnight and shared the rum with the loser. In 2022 Bernstein, Nanongkai and Wulff-Nilsen showed that negative-weight shortest paths can be found in near-linear time.

Sources: R. Bellman, “On a Routing Problem”, Quarterly of Applied Mathematics 16 (1958); L. R. Ford, “Network Flow Theory”, RAND P-923 (1956); R. W. Floyd, “Algorithm 97: Shortest Path”, CACM 5(6) (1962); S. Warshall, “A Theorem on Boolean Matrices”, JACM 9(1) (1962); Bernstein, Nanongkai, Wulff-Nilsen, FOCS 2022. See docs/research/HISTORY.md.

Practice

  1. Transitive closure of a relation
    Easy

    Given “a is a prerequisite of b” pairs for n courses, build the matrix that says whether course a is needed, directly or indirectly, for course b. What is the running time, and how would a course that is its own prerequisite show up?

  2. Cheapest flights within K stops
    Medium

    Flights have prices. Find the cheapest trip from a source to a destination using at most K stops. Explain why exactly K + 1 rounds of Bellman-Ford answer this, and why each round must read the previous round’s distances rather than update in place.

  3. City with the fewest neighbours within a threshold
    Medium

    Given n cities, weighted roads and a distance threshold, return the city that can reach the fewest other cities within the threshold (the largest number if tied). Compute all distances with Floyd-Warshall and count.

  4. Print the negative cycle
    Medium

    Return the vertices of some negative cycle in a directed graph, or report that there is none, even for cycles no single source can reach. Start every distance at 0 and use the parent pointers.

  5. Detect currency arbitrage
    Hard

    Given a table of exchange rates for n currencies, decide whether some sequence of trades ends with more than it started with, and print the sequence. Convert rates to weights, and say what precision problem you must guard against when a cycle is only barely profitable.
