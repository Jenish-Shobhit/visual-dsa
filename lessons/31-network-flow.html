Max flow & min cut

How much can you push through a network of pipes? Find a route with room, fill it, repeat. When no route is left, a bottleneck you can point at proves you are done.

  • 22 min
  • Advanced
  • Needs: graphs, BFS

The problem

A water company has a tap s, a drain t and a web of pipes between them. Each pipe is as thick as the water it can carry per second, its capacity. The question sounds simple: how much water can you get from s to t at once?

Open the tap slowly. The water spreads over several routes, pipes fill up, and at some point the network refuses to take more. The same question hides in road traffic, internet links, supply chains and job assignments.

Two numbers here are worth remembering. The tap could deliver 14 and the drain could take 15, yet only 12 arrive. Something in the middle limits everything, and finding it is the second half of this lesson.

The two rules

A flow network is a directed graph with a source s, a sink t and a capacity on every edge. A flow assigns each edge an amount that obeys two rules.

Definitions

Capacity rule: an edge carries between 0 and its capacity. Conservation rule: at every vertex except s and t, what flows in equals what flows out. The value of a flow is the amount leaving s, which by conservation equals the amount arriving at t.

Build the intuition

Pick any route from s to t whose pipes all have room. Push water along it. Because water cannot pile up at a vertex, the same amount must pass through every pipe of the route, so the route carries only as much as its narrowest pipe, the bottleneck.

So one algorithm suggests itself: find a route with room, push its bottleneck, repeat. Each round is called an augmentation and the route is an augmenting path. But is it enough?

Why greedy gets stuck

Not quite. Watch the smallest network where the naive method fails. It picks a route that looks wonderful, fills it, and then finds that no route remains, although the pipes could still carry more.

The trick

The reverse arc did not move new water. It rerouted water already in transit: 2 units that were going a→b now leave a through a→t instead. An algorithm made of purely local pushes reaches a global optimum because it can always take an earlier choice back.

The residual graph

The residual graph collects everything you can still do. A pipe with capacity c and flow f gives two arcs: a forward arc with room c − f (the unused room), and a reverse arc with room f (the flow you could take back). An augmenting path is any route s to t in this graph.

Min cut: the proof you are done

When the search fails, the vertices it did reach, call them S, are cut off from t. Every edge leaving S must be full, or the search would have crossed it, and every edge coming back into S must be empty. The flow across the split equals the capacity of the edges that cross it. Watch the last search.

Max-flow min-cut theorem

The largest flow from s to t equals the capacity of the smallest cut. Any flow is at most any cut, since everything must cross it. The algorithm's last search finds a flow and a cut with the same number, so neither can be improved.

Now try cuts yourself. Click a vertex to move it between the source side and the sink side. Every cut you make will be at least 12; can you find one that is exactly 12?

Application: workers and jobs

Max flow solves problems that have no water in them. Say each worker can do only some jobs and every job needs one worker. Build a network: a tap feeds each worker one unit, each worker is joined to their jobs, each job drains one unit into t. All capacities are 1, so a flow of value k is a matching of k pairs.

Augmenting paths now re-route people: to seat a new worker, the algorithm may take a job from someone who has another option. That is the reverse arc again, and it is why greedy assignment fails where flow succeeds.

What it costs

Each augmentation costs one BFS, O(E). What matters is how many augmentations are needed, and that depends on which paths you pick.

Ford-Fulkerson, any pathup to the flow value |f|O(E·|f|)
Edmonds-Karp, shortest path (BFS)at most V·E/2, whatever the capacitiesO(V·E²)

Two searches race on the trap network below. Both find the same maximum flow; one needs two rounds and the other one per unit.

Raise the capacity and the gap explodes: plain Ford-Fulkerson with unlucky choices needs 2M rounds, Edmonds-Karp still needs 2. Its count never depends on the capacities.

The bound V·E/2 is a guarantee, not a forecast. On random networks the real count is far below it.

The frontier is much better than these textbook bounds: in 2022 Chen, Kyng, Liu, Peng, Probst Gutenberg and Sachdeva found a max-flow algorithm with running time m1+o(1), almost linear in the number of edges.

Variations and patterns

Many problems that look nothing like plumbing are flows or cuts in disguise. The trick is always in how you build the network.

Bipartite matching. Source to workers, workers to jobs, jobs to sink, all capacities 1. The value is the largest matching, and the min cut is a proof that no bigger one exists (König and Hall). Airline crew scheduling and baseball elimination use the same idea.

Limits on vertices. When a vertex can pass only 3 units, split it into an entry and an exit joined by an edge of capacity 3. Now the limit is an ordinary edge capacity. The same split gives vertex-disjoint paths.

Several sources or sinks. Add a super source with infinite-capacity edges to every source, and a super sink the same way. Then it is one ordinary max-flow problem.

Cutting an image. Pixels are vertices, neighbours are joined by edges whose capacity says how alike they are, and s and t are “foreground” and “background”. The min cut is the cheapest way to separate them.

History

1955the rail report

T. E. Harris, F. S. Ross, L. R. Ford, D. R. FulkersonRAND, 1955 · Ford-Fulkerson, 1956 · Edmonds-Karp, 1972

Max flow began as a Cold War question. In 1955 Ted Harris and General Frank Ross at RAND modelled the railways from the Soviet Union into Eastern Europe as a network of 44 nodes, found a flow of 163,000 tons, and marked the matching minimum cut as “the bottleneck”: the links an air force would target. The report was secret until 1999.

Lester Ford and Ray Fulkerson, also at RAND, proved the general rule and gave the augmenting-path method in 1956. In 1972 Jack Edmonds and Richard Karp showed that taking shortest paths bounds the running time whatever the capacities are.

Sources: Harris and Ross, “Fundamentals of a Method for Evaluating Rail Net Capacities” (RAND, 1955); A. Schrijver, “On the History of the Transportation and Maximum Flow Problems” (2002); Edmonds and Karp, JACM 19(2), 1972. See docs/research/HISTORY.md.

Practice

  1. Run it by hand
    Easy

    On the network s→a 3, s→b 2, a→b 5, a→t 2, b→t 3, run Edmonds-Karp by hand. List each augmenting path, its bottleneck and the flow after it, then name the minimum cut.

  2. Maximum invitations
    Medium

    Boys and girls, and a grid saying who may invite whom, with each person invited at most once. Model it as a flow and return the largest number of accepted invitations.

  3. Edge-disjoint paths
    Medium

    Find the largest number of paths from s to t that share no edge. Which capacities do you give the edges, and what does the min cut tell you?

  4. Vertex-disjoint escape routes
    Hard

    On a grid with blocked cells, find the most people who can walk from the start cells to the exits without any two sharing a cell. Use vertex splitting.

  5. Why does the reverse arc matter?
    Hard

    Build a network where the algorithm that ignores reverse arcs returns half of the maximum flow. Then explain, in two sentences, why the version with reverse arcs cannot get stuck.
