Give the table a second index. Every cell compares a prefix of one thing with a prefix of another, and a glowing path of arrows leads back from the corner to the answer.

The problem

A robot stands in the top-left corner of a grid and wants to reach the bottom-right. It moves only right or down. How many different routes are there?

Click any cell to drop an obstacle, or pick a layout. Step through, and watch each cell collect the routes that arrive from above and from the left.

Every route into a cell ends with a step from above or a step from the left, so paths(r, c) = paths(r − 1, c) + paths(r, c − 1). That is lesson 34's idea with one new twist: the state has two numbers, a row and a column, so the table is a grid.

Build the intuition

Strings give you the same shape without a robot. Take two of them and ask: what is the longest thing they share, in order? Step through a few guesses.

Definition

A subsequence keeps some letters of a string, in their original order, and drops the rest. The longest common subsequence (LCS) is the longest one that both strings contain. Diff tools, version control and DNA comparison all rest on it.

Trying every subsequence of a 6-letter string means testing 64 of them, and each extra letter doubles that. Instead, shrink the problem. Compare only the first i letters of A with the first j letters of B, for every i and j. Those small answers are the cells of a table.

Analogy: two bookmarks

Read two books side by side with a bookmark in each. A cell records "how much do the pages up to my two bookmarks share?". Push either bookmark forward by one letter and the answer can only change a little, so it is worth remembering the answer for every pair of bookmark positions.

Click any cell to see which two prefixes it stands for, and a longest subsequence they share.

Where the analogy breaks

The table only works because a cell depends on smaller prefix pairs and nothing else. If the answer for two prefixes also depended on what comes later in the strings, no table of prefixes could hold it.

Two rules fill the table

Look at one cell and its three neighbours: up-left, up and left. The last letters of the two prefixes decide which neighbour matters.

Key idea

If the last letters match, they can both end the subsequence, so extend the diagonal cell by 1. If not, at most one of them can be used, so keep the better of "drop the last letter of A" (up) and "drop the last letter of B" (left). Row 0 and column 0 are the base cases.

Why the match rule is safe

If A and B both end in T, some longest common subsequence ends with that T. Any answer that ignores one of the two T's can be improved by matching them, so the shorter prefixes' best plus one is always achievable and never beaten.

The LCS lab

Type two strings, or pick a preset, and step. Each cell lights its neighbours, draws an arrow, and weighs the options underneath. When the table is full, the traceback follows the arrows from the corner, and the letters where it goes diagonally spell the answer.

Two things to try: enter ABC, CBA and see why the answer is only 1, then enter two identical strings and watch the arrows run down the diagonal.

Any order that finishes the inputs first

Every arrow points up, left or up-left, so a cell can be filled once its three neighbours are done. Row by row is the usual choice. Try the others, and try the broken one.

The diagonal order is a hint about speed: all the cells on one anti-diagonal are independent, so a computer with many cores can fill them at once. That is how bioinformatics tools align long DNA strands quickly.

Edit distance

Spell checkers ask a different question: how few edits turn word A into word B? An edit is one of three moves. Each move is one arrow in the table.

Definition

The edit distance (Levenshtein distance) between two words is the fewest single-letter insertions, deletions and replacements that turn one into the other. Same table as LCS: dp[i][j] is the distance between the first i letters of A and the first j of B.

The base cases are forced: turning i letters into nothing takes i deletions, and building j letters from nothing takes j insertions. Then each cell takes the cheapest of three arrows. When the table is full, the traceback path is the edit script, and the lab replays it on the word.

The knapsack

You have a bag with room for 5 units and three items. Each item has a weight and a value, and you may take it or leave it, never half of it. Which items maximise the value?

The tempting rule, "take the best value per weight first", can fail. Compare the two bags.

The table fixes it by trying both choices for every item and every capacity. Rows are items (one more row per item available), columns are capacities. Cell dp[i][c] is the best value using the first i items in a bag of size c: skip item i and keep the row above, or take it and add its value to the best with the room that is left.

Before the next section, try the same kind of question yourself. Click the item that the best bag leaves out.

One row is enough, if you go backwards

Row i of the knapsack table reads only row i − 1. So you could keep one row and update it in place. But the direction of the loop now matters: the figure runs both directions on the same item (weight 2, value 3, only one of it) and shows one of them breaking.

Key idea

Looping capacity downwards keeps every read pointing at the "before this item" row, which is exactly the 0/1 rule. Looping upwards lets a cell read a value that already contains the item, which allows unlimited copies: the unbounded knapsack that you met as coin change.

What it costs

      
A table costs (number of cells) × (work per cell). LCS and edit distance fill (m + 1)(n + 1) cells with constant work each. Knapsack fills (n + 1)(W + 1) cells. Brute force, by contrast, tries every subset or every subsequence: 2n of them.

For strings of length 5,000 the LCS table holds 25 million cells: seconds of work. In 2015, Backurs and Indyk showed that a genuinely faster edit distance algorithm would break a widely believed conjecture about satisfiability, so the quadratic table is likely the best you can do in general.

      

        Why is O(n·W) called “pseudo-polynomial”?
        
The time grows with the value of W, not with the number of digits needed to write it. A capacity of a billion needs a table with a billion columns, though “1000000000” is ten digits. Knapsack is NP-hard in the length of its input, yet perfectly practical whenever W is modest.

Variations and patterns

      
The same tables, with one rule changed, solve a family of problems.

      

        

          
          
          
          
        

        

          

          

            
A substring must be contiguous. One rule changes: a mismatch resets the cell to 0. Because a run can end anywhere, the answer is the largest cell, not the corner.

            

          

        

        

          

          

            
Some problems ask about a range of one sequence, such as the cheapest way to multiply a chain of matrices. The cell (i, j) means "the range i..j", and it combines two shorter ranges. The order is by length: short ranges before long ones.

            
Interval DP is the one place where the fill order is not row by row. It costs O(n³) when every split point is tried.

          

        

        

          

          

            
Replace the values by yes and no and knapsack becomes subset sum: can some of the numbers add up exactly to the target? Splitting an array into two equal halves is subset sum with half the total as the target.

            

          

        

        

          

          

            
LCS and edit distance also read only the previous row, so two rows are enough when you need the number. To rebuild the string as well, you need the full table, or Hirschberg's divide-and-conquer trick that regains the string in linear space.

History

      

        
1965–74found again and again

        
Levenshtein, Vintsyuk, Needleman & Wunsch, Wagner & FischerJACM 21(1), 1974

        

          
The edit-distance table was discovered again and again: by speech researchers in 1968, by biologists aligning proteins in 1970, and by computer scientists Robert Wagner and Michael Fischer in 1974. Vladimir Levenshtein had defined the distance in 1965 while studying codes that repair dropped and inserted bits.

          
In 2015 Arturs Backurs and Piotr Indyk explained why nobody has beaten the quadratic table: doing so would mean a breakthrough on satisfiability.

        

        
Sources: R. Wagner and M. Fischer, “The String-to-String Correction Problem”, JACM 21(1), 1974; A. Backurs and P. Indyk, “Edit Distance Cannot Be Computed in Strongly Subquadratic Time (unless SETH is false)”, STOC 2015.
