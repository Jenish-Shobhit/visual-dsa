P, NP & hard problems

Some answers are easy to check and, as far as anyone knows, brutally hard to find. Learn to spot those problems, prove they are hard, and cope with them anyway.

Easy to check, hard to find

Hand someone a blank Sudoku and they may struggle for an hour. Hand them a filled grid and ask whether it is correct, and they can tell you in a minute: every row, column and box must hold 1 to 9 once each.

That gap between checking an answer and finding one is the subject of this lesson. Race the two below. Both run on the same operation clock, so the difference is not a matter of hardware.

A fair warning about Sudoku

A fixed 9 × 9 grid is a finite puzzle, so on paper even brute force is “constant time”. The claim about hardness is for the generalised puzzle on n² × n² grids, which is NP-complete (Yato and Seta, 2003). The 9 × 9 race is a model of that behaviour, not the proof.

Two kinds of effort

Theory works with decision problems: questions with a yes or no answer. “What is the shortest tour?” becomes “Is there a tour of length at most k?” Nothing is lost, because asking for every k (with a binary search) recovers the optimum.

P

P is the set of decision problems that an algorithm can solve in polynomial time, meaning O(nk) for some fixed k. Sorting, shortest paths, matching and primality testing all live here.

NP

NP is the set of decision problems where every “yes” instance has a short certificate that a polynomial-time verifier can check. It does not stand for “not polynomial”. It stands for nondeterministic polynomial time.

Each certificate below is easy to check. Whether it is easy to find is the open part.

Every problem in P is also in NP: to verify, just solve the problem yourself and ignore the certificate. So P ⊆ NP, and that part is proven. Whether the two sets are equal is the P vs NP question, which nobody has answered.

Verifying is a skill. Try it: a friend claims five vertices form a clique, meaning every pair is joined by an edge. The claim is the certificate.

You needed at most 10 pair checks for 5 vertices, k(k−1)/2 in general. Checking is fast, but you found the flaw only because someone gave you the candidate. Searching for a clique of size 5 among 200 vertices means facing about 2.5 billion candidate sets.

Why brute force stops working

The obvious way to solve most NP problems is to try every candidate. The number of candidates grows like 2n (each item in or out) or n! (every ordering). Compare those with the polynomial n³ on a computer doing a billion steps per second.

Move the slider. At n = 20, n³ is 8,000 steps, 2n is about a million, and n! is 77 years. Buying a computer a thousand times faster only shifts the wall: the age-of-the-universe line moves up by a factor of 1,000, and n! reaches it at n = 29 instead of 27.

Polynomial is the dividing line

Polynomial time is closed under composition, does not depend on the machine model, and separates “grows gently” from “hits a wall”. It is a modelling choice, not a law: an n100 algorithm is useless, and 2n/100 is fine for small n. But as the line between tractable and hopeless it works remarkably well.

The travelling salesperson lab

A salesperson must visit every city once and return home by the shortest route. Click the map to place cities, then pick a method. Brute force measures every tour and is exact but stops at 9 cities. The other three are fast and give up the guarantee of the best answer.

Every added city multiplies brute force's work. With 9 cities there are 8! = 40,320 tours; 12 cities would already mean 39.9 million, and 20 cities 1.2 × 1017. The clever exact method, Held–Karp dynamic programming, still costs O(n² 2n): far better than n!, yet still exponential.

The heuristics are the opposite trade. Nearest neighbour does about n² work, 2-opt keeps repairing crossings, and the MST method comes with a proof. How close do they land to the true optimum on your map?

Why the MST bound holds

Delete one edge from any tour and you have a spanning path, which is a tree, so the minimum spanning tree weighs no more than the optimal tour. Walking the tree around costs 2 × (tree weight). Shortcutting past already-visited cities never lengthens the walk (triangle inequality). So the tour is at most 2 × tree ≤ 2 × optimum. Christofides (1976) improves the factor to 1.5.

Reductions: one problem wearing another's clothes

A reduction from A to B is a polynomial-time recipe that turns any instance of A into an instance of B with the same yes or no answer. If you had a fast algorithm for B, you would get one for A: convert, solve, read off the answer. We write A ≤p B, and it says B is at least as hard as A.

The direction trips everyone up

To show a new problem H is hard, reduce a known hard problem to H: known ≤p H. Then a fast algorithm for H would crack the known problem. Reducing H to a known hard problem proves nothing about H: every easy problem reduces to something hard.

SAT, and the wall of NP-complete problems

A 3-SAT formula is an AND of clauses, each an OR of three literals (a variable or its negation). Is there a true/false assignment making every clause true? Toggle the variables. A clause lights up green when at least one of its literals is true.

Two things to notice. Checking one assignment is a single pass over the clauses, and finding one may mean trying most of the 2n. And the unsatisfiable preset shows an asymmetry: a “yes” comes with a short certificate, while a “no” seems to need the entire search.

NP-hard and NP-complete

A problem is NP-hard if every problem in NP reduces to it. It is NP-complete if it is NP-hard and in NP. NP-hard problems need not be in NP at all: the halting problem is NP-hard and not even decidable.

Why would any problem be NP-hard? Because of the Cook–Levin theorem (1971 and 1973): SAT is NP-complete. The idea is that a computation is locally checkable. Write the machine's tape at each moment as a row of a table; the run is valid exactly when every small window of the table obeys the machine's rule, and each window is one small clause.

Once one problem is known to be NP-complete, others follow by reduction, and the web grows. Karp showed in 1972 that 21 classic problems are all NP-complete. Click a problem below: it lights everything that becomes easy if that problem had a fast algorithm.

One wall, many doors

Every NP-complete problem reduces to every other, through SAT. So a fast algorithm for any one of them would give a fast algorithm for all of NP, and P = NP. A proof that any one of them needs exponential time would settle P ≠ NP. After more than fifty years of trying, nobody has done either.

What is proven and what is believed

Proven: P ⊆ NP; SAT is NP-complete; the reductions in the web; the halting problem is undecidable. Believed, not proven: P ≠ NP (most researchers expect it, as no polynomial algorithm has surfaced for thousands of NP-complete problems); that factoring and graph isomorphism are not NP-complete. Open: P vs NP itself, one of the seven Clay Millennium Prize problems.

“If P = NP” would not make life magically easy: an algorithm running in n1000 steps would still be useless. But it would make searching for proofs, schedules and designs as cheap as checking them, and it would break most of today's cryptography.

My problem seems NP-hard. Now what?

NP-hardness is not a verdict of “give up”. It tells you to stop hunting for a fast exact algorithm that works on every input, and to change the deal. The cleanest change is an approximation algorithm: fast, with a proof that the answer is close to optimal.

Vertex cover has a beautiful one. While some edge is uncovered, take both its endpoints. The chosen edges never share a vertex (a matching), and any cover needs at least one endpoint of each, so the optimum is at least the matching size while ours is exactly twice that.

Approximation is one tool among several. Choosing among them is a decision you can walk through. Click the answers.

Patterns and traps

Four ideas that come up whenever hardness does.

Vertex cover of size at most k can be decided by branching: pick an uncovered edge, then either its left or its right endpoint must be in the cover. That is a binary tree of depth k, so at most 2k+1 − 1 nodes, whatever the size of the graph.

The exponential part depends on k, not on n. Problems like this are fixed-parameter tractable: fine when the parameter is small.

NP-hardness is a worst-case, all-inputs statement. Restrict the inputs and the problem can fall into P. 2-SAT (clauses of two literals) is in P. Independent set and vertex cover are easy on trees and bipartite graphs. TSP with distances on a tree metric or a line is easy.

Ask what your real inputs look like before you fight the general problem.

Subset sum with target T has a dynamic programme costing O(n · T). That looks polynomial, but T is written in log T bits, so the cost is exponential in the input size. It is pseudo-polynomial, fast only when the numbers are small, which is why subset sum is still NP-complete.

The same goes for the “textbook” knapsack DP. Always ask: polynomial in what?

NP-complete means “believed to need exponential time, but a finite search always works”. Undecidable means no algorithm at all can answer every instance, however much time it gets. Turing proved in 1936 that the halting problem (“does this program stop?”) is undecidable.

The halting problem is NP-hard, but it is not in NP. Do not mix up “slow” with “impossible”.

History

1971Cook's theorem

Stephen Cook, Leonid Levin, Richard KarpSTOC 1971 · Moscow 1973 · 1972

In March 1956 Kurt Gödel wrote to a dying John von Neumann asking whether proofs of length n could be found in time growing only like n or n². Von Neumann never answered. Stephen Cook stated the question precisely in 1971 and proved that SAT is NP-complete; Leonid Levin reached the same idea independently in Moscow. In 1972 Richard Karp showed that 21 familiar problems are all equally hard. The Clay Institute put a million-dollar price on the question in 2000, and it is still unclaimed.

Sources: S. A. Cook, “The Complexity of Theorem-Proving Procedures”, STOC 1971; R. M. Karp, “Reducibility Among Combinatorial Problems”, 1972; L. A. Levin, “Universal Sequential Search Problems”, Problemy Peredachi Informatsii 9(3), 1973; Gödel's letter to von Neumann, 20 March 1956.

Practice

  1. Write a verifier
    Easy

    Given a graph, an integer k and a list of vertices, write a polynomial-time check that the list is a vertex cover of size at most k. What is its running time in terms of V and E?

  2. Decision to optimisation
    Medium

    Suppose you have a black box that answers “is there a tour of length at most k?” in polynomial time. Show how to find the length of the shortest tour with polynomially many calls. Then show how to recover the tour itself.

  3. Which way does it reduce?
    Medium

    Given that independent set is NP-complete, prove that clique is NP-hard. State the reduction, the direction, and why the yes and no answers are preserved.

  4. Beat the 2-approximation on paper
    Medium

    Draw a graph where the matching-based vertex cover is exactly twice the optimum, and another where it is optimal. Can you find a graph on 6 vertices where the ratio is strictly between 1 and 2?

  5. Cheat with the triangle inequality
    Hard

    The MST tour argument used the triangle inequality. Build a small 4-city instance whose distances violate it, and show the shortcutting step can make the tour longer than twice the tree.
