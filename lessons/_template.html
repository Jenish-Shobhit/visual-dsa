Find the maximum

Walk the list once, keep the best value in your hand, and swap it only for something strictly larger.

The problem

A weather station logs the temperature every hour. At midnight it has to report the day’s high. You cannot sort the readings first, and you can only look at each number as it arrives.

Try it yourself. Which reading below is the highest?

You probably scanned from left to right and kept the largest number in your head. That habit is the whole algorithm. The rest of this lesson makes it precise and proves it is right.

Build the intuition

Picture a queue of people walking past you one at a time. You hold a card with the height of the tallest person so far. When someone taller walks by, you rewrite the card.

Analogy: the tallest person in the queue

You never compare two people in the queue with each other. Each newcomer is compared once, with the card in your hand.

Where the analogy breaks

People walk past once and are gone. A list stays in memory, so a program could look back. Finding the maximum never needs to, which is why it also works on a stream of readings.

One value in hand

Start by holding the first value. Then compare every other value with the one in your hand, exactly once, from left to right. Replace the held value only when the new one is strictly larger.

Definition

An invariant is a fact that stays true at every step of a loop. Here it is: best is the largest of the values scanned so far.

Step through the figure. The dashed region is everything already scanned. The invariant says nothing inside it is larger than best.

Key idea

When the scan reaches the end, the scanned region is the whole list. So the invariant becomes the answer: best is the largest value in the list.

Why strictly larger?

With > the first copy of a repeated maximum wins, and equal values never cost an update. With >= the last copy wins. Both give the same value, but a different position.

What it costs

Count two operations: comparisons (a[i] > best) and updates (best = a[i]). Comparisons never depend on the order of the input. Updates do.

Why n − 1 comparisons, and not n?

The first value is taken without comparing it to anything. Each of the other n − 1 values is compared exactly once. No correct algorithm can do better: every value except the maximum must lose at least one comparison, or you could not rule it out.

Where does Hn − 1 come from?

In a random order, the value at position i is the largest of the first i with probability 1/i. Adding those chances for i = 2 … n gives 1/2 + 1/3 + … + 1/n = Hn − 1, which grows like ln n.

Variations and patterns

Change what you hold, or the rule for replacing it, and the same scan answers other questions.

Hold the smallest value so far and replace it when a value is strictly smaller. Same cost: n − 1 comparisons.


        
Keep bestIndex instead of best and compare a[i] with a[bestIndex]. Many interview questions want the position.

        
Hold first and second. A new value larger than first pushes first down to second. Still one pass.

History

      

        
1968published

        
Donald KnuthThe Art of Computer Programming, volume 1

        

          
Knuth chose “find the maximum” as the first algorithm he analysed in full. He asked how many times the held value changes, and showed that on a random order the average is Hn − 1: the harmonic numbers, hiding inside the humblest loop.

        

        
Source: D. E. Knuth, The Art of Computer Programming, Vol. 1, §1.2.10, “Analysis of an Algorithm”.
