Lecture 01 left an asterisk on every “append is Θ(1).” Here we cash it. A single append to a growing array can copy the entire array — a genuine Θ(n) spike — and yet a run of n appends costs under 3n steps in total, fewer than three apiece. This lecture explains how an operation that is sometimes linear can be always constant on average, and gives you the three instruments that prove it: the aggregate, the accountant's ledger, and the potential.

A number to unsettle you. Append a million items to an initially empty dynamic array that doubles when full. One unlucky append copies 524,288 existing elements to a bigger block — a Big-Θ(n) operation, plainly not constant. So is the structure “O(1) append” a lie?

No. The million appends together perform fewer than 3,000,000 primitive steps. The spikes are real but rare, and the cheap steps in between have already paid for them. That is not an average over luck — it is a guarantee over every sequence. Learning to see, and to prove, that guarantee is the whole of this lecture.

01 the asterisk comes due

An operation that is sometimes linear, yet always constant

I drew a sharp line in Lecture 01 between the operation — the abstract thing we wish to perform — and the engine that carries it out. We met the engine's bill there: contiguity makes access(i) a single arithmetic step but charges Θ(n) to edit the interior, because the no-gaps promise forces a cascade of shifts. I attached an asterisk to one happy exception. Appending at the end disturbs no one — provided spare capacity exists. Manufacturing that spare capacity cheaply is the whole trick, and the asterisk now comes due.

Here is the difficulty stated plainly. A dynamic array keeps a backing block larger than it strictly needs, so most appends just drop a value into a waiting slot — one step. But the block has a fixed size. When it fills, there is nowhere to put the next element, so we must allocate a larger block and copy every existing element across. That copy is Θ(n). So append has two faces: a constant-time common case and a linear-time rare case. Its worst-case-per-operation cost is therefore Θ(n), and if that were the honest figure, dynamic arrays would be useless.

They are not useless; they are the default sequence in every language's standard library. The resolution is that worst-case-per-operation is the wrong lens here. It prices each append as if every one might be the expensive one — but they cannot all be expensive, because each costly copy doubles the runway before the next. The expensive operations are forced to be rare by the very mechanism that makes them expensive. To see that, we need a lens that prices a sequence, not an isolated call. That lens is amortized analysis, and it is the second of this course's three ways to measure cost — worst-case, amortized, and (later, Lecture 05) expected.

the formal lensAmortization is what you reach for whenever a structure has occasional expensive operations whose cost is structurally bounded by the cheap operations preceding them. The art is to make “preceding cheap work pays for the rare spike” into a theorem, not a hope.
02 objectives & prerequisites

What you will be able to do

  • State precisely what amortized cost is — a worst-case bound on the average over a sequence — and distinguish it cleanly from worst-case-per-operation and from average/expected cost.
  • Prove the doubling array's O(1) amortized append three independent ways: by aggregate (sum it all up), by accounting (assign credits), and by the potential method (a function of state).
  • Derive why the growth must be geometric: that doubling (or any factor a>1) gives Θ(1) amortized while adding a constant gives Θ(n) — and read off the time–space trade-off in the factor.
  • Build a potential function, verify its two obligations (Φ starts at 0 and never goes negative), and compute amortized cost as actual + ΔΦ.
  • Diagnose the failures: the halve-at-half-full thrash, arithmetic growth, mistaking amortized for worst-case under a real-time deadline, and a potential that dips below its start.
  • Recognise the same telescoping argument when it returns — in the binary counter, the two-stack queue, KMP's failure links (Lecture 18), and union-find's near-constant operations (Lecture 12).

03 the definition, and what it is not

What “amortized” actually means

The word is borrowed from finance: to amortise a debt is to spread a large one-off cost into small regular instalments. We do the same to a large one-off computation. The definition is deceptively simple and worth stating with care.

definitionThe amortized cost of an operation is a value α such that, for every sequence of m operations starting from an empty structure, the total actual cost is at most m·α. Equivalently: assign each operation a charge so the charges always sum to an upper bound on the true work. We then say each operation “costs α amortized.”

Read the quantifier slowly: for every sequence. There is no probability here, no typical input, no luck. Amortized cost is a deterministic worst-case guarantee — just a worst case taken over a whole run rather than over a single call. If I tell you append is O(1) amortized, I am promising that an adversary who hand-picks the cruellest million appends still cannot make them total more than about three million steps. That is a far stronger and more useful promise than it first sounds.

Two confusions to kill immediately, because both are everywhere.

Amortized is not worst-case-per-operation

A single append is still Θ(n) in the worst case — that fact is untouched. Amortized analysis does not make the costly step cheap; it proves the costly step is rare enough that the average over the sequence is constant. The individual spike survives. If your setting cannot tolerate the spike — a hard deadline on each operation — amortized O(1) is not the guarantee you need (we return to this in §13).

Amortized is not average-case

This is the subtle one, and the one I care most that you get right. Average-case (or expected) cost averages over a distribution of inputs or random coin-flips: a hash table is O(1) expected because, if the keys land badly, it can degrade — you were merely unlikely to be unlucky. Amortized cost averages over the operations in one fixed sequence, with no randomness at all: a dynamic array is O(1) amortized because no sequence, however chosen, can be expensive. One is a statement about probability; the other is a theorem about arithmetic. Conflating them is the most common error in this entire subject.

04 the worked structure

The doubling array, precisely

Fix the engine before we price it. A dynamic array D holds two numbers and a block of memory: a backing array D.A of capacity C (its physical length), and a size n ≤ C (how many slots are live). The invariant is simply 0 ≤ n ≤ C; the slack C − n is the runway of waiting slots.

APPEND(D, x):
  if D.n == D.C:                 // full — no runway left
    GROW(D)                       // the rare, costly case
  D.A[D.n] ← x                  // the common case: one write
  D.n ← D.n + 1

GROW(D):
  newC ← if D.C == 0 then 1 else 2 × D.C   // DOUBLE the capacity
  B ← allocate array of length newC
  for i in 0 .. D.n−1:        // copy every live element across
    B[i] ← D.A[i]
  D.A ← B;  D.C ← newC      // old block is freed

The cost model, in the RAM units of Lecture 00: a cheap append is 1 (a single write). A growing append copies the n live elements and then writes the new one, costing n + 1. We start from an empty array with no backing store (C = 0); the first append allocates a single cell, copying nothing. The doubling rule — C → 2C — is the one design choice that makes everything work, and §05 shows why no gentler rule will do.

The picture below is the physical event hidden inside GROW: a full block of capacity C is abandoned for a fresh block of capacity 2C, every element copied across, leaving the new block exactly half full. That “half full just after growth” fact is the seed of the entire analysis — remember it.

05 the one design choice that matters

Why double, and not merely add?

It is tempting to grow more frugally — “when full, just add room for ten more.” That instinct, growing by a fixed constant k, is a catastrophe, and seeing why is the cleanest way to understand why doubling works.

Arithmetic growth (add k each time). Now the array fills again after only k appends, so over n appends we resize about n/k times, and the j-th resize copies jk elements. The total copy work is an arithmetic series:

total copies under arithmetic growth
∑j=1n/k jk = k· (n/k)(n/k+1)2 ≈ n22k = Θ(n2)

Total work Θ(n²) over n appends means Θ(n) amortized per append — no better than rebuilding from scratch each time. And note: enlarging k only shrinks the constant. Even k = 1{,}000{,}000 leaves append Θ(n) amortized; for n = 109 it is still quadratic. The defect is qualitative, not a matter of tuning.

Geometric growth (multiply by a > 1). Multiply the capacity instead, and the resize sizes themselves form a geometric series running backwards from n: roughly n, n/a, n/a², … Summed, a geometric series is dominated by its largest term, so the total is a constant multiple of n:

amortized copies per append, growth factor a
1n · n(1+1a+1a2+⋯) = aa−1

For a = 2 that is 2 copies amortized; add the one write and you get the headline 3. The factor is a dial trading time against space, and real libraries set it knowingly:

Amortisation does not make the costly step cheap. It proves the costly step was already paid for.
06 method I

Aggregate analysis: just add it all up

The bluntest method, and the one to try first. Compute the total cost T(n) of any n operations directly, then declare the amortized cost to be T(n)/n. No credits, no functions — just an honest sum. It works whenever the total is easy to bound from above.

For n appends starting from empty, separate the bill into the two kinds of work. The writes are easy: exactly one per append, n in all. The copies happen only at resizes, when the size passes a power of two; the copy amounts are 1, 2, 4, … up to the last resize, which copies fewer than n elements. That is a geometric series, and the decisive fact about a geometric series is that its sum is barely more than its largest term:

total copies = a doubling series < 2n
1+2+4+⋯+2k = 2k+1−1 < 2·2k ≤ 2n

So T(n) < n (writes) + 2n (copies) = 3n, and the amortized cost per append is

aggregate amortized cost
c― = T(n)n < 3nn = 3 = O(1)

That is the whole proof. The single insight carrying it is the one drawn in §04: each resize copies what all previous resizes copied, plus one — so the last resize alone is as expensive as the entire history before it, and the grand total is just twice the final copy. The rarity of the spikes is not assumed; it is forced by doubling and counted exactly.

Aggregate analysis has a limitation worth naming. It hands every operation the same amortized cost, T(n)/n. That is fine when one operation dominates, but when a structure mixes operations of genuinely different amortized cost — cheap pushes and an expensive-but-rare flush, say — we want to charge them differently and still bound the total. For that we need a ledger.

07 method II

The accounting (banker's) method: pay it forward

Now we run a bank. Each operation is charged an amortized cost we get to choose — the price at the counter. When an operation is charged more than its real work, the surplus is stored as credit on a specific spot in the structure. When an operation's real work exceeds its charge, it pays the difference out of stored credit. One law governs the whole scheme:

the banker's invariantThe total stored credit must never go negative. The bank may not lend against money it does not have. If credit stays ≥ 0 after every operation, then total charges ≥ total actual cost — so the charges are a valid amortized bound.

Why does the invariant suffice? Total credit = total charged − total spent. Credit ≥ 0 says total charged ≥ total spent = total actual cost. The charges therefore upper-bound the true work — which is exactly the definition of amortized cost from §03.

The scheme for the doubling array: charge 3 coins per append. Spend them like this:

  • 1 pays for the immediate write of the new element. (Real work, done now.)
  • 2 are banked on the new element itself, to prepay copy work later.

Why exactly two? Here is the accountant's intuition, and it is lovely. When the array next doubles, every element must be copied — but only the elements added since the previous doubling still carry credit; the older half spent theirs in that previous doubling and are now “freeloaders.” Because the array is always half full just after a doubling (§04, Figure 1), the new half is exactly as numerous as the old half. So give each new element two coins: one to copy itself at the next doubling, and one to copy one freeloader from the old half. Every copy at the next doubling is then prepaid, and the bank never dips below zero.

Where exactly is the credit stored, formally?

Attach the credit to elements, not to the array as a whole. State the invariant as: after every operation, the number of stored coins equals twice the number of elements in the upper half of the current block — i.e. 2·max(0, n − C/2). A cheap append adds one element to the upper half and banks its 2 coins, preserving it. A doubling spends all upper-half coins to perform the copies, then the new block is half full so the upper half is empty and credit is 0 — still satisfying the invariant. You will notice this stored-coin formula is 2n - C whenever n ≥ C/2. Hold that thought; in §08 it becomes the potential function, and in §10 we see the accounting method and the potential method were the same method all along.

08 method III

The potential method: stored energy in the structure

The accounting method tracks credit operation by operation. The potential method does something more elegant: it makes the total stored credit a function of the structure's current state, written Φ(D) — think of it as potential energy latent in the configuration, prepaid work waiting to be spent. We never again track coins by hand; we just read Φ off the state before and after. The amortized cost of an operation is its actual cost plus the change in potential:

amortized cost via potential
c^i = ci + Φ(Di) − Φ(Di−1)

Why is this legal? Sum the amortized costs over a whole run and the potential terms telescope — each Φ(Di) appears once with a plus and once with a minus, cancelling all but the endpoints:

the telescoping sum — the engine of the method
∑i=1m c^i = ∑i=1m ci + Φ(Dm) − Φ(D0)

So if we choose Φ with two obligations — Φ(D0) = 0 and Φ(Di) ≥ 0 for all i — then the trailing terms can only help us: Σ ci = Σ ĉi − Φ(Dm) ≤ Σ ĉi. The actual total is bounded by the amortized total. These two obligations are the entire discipline of the method; forget either and the bound is invalid (see §13).

The right potential for the doubling array

Choose, exactly as §07's stored-coin formula suggested:

the potential function
Φ(D) = 2n−C

Sanity-check the two obligations and the geometry. Just after a doubling the array is half full (n = C/2), giving Φ = 2(C/2) - C = 0 — the structure is “discharged.” As we append, n climbs while C holds, so Φ rises by 2 each step until the array is full (n = C), where Φ = 2C - C = C = n — fully charged, holding exactly enough potential to pay for copying all n elements. The doubling array keeps n ≥ C/2 throughout (it is half full right after each growth and only climbs), so Φ = 2n − C ≥ 0 always, and starting empty gives Φ(D0) = 0. Both obligations met.

Now the two-line payoff. Compute the amortized cost in each of the two cases:

cheap append (n < C): actual = 1
c^ = 1 + ( Φ↑2 ) = 1+2 = 3
resize append (n = C): actual = n+1
c^ = (n+1) + (2−n) = 3

Look at what happened in the resize case. The actual cost spiked to n+1 — but the potential crashed by almost exactly the same amount. Before the resize the array was full and fully charged (Φ = n); after, it is half full and discharged (Φ = 2). That −(n-2) drop is the structure spending its stored energy to pay for the copy, leaving an amortized cost of a flat 3. The spike in actual cost and the crash in potential cancel by design. That cancellation is the whole beauty of the method, and the figure below is its portrait.

09 worked example

One trace, three ledgers

Theory earns its keep on a concrete run. Append seventeen elements into an empty dynamic array and watch all three methods certify the same total, from the same trace. One small wrinkle worth seeing: append 1 has amortized cost 2, not 3, because expanding from C=0 copies nothing. The bank therefore ends one coin richer than Φ (bank = Φ + 1 after the first op). It is the same coin, counted in two notations — which is the subject of the next section.

10 the abstraction beneath

The three methods are one idea

I am, by temperament, unsatisfied until three surface techniques resolve into one structure. They do here, and seeing it is worth more than any single proof.

Begin with the accounting method's invariant from §07: the stored credit, as a function of state, is 2n - C. Now read the potential function of §08: Φ(D) = 2n - C. They are identical. The potential function is nothing but the accountant's total bank balance, re-expressed as a formula in the structure's state instead of tracked transaction by transaction. Accounting is the potential method done by hand; the potential method is accounting with the bookkeeping closed into an equation. Choosing “3 coins, store 2” is choosing Φ = 2n - C.

Method I
Aggregate

Bound the total directly; divide by m. One amortized price for all. The honest brute-force check — and the sanity test the other two must match.

Method II
Accounting

Assign per-operation charges; bank the surplus on parts of the structure; keep credit ≥ 0. Lets different operations carry different prices.

Method III
Potential

Write the bank balance as Φ(state); read ĉ = c + ΔΦ. The same ledger, closed-form and telescoping. The most reusable.

So why teach all three? Because they differ in ergonomics, not in truth. Aggregate is fastest when one cost dominates and the sum is easy. Accounting gives the clearest intuition — “this element prepays that copy” — and shines when operations deserve unequal charges. The potential method is the most powerful and transferable: once you can name the stored energy of a structure as a function of its state, the proof becomes two lines of arithmetic, and the very same move scales up to structures whose ledgers no human would track by hand. Which brings us to the horizon.

the formal lensWhen a problem offers three methods, look for the invariant they share. Here it is the quantity 2n - C — “twice the elements past half-full.” Find that quantity and the choice of method becomes mere convenience.
11 the symmetric danger

Shrinking without thrashing

A dynamic array usually supports deletion too, and a tidy engineer wants the block to shrink when it empties so memory is reclaimed. The obvious rule is the trap: “halve the capacity when the array falls to half full.” Pair it with “double when full” and you have built a machine that can be driven to Θ(n) on every single operation.

Sit the array exactly at the boundary n = C/2 and feed it append, delete, append, delete, … The append fills it, triggering a doubling (copy C). The delete drops it to half full, triggering a halving (copy C/2). Each operation copies Θ(n) elements; nothing is ever amortised because no cheap operations accumulate between the expensive ones. The amortized bound is destroyed. The cause, in potential terms: after each resize the structure sits right at the next resize's trigger, with zero runway — exactly what doubling was careful to avoid.

The fix is hysteresis: grow at full (double), but shrink only at quarter full (halve). The gap between the two thresholds is the runway. After a doubling the array is half full; after a halving it is also half full (n = C/4 elements in the new capacity C/2). Either way it rests at the midpoint, so Θ(C) operations of either kind must occur before the next resize fires. The amortized bound returns for both operations.

The potential function that proves both directions O(1)

The asymmetry of the thresholds forces a two-piece potential, the “distance from half full” in each regime:

Φ = { 2n−Cif n≥C/2 C/2−nif n<C/2

It is 0 at exactly half full (both branches agree), rises toward either trigger, and is ≥ 0 everywhere with Φ(empty) = 0. A grow fires when Φ (upper branch) has climbed to C; a shrink fires when Φ (lower branch) has climbed to C/4. In both cases the stored potential is large enough to pay the copy, and a routine case-check shows every operation is amortized O(1). This is the classic CLRS dynamic-table result; the quarter-full threshold is what makes it go through.

12 horizon

The same trick bounds union-find

I want you to leave this lecture seeing the potential method not as a fact about arrays but as a general instrument, because its most striking use is still ten lectures away. In Lecture 12 we build union-find (the disjoint-set structure) to track connected components — the engine inside Kruskal's minimum-spanning-tree algorithm. It supports find (which set is x in?) and union (merge two sets), and with two optimisations — union by rank and path compression — its operations run in inverse-Ackermann time:

union-find with both heuristics
m operations on n elements : O(m·α(n))  amortized,  α(n)≤4  for all practical n

The function α(n), the inverse of Ackermann's explosively-growing function, is below 5 for any n you could ever store — effectively a constant, though provably not one. No single find is O(1) worst-case; the near-constant cost is purely amortized, and the proof is a potential argument exactly in the shape you just learned. The difference is only in the potential function: instead of 2n - C, one assigns each node a potential derived from its rank and how far its rank sits below its parent's, so that the rare expensive find — the one that walks a long path — is paid for by the potential released when path compression flattens that path. Same telescoping sum; same “spike cancels a potential drop”; a vastly cleverer Φ.

That is the promise of this method, and the reason it is worth mastering on the humble array first: the technique you just used to tame a doubling array is, structurally, the technique that tames one of the most subtle data structures in the field. When you meet it again in Lecture 12, you will recognise the machinery — and the recognition is the point.

13 where it breaks

Failure modes & common mistakes

Keep the gap between the clean theorem and the running machine in view. Amortized analysis is unusually easy to misquote — here is where it bites.

1 · Amortized is not a per-operation guarantee — the real-time trap

The single resize really does cost Θ(n). If your system has a hard deadline on every operation — a 60-frames-per-second render loop with 16ms per frame, an audio callback, a pacemaker — then “O(1) amortized” is cold comfort the frame the array decides to copy a million elements. Remedies: pre-reserve capacity when the final size is known (one growth up front, then all cheap appends), or de-amortize by spreading the copy incrementally across many operations so each does O(1) worst-case work (this is Practice Problem 3). Know which guarantee your setting demands.

2 · Amortized is not average-case

Worth repeating because it is the field's favourite slip. There is no probability in our proof; the bound holds for the adversary's worst sequence. Do not “defend” an amortized bound by saying expensive operations are unlikely — they are not unlikely, they are infrequent by construction, which is stronger. And do not import amortized reasoning to justify a hash table's O(1): that one really is probabilistic and really can be unlucky (Lecture 08).

3 · The halve-at-half-full thrash

The §11 disaster, restated because it is a real bug in real code: making the shrink threshold meet the grow threshold lets an adversary (or an unlucky workload) force Θ(n) on every operation. Always leave hysteresis — shrink at a quarter, not a half. The same logic forbids shrinking eagerly after a single delete.

4 · Arithmetic growth — the frugal-looking Θ(n)

“Grow by 10 each time” or “capacity += 1” looks thrifty and quietly makes append Θ(n) amortized (§05). It is one of the most common performance bugs in hand-rolled containers, and it hides because small tests never reveal the quadratic. Growth must be multiplicative.

5 · A potential that dips below its start

The method is only valid if Φ(Di) ≥ Φ(D0) for all i (with Φ(D0)=0 the usual normalisation). If your Φ can go negative, the telescoped -Φ(Dm) term becomes positive and your “bound” can sit below the true cost — a proof that proves nothing. Always discharge both obligations explicitly: Φ starts at zero, and Φ never drops below it. A negative potential is the single most common error in student potential proofs.

6 · Amortization can break under persistence / backtracking

The whole argument assumes a single linear timeline of operations, so that spent potential stays spent. If a structure is persistent — you can save an old version and resume from it — an adversary can return to a fully-charged state (an about-to-resize array) and trigger the expensive operation again and again, each time from the same saved state, paying the spike repeatedly without the cheap operations that funded it. The amortized bound is then false. This is why naive amortized structures need care (and techniques like lazy evaluation) to survive in a functional setting. Amortized bounds are about histories, not snapshots.

7 · Forgetting to free, or to null, the old block

An engine-level mistake, not an analysis one: after GROW copies into the new block, the old block must be released, and in a garbage-collected language any references the array held to popped objects must be nulled (the classic loitering-reference leak from Lecture 01's stack). The amortized time bound says nothing about space hygiene; mind both.

The binary counter

aggregate & potential · the canonical second example

A k-bit binary counter starts at 0 and supports INCREMENT, which flips the trailing run of 1-bits to 0 and sets the next bit to 1 (e.g. 0111 → 1000). A single increment can flip up to k bits. Prove that n increments cost O(n) total — i.e. O(1) amortized per increment — (a) by aggregate analysis (count how often each bit flips), and (b) by the potential method with Φ = the number of 1-bits currently set. Show your Φ satisfies both obligations, and compute the amortized cost of an increment that resets t ones. (c) What breaks if the counter overflows its k bits?

De-amortize the doubling array — make every append O(1) worst-case

incremental resizing · real-time guarantee

The single resize spike (§13, failure 1) is unacceptable under a hard per-operation deadline. Redesign append so that every call is O(1) worst-case, not merely amortized, by copying the old block into the new one incrementally — a constant number of elements per append — while both blocks are live during a growth phase. (a) Describe the two-array invariant and how a read finds an element mid-migration. (b) Choose the per-append copy quota and prove the migration finishes before the new block itself fills (so a second growth never overtakes an unfinished first). (c) State the time per operation and the peak space overhead, and contrast both with the plain doubling array.

The multipop stack

accounting & potential · a single op can be Θ(n)

Augment a stack with MULTIPOP(k), which pops min(k, size) elements in one call, alongside ordinary push(x) and pop(). A single MULTIPOP can cost Θ(n). Prove that any sequence of n operations on an initially empty stack costs O(n) total — so all operations are O(1) amortized — (a) by an accounting argument (what does each push prepay, and for what?), and (b) by the potential method with Φ = the current number of elements on the stack. Give the amortized cost of push, pop, and a MULTIPOP that removes t items, and confirm they sum correctly over a run.

15 recap & next

The one thing to remember

Amortized cost is a worst-case average over a sequence, not an average over luck — and an expensive operation is no problem if the cheap operations have already paid for it. The doubling array makes every append O(1) amortized because each Θ(n) copy doubles the runway before the next, so the copies form a geometric series summing to under 2n. Prove it three ways that are secretly one: aggregate (total < 3n), accounting (charge 3, bank 2 per element), potential (Φ = 2n − C, and ĉ = c + ΔΦ = 3). Growth must be multiplicative; shrinking must keep hysteresis; and the very same potential argument will return to tame union-find.
