To multiply two polynomials — or two enormous integers — schoolbook arithmetic pairs every digit with every other: Θ(n²). Lecture 03 reduced the growth to Θ(n1.585) with Karatsuba. This lecture changes representation: evaluate the polynomials at carefully chosen points, multiply the values, then interpolate. The translations cost Θ(n log n) overall.

A number to unsettle you. Multiply two integers of one million digits each. Schoolbook uses about (106)2 = 1012 digit products. Karatsuba's recurrence gives an order of (106)1.585 ≈ 3 × 109 subproblems. An FFT based method needs a transform length at least twice the input length and work proportional to that length times its logarithm.

These counts omit large and different constant factors, carries, and precision management, but the growth gap is real. It comes from a change of coordinates: transform to values, multiply point by point, and transform back.

01 the idea vs. the engine

One object, told in two languages

Start with a useful separation: there is the operation we wish to perform, and there is the representation in which we perform it. Confuse them and a problem looks hard that is merely badly coordinated. Ada Lovelace described how the Analytical Engine weaves algebraic patterns as the Jacquard loom weaves leaves — but only once the pattern has been arranged onto cards the loom can read. The art is in the arranging.

The Fast Fourier Transform is a clear illustration of that art. Direct coefficient convolution takes Θ(n²) time. A polynomial also has a second, equally faithful representation as values at distinct points, where the multiplication step is pointwise. The algorithm pays to translate out of coefficient form and back.

So this lecture is not really about Fourier, or about signal processing, or even about complex numbers — those are the engine. It is about a move you will make again and again in this course: when an operation is expensive, do not grind harder at it. Ask whether a change of representation makes it disappear. The FFT is the canonical case where it does.

02 objectives & prerequisites

What you will be able to do

  • Recognise polynomial (and big-integer) multiplication as a convolution of coefficient vectors, and state why the obvious method is Θ(n²).
  • Move a polynomial between its coefficient and point‑value representations, and explain why multiplication costs Θ(n) in the second and Θ(n²) in the first.
  • Derive why the complex n-th roots of unity let evaluation split recursively — the squaring map halves the problem.
  • Build the FFT from the even/odd identity A(x)=Ae(x²)+x Ao(x²), solve its recurrence T(n)=2T(n/2)+Θ(n) to Θ(n log n), and read the butterfly.
  • Justify interpolation as the same transform run with conjugate roots and a 1/n scaling — via the summation lemma — and assemble the full Θ(n log n) multiply.
  • Diagnose the real failures: too-small transform length (circular wraparound), floating-point round-off, non‑power‑of‑two sizes, and forgotten carries.

Prerequisites

Four ideas, each a short phrase you should hold comfortably. The first two are taught in earlier lectures of this very syllabus.

03 the operation, named precisely

Multiplication is convolution

Fix the operation before any cleverness. A polynomial of degree <n is an ordered list of n coefficients:

a polynomial, in coefficient form
A(x)= ∑j=0n−1 ajxj = a0+ a1x+ ⋯+ an−1xn−1

Multiply two of them, A and B, each with n coefficients. The product C=A·B has degree 2n−2 — that is 2n−1 coefficients — and each one is a sum of all the cross-products whose exponents add up correctly:

the convolution — coefficient k of the product
ck= ∑i+j=k aibj = ∑i=0k aibk−i

This operation — the vector c whose k-th entry sums aibj over all i+j=k — is the convolution of a and b, written c = a ∗ b. It is everywhere: a moving average convolves a signal with a window; a probability distribution of the sum of two dice is the convolution of their distributions; a polynomial product is the convolution of coefficients. Solve fast multiplication and you have solved fast convolution — they are one problem.

The direct method computes every aibj exactly once: n² products, Θ(n²) work. Integers are the same story in disguise: a number written in base 10 is just a polynomial evaluated at x=10, so multiplying two n-digit numbers is convolving their digit vectors, then propagating carries. Everything below about polynomials transfers verbatim to the integers.

the formal lensName the operation as a map on vectors, not on “polynomials.” The instant you see convolution, you may forget multiplication ever mentioned x: you are transforming one length-2n vector into another. That re-framing is what exposes the structure the next sections exploit.
04 the second representation

A polynomial's two faces

Here is the lever. A polynomial of degree <n can be pinned down in two utterly different ways, and they carry exactly the same information.

Face one — coefficients. The list (a₀,a₁,…,an−1). This is what we have been using.

Face two — point‑values. Pick any n distinct input points x₀,…,xn−1 and list what the polynomial evaluates to at each: {(x₀,A(x₀)),…,(xn−1,A(xn−1))}. The deep fact that makes this a faithful representation is the uniqueness of interpolation: through n points with distinct x-coordinates there passes exactly one polynomial of degree <n. (Two such agreeing at n points would differ by a degree-<n polynomial with n roots — impossible unless it is zero.) So the n values, like the n coefficients, name the polynomial without ambiguity.

Now watch what multiplication costs in each language. In coefficients it is the Θ(n²) convolution. In point‑values, if we have A and B evaluated at the same points, the product's value at each point is just the product of the two values:

multiplication in point-value form is pointwise
C(xk) = A(xk) ⋅ B(xk)

One multiplication per point. Θ(n) for the whole product, against Θ(n²) for the same answer in the other language. The catch — and it is the only catch — is that C has degree 2n−2, so it needs 2n−1 point‑values to be determined. We must therefore evaluate A and B at at least 2n−1 points (we will pad up to the next power of two), treating each as a degree-<2n polynomial with high coefficients zero.

05 where the difficulty went

The cost moved to the door

Look hard at Figure 1, because it contains a trap as well as a strategy. We have made the middle step cheap, but we have not yet said what the two blue translations cost. Evaluation — turning n coefficients into n values — and interpolation — turning n values back into n coefficients — are where the whole problem now lives.

And here is the trap: if we choose the evaluation points carelessly, evaluation is itself Θ(n²). Evaluating one polynomial at one point by Horner's rule is Θ(n); do that at n arbitrary points and you are back to Θ(n²), having gained nothing. The detour would be a scenic route to the same toll.

The points are not a detail. Choosing the evaluation points is the entire algorithm.

So the question sharpens to something precise and answerable: is there a set of n points so specially structured that evaluating any polynomial at all of them, together, costs less than doing them one at a time? We want the n evaluations to share work. The answer is yes. We will derive one useful choice from a recursive symmetry requirement. This is the move my method always reaches for: do not optimise the computation as given; find the abstraction (here, the right points) at which the computation reorganises itself.

06 reverse-engineering the points

The magic points: complex roots of unity

Let us derive the points from what we need of them, rather than pulling them from a hat. To evaluate A at many points by divide and conquer, split its coefficients by the parity of their index — evens in one bucket, odds in another:

split a polynomial into even- and odd-indexed coefficients
Ae(y) = a0+ a2y+ a4y2+⋯ , Ao(y) = a1+ a3y+ a5y2+⋯

Each half has n/2 coefficients. They reassemble into A by the one identity this whole lecture turns on — verify it term by term:

the even/odd identity — the algebraic heart of the FFT
A(x) = Ae(x2) + x⋅ Ao(x2)

To evaluate A at our n points, this identity says we need Ae and Ao at the squares of those points. Two subproblems — good. But each is a polynomial in x² evaluated over the set {x₀²,…,xn−1²}, and in general squaring n distinct numbers gives n distinct squares. The subproblems would still be size n, not n/2. No saving — unless the squares collide.

So demand it. We want a set of n points whose squares number only n/2 — a set that collapses by half under squaring, and keeps collapsing, recursively, all the way down. The n-th roots of unity, the n solutions of zn=1, give us exactly this structure and a simple inverse transform.

the n-th roots of unity
ωnk = e2πik/n , k=0,1,…,n−1 where ωn=e2πi/n

They sit equally spaced around the unit circle. Now square one: (ωnk)² = ωn2k = ωn/2k — squaring an n-th root yields an (n/2)-th root. And it is exactly two-to-one: the points ωnk and ωnk+n/2 — diametrically opposite, since ωnn/2=eiπ=−1 makes them negatives of one another — square to the same value. So the n roots, squared, give precisely n/2 distinct points, which are themselves the (n/2)-th roots of unity: the structure we demanded, delivered exactly, and self-similar at every level.

07 the recursion

Divide and conquer: the FFT

Assemble it. To evaluate A (degree <n, with n a power of two) at all n roots of unity ωn0,…,ωnn−1:

  1. Divide. Form Ae and Ao, each of n/2 coefficients.
  2. Conquer. Recursively evaluate Ae and Ao at the n/2 roots ωn/20,…,ωn/2n/2−1 — which are exactly the squares of our points.
  3. Combine. For each k in 0…n/2−1, the identity A(x)=Ae(x²)+x Ao(x²) reads off two outputs from one pair of recursive values, because ωnk+n/2=−ωnk while their squares are equal:
the combine step — two outputs from one shared computation
A(ωnk) = Ae(ωn/2k) + ωnk Ao(ωn/2k) A(ωnk+n/2) = Ae(ωn/2k) − ωnk Ao(ωn/2k)

Two outputs — one in the top half of the answer, one in the bottom half — from one multiplication ωnk·Ao(…) computed once. That sharing is what makes the combine step Θ(n) rather than Θ(n²). The work obeys

the FFT recurrence (Lecture 03's Master Theorem, case 2)
T(n) = 2T(n/2) + Θ(n) = Θ(nlogn)

This is the merge-sort recurrence exactly. Two halves, linear glue, logarithmically many levels — the same shape Lecture 03 taught you to recognise on sight. The map from coefficients to values at the roots of unity is the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT); this recursive way of computing it in Θ(n log n) instead of the definition's Θ(n²) is the Fast Fourier Transform.

08 the combine step, drawn

The butterfly

The combine step has a shape so distinctive it earned a name. Take one recursive even-value E=Ae(ωn/2k) and one odd-value O=Ao(ωn/2k). Compute the twiddle factor t=ωnk·O once. Then two outputs fall out:

one butterfly — two outputs, one multiply
A(ωnk) = E+t A(ωnk+n/2) = E−t with t=ωnkO

An E shared, an O twiddled once, an add and a subtract. Cross two lines, fork two outputs — the wings give it its name. A full FFT level is just n/2 of these butterflies side by side; the whole transform is log n stacked levels of them. Below, the network for n=4 — the exact size of the worked example in §10.

09 the road home

Interpolation is the same transform, inverted

We can now go down the left arrow of Figure 1 in Θ(n log n) and across the bottom in Θ(n). The right arrow — interpolation, recovering C's coefficients from its values at the roots — looks like it could be a different, harder problem. It is not. It is the same FFT, with two tiny edits.

Write the DFT as a matrix. Stacking A(ωnj)=∑kakωnjk for all j is multiplication of the coefficient vector by the Vandermonde matrix V with entries Vjk=ωnjk. Interpolation is therefore multiplication by V−1. The miracle is how clean that inverse is, and the engine is one lemma about how roots of unity sum.

the summation lemma — the geometric series that makes it all work
∑k=0n−1 ωnk(j−l) = { nif j=l 0if j≠l

When j=l every term is ωn0=1, summing to n. When j≠l it is a geometric series in the ratio ωnj−l≠1 with ratio q satisfying q≠1 and qn=1. Its sum is (1-qn)/(1-q)=0. This says V times its own conjugate-transpose is n times the identity — so the inverse is just the conjugate, scaled:

the inverse transform — conjugate the roots, divide by n
aj = 1n ∑k=0n−1 a^k ωn−jk

Compare it to the forward transform: identical, but with ωn replaced by its conjugate ωn−1 (run the circle backwards) and a final divide by n. So the inverse FFT is the forward FFT with two edits — you reuse the very same butterfly code. A clean way to say it in one line: IFFT(y)=​conj(FFT(conj(y)))/n. The road home costs exactly what the road out did.

show the one-line proof that V·conj(V) = nI

The (j,l) entry of V·conj(V) is ∑k Vjk conj(Vkl) = ∑k ωnjkωn−kl = ∑k ωnk(j−l). By the summation lemma that is n when j=l and 0 otherwise — i.e. n I. Hence V−1 = (1/n) conj(V), whose (j,k) entry is (1/n)ωn−jk. That is the inverse formula above. The whole interpolation problem is dispatched by one geometric-series cancellation.

11 the assembled algorithm

The full multiply, and its bill

Here is the whole thing, four lines of strategy made literal. FFT is the recursion of §07–08; IFFT is its two-edit twin from §09.

FFT(a):                          # a: length n, n a power of two
  n ← length(a)
  if n == 1: return a            # a 1-point DFT is the value itself
  E ← FFT(a[0::2])             # even-indexed coefficients
  O ← FFT(a[1::2])             # odd-indexed coefficients
  y ← array(n);  w ← 1;  wn ← exp(2πi / n)
  for k in 0 .. n/2 − 1:
    t ← w * O[k]                 # the twiddle, computed ONCE
    y[k]        ← E[k] + t        # A(ωₙᵏ)
    y[k + n/2]  ← E[k] − t        # A(−ωₙᵏ), the antipode
    w ← w * wn                   # advance to the next root
  return y

IFFT(y):
  return conj(FFT(conj(y))) / length(y)   # conjugate roots, ÷ n

MULTIPLY(A, B):                    # coefficient vectors
  L ← len(A) + len(B) − 1        # nonempty integer coefficients
  N ← next power of two ≥ L
  pad A, B to length N with zeros
  FA ← FFT(A);  FB ← FFT(B)    # compute each transform once
  Ĉ ← [ FA[k] * FB[k] for k in 0..N−1 ]   # Θ(n)
  c ← IFFT(Ĉ)
  return [ round(real(c[k])) for k in 0..L−1 ]    # trim padding; rounding error must be < 0.5

Three transforms and a linear pass: Θ(n log n) overall. Set against the alternatives we have met, the ledger is decisive.

What this unlocks

The payoff is wider than fast multiplication. Big integers: digits are coefficients in base 10 (or, in practice, base 216); convolve, then propagate carries — this is how arbitrary-precision libraries multiply million-digit numbers, and a refinement underlies the O(n log n) integer multiplication of Harvey–van der Hoeven (2019). Convolutions everywhere: signal filtering, image blurs, large-kernel correlations, and probability — the distribution of a sum of independent variables is the convolution of their distributions, so the FFT computes it in Θ(n log n). String matching with wildcards (Practice 3) reduces to a few convolutions. The lesson generalises past this one transform: find the basis in which your operation diagonalises, and it becomes pointwise.

12 where it breaks

Failure modes & common mistakes

The FFT is beautiful in theory and treacherous in the small print. The clean derivation hid four practical hazards; here is where a correct-looking implementation silently returns garbage.

1 · Too-short transform → circular wraparound

This is the cardinal sin. If you transform at only n points when the product has 2n−1 coefficients, you do not get the product — you get its circular convolution, where coefficients past index n−1 wrap around and add back onto the low coefficients, corrupting them. The DFT of length N computes convolution modulo xN−1. Always pick N ≥ deg A + deg B + 1 (then round up to a power of two). The symptom — low coefficients too large by exactly a high coefficient — is unmistakable once you know it.

2 · Floating-point round-off

Many roots of unity cannot be represented exactly in finite binary floating point; the arithmetic is in double. Integer answers emerge as 2.9999999998 or carry 1.3×10−13i of imaginary dust, so you must round the real part. That is safe only while the rounding error stays below 0.5. The accumulated error depends on the transform size, coefficient magnitudes, and implementation. Exact representability of the final integer alone does not guarantee correct rounding: the error in each recovered coefficient must be below 0.5. Split coefficients into smaller limbs with a justified error bound, or use the Number-Theoretic Transform (NTT): the identical algorithm run in a modular field ℤp with a primitive root of unity in place of e2πi/n — exact modular arithmetic, with no floating-point round-off. Recovering integer coefficients also requires a modulus large enough to avoid modular wraparound, or several suitable primes and reconstruction with the Chinese remainder theorem.

3 · N not a power of two

The radix-2 split assumes n halves cleanly to the leaf. Hand it N=6 and the recursion stalls. Pad up to the next power of two (the extra coefficients are zero and harmless). General sizes need mixed-radix or Bluestein's algorithm — out of scope here; in practice, just pad.

4 · The inverse's two edits, half-done

Forgetting the final /N scales every coefficient by N; using +i where the inverse needs the conjugate −i reverses and scrambles the output. Both produce plausible-looking nonsense. If your round-trip IFFT(FFT(a)) does not return a on a random vector, you have one of these — test that invariant first, before testing any multiply.

5 · Forgotten carries (integers)

Convolving digit vectors gives digit columns, not digits: a column can exceed the base. The vector [3,10,8] from (1+2x)(3+4x) is correct as a polynomial, but to print it in base 10 you must carry the 10. Check it at x=10: A(10)=21, B(10)=43, and 21·43=903; meanwhile 3+10·10+8·100=903 as well. The coefficients are right; you still owe the carry pass to turn columns into digits.

6 · FFT-ing a tiny multiply

Below the crossover, the FFT's constant factor and round-off risk make it lose to schoolbook. Do not transform a 3-coefficient product. Real libraries branch on size: schoolbook for small, Karatsuba for medium, FFT/NTT only when n is large enough to pay back the overhead.

the boundary of the modelIt is tempting to trust the clean algebra — V−1=(1/n) conj(V) is exact, after all. The gap I must keep in view is that the machine works in finite-precision double, not in ℂ. The mathematics is exact; the engine rounds. Respecting that gap — bounding the error, or stepping into ℤp where the arithmetic is exact — is the difference between a correct multiply and one that fails silently on large input.

Pair-sum frequencies via self-convolution

Wildcard string matching in O(n log n)

14 recap & next

The one thing to remember

An operation that is expensive in one representation can be cheap in another — so change representation. Direct coefficient convolution takes Θ(n²), while pointwise multiplication takes Θ(n) once the values are known. The FFT evaluates and interpolates in Θ(n log n) by using roots of unity whose squares halve recursively, giving the recurrence T(n)=2T(n/2)+Θ(n). Interpolation uses conjugate roots and a 1/n scale. Mind the length (or convolution wraps) and the precision (or rounding lies).
