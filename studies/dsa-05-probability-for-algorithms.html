Lecture 05 the third lens on cost

Probability for Algorithms

Expectation, indicator variables, and concentration bounds — the small, sharp toolkit that lets us reason about algorithms which flip their own coins. With it we will prove not just what an algorithm costs on average, but that it almost never costs much more.

A number to unsettle you. Sort a billion items with quicksort. Its worst case is Θ(n²) — about 10¹⁸ comparisons, roughly thirty years of computing. Its expected case is ≈ 2n ln n — about 4×10¹⁰ comparisons, under a minute.

Both numbers describe the same program on the same machine. The only thing that changed is a coin we chose to flip when picking each pivot. Worst-case asymptotics (Lecture 00) and amortized cost (Lecture 02) both reason about a fixed, adversarial world. This lecture installs the third cost lens — expected cost under randomness — and, crucially, the machinery to prove the bad case stays improbable.

01 possible vs probable

What changes when the algorithm flips a coin

Ada Lovelace observed that the Analytical Engine does not originate anything — it can do only what we know how to order it to perform. A coin appears to violate that creed: how can a machine that merely follows orders make a choice? It cannot. What we do instead is something more honest and far more powerful. We hand the machine a stream of random bits as an additional input, and we order it, deterministically, to use them. The randomness is not in the engine; it is a resource we feed it. The question then becomes formal again: over the distribution of those bits, what does the algorithm cost?

This is the move that rescues quicksort. There are two quite different stories one can tell, and confusing them is the most common error in this subject, so let me draw the line sharply.

  • Average-case analysis. The algorithm is deterministic; the input is drawn from some assumed distribution. The cost is averaged over inputs. This is a promise about the world — and the world rarely keeps it. Feed a “sort random arrays” analysis a nearly-sorted file and the promise is void.
  • Randomized analysis. The input is arbitrary — chosen by an adversary if you like — and the algorithm draws its own random bits. The cost is averaged over the algorithm's coins, for every input. This is a guarantee you control, because you own the coin.

Randomized quicksort makes the worst case vanish not by assuming nice inputs but by choosing the pivot at random, so that no fixed input can reliably trigger the bad split. The expected Θ(n log n) then holds for a sorted array, a reversed array, an adversarial array — all of them. That guarantee is what we are here to learn to prove, and it requires exactly three ideas: expectation, the indicator variable, and the concentration bound.

Θ(n²)

e−Θ(n)

The slogan for the whole lecture: an algorithm's worst case is what is possible; its expectation is what is typical; and a concentration bound is the proof that the possible stays improbable. Cost analysis is no longer a single number — it is a distribution, and we are learning to bound its shape.

02 objectives & prerequisites

What you will be able to do

  • Model an algorithm's cost as a random variable over a probability space, and read off its expectation from first principles.
  • Wield linearity of expectation — decompose a complicated count into a sum of 0/1 indicators and total it, without ever proving independence.
  • Derive the ≈ 2n ln n expected comparison count of randomized quicksort, end to end.
  • Compute variance and explain why the mean alone never tells you whether an algorithm is reliable.
  • Apply Markov, Chebyshev, and Chernoff–Hoeffding to bound the probability of a bad run — and know which hypotheses each one demands.
  • Distinguish average-case from randomized analysis, and name the failure that follows from mixing them up.

Prerequisites

This lecture assumes two things, both light:

discrete math — counting & summations harmonic & geometric sums asymptotic notation (Big-O / Θ / Ω)

If Θ, O, and Ω are not yet reflexes, revisit Lecture 00 — The Cost of a Computation; the harmonic number Hₙ = 1 + ½ + ⅓ + ⋯ + 1/n ≈ ln n will appear in §07 and is the one summation worth memorizing. No prior probability is assumed — we build it.

03 the probability space

The computational skeleton of chance

Strip the mystique away and probability is just weighted counting. Three objects, and no more, are needed.

A sample space Ω is the set of all possible outcomes of an experiment — every sequence of coin flips the algorithm might draw. An event A ⊆ Ω is any subset of outcomes; “the first two pivots are both the minimum” is an event. A probability measure P assigns each event a weight in [0,1], with P(Ω) = 1 and weights of disjoint events adding. For a fair coin flipped n times, Ω has 2ⁿ equally-likely outcomes, and P(A) = |A| / 2ⁿ — counting, divided by the total.

The object we actually compute with is the random variable. A random variable X is not a number and not random in itself — it is a deterministic function X : Ω → ℝ that reads an outcome and reports a number. “The number of comparisons quicksort performs” is such a function: fix the coin sequence ω and the comparison count X(ω) is fully determined. The randomness lives in which ω we drew; X merely translates it into cost.

the abstraction that matters A random variable is a lens that turns an outcome into a cost. Once an algorithm's running time is named as a random variable T, every question we care about — “typical cost?”, “risk of a slow run?” — becomes a question about the distribution of one function, and the rest of this lecture is a kit for answering those.

We will lean almost entirely on discrete random variables — counts, which take values in {0, 1, 2, ⋯} — because the cost of an algorithm is a count: of comparisons, of probes, of recursive calls. Continuous probability we will not need.

04 expectation

The center of mass of a cost

The expectation (or expected value, or mean) of a discrete random variable is its probability-weighted average — the single number a long run of trials converges to. Two equivalent forms, and you must be fluent in both:

expectation — two equivalent definitions
E[X] = ∑x x•P(X=x) = ∑ω∈Ω X(ω)•P(ω)

The left form sums over values, weighting each by how likely it is. The right form sums over outcomes, weighting each by its probability — and this second form, dull as it looks, is the secret engine of the whole lecture. It says expectation is a sum over Ω, and sums can be reordered freely. Hold that thought one section.

A die: E[X] = ⅙(1+2+3+4+5+6) = 3.5. Note the expectation need not be a value the variable can take — you never roll a 3.5. Expectation is the balance point of the distribution, not a typical outcome. A fair coin paying $1 for heads and $0 for tails has E = $0.50, a payout that can never actually occur.

One more form earns its keep for counts. For a non-negative integer variable, expectation is the sum of upper-tail probabilities:

tail-sum identity (for X in {0,1,2,⋯})
E[X]= ∑k=1∞ P(X≥k)

It converts a question about a count into a question about a chain of events — convenient when “how big does X get?” is easier to answer than “what is each value's probability?”. We will not lean on it here, but it is the kind of representational change — rewrite the same quantity in a form that exposes structure — that this whole course is built upon.

05 linearity — the loom

The most useful theorem you will ever underuse

Here is the result that does nine-tenths of the work in algorithm analysis, stated in full force:

linearity of expectation — holds for ANY X, Y
E[aX+bY] = a E[X] + b E[Y]

Read the small print, because it is the whole point: there is no independence hypothesis. X and Y may be wildly, intricately dependent — the expectation of their sum is still the sum of their expectations. Why should so strong a claim be free? Because expectation is a sum over outcomes, and addition commutes:

the one-line proof
E[X+Y] = ∑ω (X(ω)+Y(ω)) P(ω) = ∑ωX(ω)P(ω) + ∑ωY(ω)P(ω)

We split one sum into two. Dependence between X and Y is a statement about the joint distribution — about which outcomes co-occur — and this calculation never touches the joint distribution. It looks at one outcome ω at a time. That indifference to tangling is precisely why linearity is the right tool for messy, correlated quantities, and why I think of it as a loom: it weaves many dependent threads into one exact total without ever needing to know how they are knotted together.

An algorithm's worst case is what is possible; its expectation is what is typical; a concentration bound is the proof the possible stays improbable.

Contrast the product. E[XY] = E[X] E[Y] is false in general; it holds only when X and Y are independent. So the discipline is exact: sums of expectations are always free; products demand independence. Keep that boundary bright — half the errors in §11 are someone crossing it without noticing.

06 indicator variables

Turning every yes/no question into a 0/1 lever

Linearity is only worth a loom if you have threads to weave. The indicator variable manufactures them. For any event A, define a random variable that is 1 when A happens and 0 when it does not:

the indicator, and its single beautiful property
𝟙A(ω) = { 1if ω∈A 0otherwise E[𝟙A] = 1•P(A) + 0•P(A¯) = P(A)

The expectation of an indicator is the probability of its event. This little equality is the hinge on which everything turns, because it lets us trade a hard quantity — an expected count — for a sum of easy ones — individual probabilities. The recipe, which I will call the indicator method, has three steps and never varies:

  1. Decompose. Write the count you want as a sum of indicators, one per “thing that might happen”: X = 𝟙₁ + 𝟙₂ + ⋯ + 𝟙ₙ.
  2. Linearize. Apply linearity — no independence needed — so E[X] = E[𝟙₁] + ⋯ + E[𝟙ₙ].
  3. Probabilize. Replace each E[𝟙ᵢ] with P(Aᵢ) and sum the probabilities: E[X] = ∑ᵢ P(Aᵢ).

A warm-up to feel the magic. Flip n fair coins; how many heads, in expectation? Let 𝟙ᵢ = 1 if flip i is heads. Then X = ∑𝟙ᵢ and E[X] = ∑ P(\text{flip } i \text{ is heads}) = n • ½ = n/2. Trivial here — the flips happen to be independent — but watch what we did not use: independence. The same three lines work when the events are gnarled together.

07 worked example A

Quicksort's expected comparison count, derived

Now the example that makes the loom sing — and which Lecture 06 will lean on directly. Randomized quicksort: to sort an array, pick a pivot uniformly at random, partition the rest into those below and above it by comparing each to the pivot, then recurse on the two sides. Every comparison in the entire run is between some element and a pivot. We want E[X], where X is the total number of comparisons, for an arbitrary input.

def quicksort(A, lo, hi):
    if lo >= hi: return
    p = random.randint(lo, hi)         # the only randomness: a uniform pivot
    A[p], A[hi] = A[hi], A[p]
    pivot = A[hi]; i = lo
    for j in range(lo, hi):              # each pass: one comparison per element
        if A[j] < pivot:                  # <-- counted comparison
            A[i], A[j] = A[j], A[i]; i += 1
    A[i], A[hi] = A[hi], A[i]
    quicksort(A, lo, i-1); quicksort(A, i+1, hi)

Step 1 — name the right indicators. The naive attempt — an indicator per recursive call — drowns in dependence between the calls. The decisive idea is to index by pairs of values, not by calls. Sort the input values in increasing order and call them z₁ < z₂ < ⋯ < zₙ (the order statistics; this is an analysis device, not a step the algorithm performs). For each pair i < j define

the pairwise indicator
Xij = 1 iff  zi  and  zj  are ever compared.

Every comparison is element-against-pivot, so any pair of values is compared at most once in the whole run — once a pivot is chosen it is never compared again. Hence the total comparison count is exactly the number of pairs that ever meet:

decompose — total comparisons as a sum over pairs
X= ∑i=1n−1 ∑j=i+1n Xij E[X] = ∑i<j P(zi,zj compared)

Step 2 — the key probability. Fix i < j and consider the block of values between them inclusive, {zᵢ, zᵢ₊₁, ⋯, zⱼ} — there are j − i + 1 of them. Track the first of these that is chosen as a pivot (one of them must be, eventually, since pivots keep being drawn until each value has been one). Two cases, and only two:

  • If that first-chosen pivot is an interior value zₘ with i < m < j, it splits zᵢ and zⱼ into different partitions. They land on opposite sides and are never compared.
  • If that first-chosen pivot is an endpoint — zᵢ or zⱼ itself — then at that moment the other endpoint is still in the same block and is compared to it. They are compared.

So zᵢ and zⱼ are compared exactly when the first pivot drawn from their block of j − i + 1 values is one of the two endpoints. Because pivots are uniform, every value in the block is equally likely to be the first one chosen, so the two favourable cases out of j − i + 1 give

the heart of the analysis
P(zi,zj compared) = E[Xij] = 2j−i+1

Read it: adjacent ranks (j = i+1) are compared with probability 2/2 = 1 — they must be, no value sits between to separate them — while far-apart ranks are compared rarely, probability ≈ 2/(j-i). Figure 2 draws this.

Step 3 — total the table. Substitute and sum. Group the pairs by their gap g = j - i; there are n - g pairs with gap g, each contributing 2/(g+1):

summing the triangle
E[X] = ∑i<j 2j−i+1 = ∑g=1n−1 (n−g) 2g+1 < 2n ∑g=1n−1 1g+1 < 2nHn

We dropped the -g (it only helps) and recognised the harmonic number Hₙ = ∑ₖ₁ⁿ 1/k. And the one summation from the prerequisites closes it: Hₙ = ln n + γ + O(1/n), with γ ≈ 0.577. Therefore

result
E[X] < 2nHn ≈ 2n ln n ≈ 1.39 n log2n = Θ(n log n)
Why uniform pivots make each block-value equally likely to be first (the one subtle step)

Restrict attention to the j-i+1 values in the block. At each level of recursion the pivot is chosen uniformly from whatever sub-array currently holds these values, and crucially, until one of the block values is chosen as a pivot, all of them travel together — any pivot chosen from outside the block either sends the whole block left or the whole block right, never separating it (because the block is a contiguous interval of values, and partitioning sends everything below the pivot one way and everything above the other). So the first pivot to fall inside the block is selected uniformly at random from the block, by symmetry. Each of the j-i+1 values is equally likely to be that first one; exactly 2 of them are endpoints. Hence 2/(j-i+1). This is the entire reason randomization works: no input can bias which block-value gets picked first.

what we just bought We never assumed the input was random — z₁ < ⋯ < zₙ is any input, sorted only in our heads for bookkeeping. The expectation is over the pivots, which we own. That is the difference between average-case (a hope about inputs) and randomized (a guarantee you carry with you). And notice we never needed the Xᵢⱼ to be independent — they are riddled with dependence — because linearity did not ask.
08 variance

When the mean is not enough

Expectation gives the center; it says nothing about the spread. Two algorithms can share an expected running time and yet one is dependable while the other lurches between instant and catastrophic. To see — and bound — that risk, we need a second number: how far the variable typically strays from its mean.

variance and standard deviation
Var[X] = E[(X−μ)2] = E[X2] − μ2 , σ=Var[X] , μ=E[X]

Variance is the expected squared deviation from the mean; we square so that overshoots and undershoots cannot cancel, and so that large deviations are penalised disproportionately. The standard deviation σ rescales it back to the variable's own units — it is the natural yardstick for “a typical distance from the mean.” The right-hand identity E[X²] - μ² is the one you compute with.

Unlike expectation, variance is not linear — squaring is not. But it has its own near-magic property under independence:

variance of a sum of INDEPENDENT variables
Var[X1+⋯+Xn] = ∑i=1n Var[Xi] (pairwise independence suffices)

Here the independence hypothesis you escaped in §05 returns and must be honoured. For an indicator, Var[𝟙ᵑ] = p(1-p). So for X = heads in n fair flips, Var[X] = n • ¼ = n/4 — the variance grows like n, the standard deviation like √n. That sublinear spread — the deviation √n being vanishingly small next to the mean n/2 — is the first whisper of concentration: big sums of independent pieces huddle tightly around their mean.

09 the three bounds

Markov, Chebyshev, Chernoff–Hoeffding

An expectation is a promise about the average of infinitely many runs; it is silent about the one run you are about to make. A concentration bound (or tail bound) fills that silence: it caps the probability that X lands far from its mean. The three canonical bounds form a ladder — each demands more of you and pays back a sharper cap.

Markov — the crudest, asks almost nothing

If X ≥ 0 and you know only E[X]:

Markov's inequality (X ≥ 0, a > 0)
P(X≥a) ≤ E[X]a

One line proves it: since X ≥ 0, the variable a•𝟙[X ≥ a] ≤ X pointwise (when the indicator is 1 the left side is a ≤ X; when 0 it is 0 ≤ X). Take expectations: a•P(X ≥ a) ≤ E[X]. The only inputs are non-negativity and the mean — which is also its weakness: it knows nothing of spread, so it decays only as 1/a.

Chebyshev — pay a variance, get a square

Feed Markov the variance and the bound sharpens. Apply Markov to the non-negative variable (X - μ)² at level k²:

Chebyshev's inequality
P(|X−μ|≥k) = P((X−μ)2≥k2) ≤ E[(X−μ)2]k2 = Var[X]k2

Now the cap is two-sided and decays as 1/k². Measured in standard deviations — set k = tσ — it reads P(|X - μ| ≥ tσ) ≤ 1/t²: being t deviations out has probability at most 1/t², for any distribution whatsoever. Remarkable for its generality; still only polynomial decay.

Chernoff–Hoeffding — demand independence, get an exponential

When X = X₁ + ⋯ + Xₙ is a sum of independent bounded variables, the tail does not merely shrink — it collapses exponentially. For independent indicators with mean μ = E[X]:

Chernoff bound (sum of independent 0/1 variables)
P(X≥(1+δ)μ) ≤ e−δ2μ/3 (0<δ≤1) , P(X≤(1−δ)μ) ≤ e−δ2μ/2

Its cousin Hoeffding's inequality handles independent variables bounded in any [aᵢ, bᵢ] (not just 0/1): P(|S - E[S]| ≥ t) ≤ 2 exp(−2t² / ∑(bᵢ-aᵢ)²). The mechanism behind both is the moment generating function: apply Markov not to X but to eᵗᵅ, exploit independence to factor the expectation into a product, then optimise over t. Independence is what licenses the factoring — remove it and the exponential is gone.

The MGF trick in four lines — where the exponential comes from

For any t > 0, the event {X ≥ a} equals {eᵗᵅ ≥ eᵗᵀ}, so Markov on the non-negative eᵗᵅ gives P(X ≥ a) ≤ E[eᵗᵅ] / eᵗᵀ. Independence factors the numerator: E[eᵗᵅ] = ∏ᵢ E[eᵗᵅᵢ]. Each factor is bounded for a 0/1 variable, and minimising the product over the free parameter t yields the exponential cap. The single inequality P(X≥a) ≤ minᵗ E[eᵗᵅ]/eᵗᵀ is the whole of the Chernoff method; everything else is algebra to make it pretty.

The ladder — what each bound demands and what it returns

10 worked example B

One tail, capped three ways

Numbers make the ladder vivid. Flip n fair coins; let X be the number of heads, so μ = n/2 and (independence) Var[X] = n/4. We ask the same question of all three bounds: how likely is X ≥ 3n/4 — a full quarter of n above the mean?

Markov. Non-negativity and the mean only: P(X ≥ 3n/4) ≤ E[X] / (3n/4) = (n/2)/(3n/4) = 2/3. A cap of 0.667 — almost worthless, and it never improves with n.

Chebyshev. The deviation is k = 3n/4 - n/2 = n/4. Then P(X ≥ 3n/4) ≤ P(|X-μ| ≥ n/4) ≤ Var/k² = (n/4)/(n/4)² = 4/n. Now it decays — but only as 1/n.

Chernoff. Write 3n/4 = (1+δ)μ with μ = n/2, so 1+δ = 3/2, δ = ½. Then P(X ≥ 3n/4) ≤ e−δ²μ/3 = e−(¼)(n/2)/3 = e−n/24. Exponential collapse.

At n = 10,000 Chebyshev still allows a 1-in-2,500 chance; Chernoff has driven it below 1 in 10¹⁸⁰. That gulf is why randomized algorithms are trusted, not merely expected to work.
the moral of the table All three are correct. They differ only in how much structure you let them exploit. Markov sees a non-negative variable; Chebyshev sees its variance; Chernoff sees that it is a sum of independent pieces — and that last fact, paid for honestly, buys you a guarantee strong enough to bet a data centre on. This is the exact pattern Lecture 08 uses to bound hash-table chains and Lecture 22 to certify skip lists and Bloom filters.
11 where it breaks

Failure modes & common mistakes

Formal elegance blinds. Here is where I have seen this toolkit misfire — each a boundary I urge you to keep bright.

1 · Using independence where linearity needed none — and vice versa

The signature error of the subject, in both directions. People add a hypothesis they do not need — agonising over whether the Xᵢⱼ in quicksort are independent (they are not, and it does not matter; linearity is unconditional). And people drop a hypothesis they do need — writing E[XY] = E[X]E[Y] or Var[X+Y] = Var[X]+Var[Y] for dependent variables, which is simply false. The rule: sums of expectations are always free; products of expectations and sums of variances require independence.

2 · Pointing Markov at a signed variable

Markov requires X ≥ 0. Apply it to something that can go negative — a centred quantity, a profit-or-loss — and you get nonsense (even “probabilities” above 1). When you want a two-sided statement, do what Chebyshev does: feed Markov the non-negative square (X-μ)², never the raw deviation.

3 · Expectation does not pass through a nonlinear function (Jensen)

E[1/X] ≠ 1/E[X], and E[X²] ≠ E[X]² (their gap is the variance). For convex g, Jensen's inequality says E[g(X)] ≥ g(E[X]) — equality only when X is constant. So you cannot “average the inputs and then apply the function.” A worked symptom: the expected depth of a random binary search tree is Θ(log n), but the expected height is also Θ(log n) only because of a tail argument — you cannot get the max from the mean.

4 · Confusing “expected O(n log n)” with “always O(n log n)”

Randomized quicksort's Θ(n log n) is an expectation; an unlucky pivot streak can still cost Θ(n²) on any input. The expectation does not forbid the bad case — it makes it rare, and only a concentration argument (not the mean) promises “rarely slow.” This is the Las Vegas bargain: always correct, running time random. Its opposite, Monte Carlo (fixed running time, occasionally wrong), trades the other way — know which you are holding.

5 · Randomize the algorithm, do not assume a random input

The whole power of §07 is that the input is arbitrary. If instead you assume the input array is uniformly random, you have proved an average-case bound that a sorted file or an adversary instantly violates. Put the randomness in the pivot, where you control it — not in a hope about the world.

6 · Chernoff on dependent variables; the mean of a maximum

Chernoff's exponential requires independence to factor the MGF; apply it to correlated indicators and the bound is unjustified (use Chebyshev, which only needs the variance, or a martingale bound). And E[maxᵢ Xᵢ] ≠ maxᵢ E[Xᵢ]: the expected longest hash chain is not the expected length of one chain. The maximum of many variables is governed by a union bound over tail events — P(some bin ≥ t) ≤ ∑ P(binᵢ ≥ t) — fed by Chernoff, which is exactly Practice Problem 3.

the meta-mistake Quoting a bound without checking its hypotheses. Before you write Markov, Chebyshev, or Chernoff, say aloud what it requires — non-negative? variance known? independent sum? — and confirm you have it. A bound applied outside its hypotheses is not a weak result; it is a wrong one.
12 practice

Problems

1

Kth largest, in expected linear time

randomized quickselect · linearity · expected Θ(n)

Implement findKthLargest(nums, k) returning the k-th largest element of an unsorted array (1 ≤ k ≤ n ≤ 10⁵, values in [−10⁴, 10⁴]) using randomized quickselect: partition around a uniformly random pivot and recurse into only the side containing rank k. Then prove the expected running time is Θ(n), not Θ(n log n) — set up the expected-comparison count E[T(n)] and show a random pivot discards a constant fraction of the array in expectation, so the work forms a geometric series summing to Θ(n). State which deterministic pivot rule degrades to Θ(n²) and name an input that triggers it. Target O(1) auxiliary space (in-place partition).

2

Uniform random sample from a stream

reservoir sampling · indicators · induction · O(1) space

Given the head of a singly-linked list whose length n is unknown and which you may traverse only once, implement getRandom() returning the value of a node chosen uniformly at random, using O(1) extra memory. Use reservoir sampling: keep the current candidate, and at step i replace it with the i-th element with probability 1/i. Prove, by indicators and induction on i, that when the stream ends every element has been selected with probability exactly 1/n. Then generalise to a reservoir of size k (keep k samples without replacement) and prove each element survives with probability k/n.

3

The longest hash chain

balls into bins · Chernoff + union bound · max load

Hash n keys independently and uniformly into n buckets (the idealized model behind Lecture 08's chaining). Let L be the maximum bucket load. (a) Using indicators, show the expected load of a fixed bucket is exactly 1. (b) Prove that with probability at least 1 − 1/n, every bucket has load O(log n / log log n) — bound P(\text{bucket } i ≥ t) with a Chernoff bound, then take a union bound over all n buckets, and solve for the threshold t that makes the total at most 1/n. (c) Conclude the worst-case chain length for hashing-with-chaining is Θ(log n / log log n) w.h.p., not O(1) — and explain why part (a) alone could never have told you this.

4

Boost a noisy estimator: Chebyshev, then median-of-means

Chebyshev · Chernoff boosting · streaming sketches

You hold an unbiased estimator Y for an unknown μ, with E[Y] = μ and Var[Y] = σ²; one evaluation costs one sample. (a) Average m independent copies into Ȳ; using Chebyshev, find the smallest m (in terms of σ², μ, ε) so that P(|Ȳ − μ| ≥ εμ) ≤ 1/3. (b) Take the median of t such independent averages; let G count how many of them are “good” (within εμ). Using a Chernoff bound on G, show the median is good with probability ≥ 1 − δ once t = O(log(1/δ)). (c) State the total sample count mt as a function of σ², μ, ε, δ — the “median-of-means” guarantee behind the streaming sketches of Lecture 23.

13 recap & next

The one thing to remember

Decompose any count into 0/1 indicators, sum their probabilities by linearity — no independence required — to get the expectation; then climb the ladder Markov → Chebyshev → Chernoff to prove the bad case is improbable, paying one extra hypothesis (non-negativity, then variance, then independence) for each sharper rung. Expectation tells you the typical cost; a concentration bound is what lets you trust it on the single run you actually make.
