No. Removing the heaviest edge of a cycle keeps connectivity and never raises total weight.

You hold a connected, weighted graph — cities and the cost of every cable that might join them — and you must wire them all together for the least money. The cheapest connector is a tree: connect everything, waste nothing on a cycle. The number of candidate trees is astronomical, yet a stubbornly local rule — always take the next-cheapest edge that does not close a loop — finds the single global optimum in nearly the time it takes to read the edges. This lecture is about why that greed is licensed, and the two structures that execute it.

A number to set the stakes. Take just 50 cities, fully interconnectable. By Cayley's formula the number of distinct spanning trees on n labelled nodes is nn−2 — for n=50 that is 5048, more than 1081. That is larger than the estimated number of atoms in the observable universe.

You could not enumerate them before the stars burn out. And yet the minimum-cost one among all 1081 is pinned down in O(E log V) — a few thousand operations — by a method so simple a clerk could run it. The whole lecture is the proof that the simple method is right, and never merely lucky.

01 the problem beneath the structure

The connection problem

I begin, as always, by separating the problem from the engine. The problem is older than computing: a set of points must be joined so that every point can reach every other, and joining any two of them carries a cost. Lay telephone lines between towns; etch traces between pads on a circuit board; choose which roads to clear of snow so the country stays connected; cluster data by linking nearest neighbours. In each, the question is the same — which connections do I buy so that the whole is connected for the least total cost?

Strip away the towns and the cables and what remains is a weighted graph G=(V,E,w): a set of vertices V, a set of possible edges E, and a weight w(e) on each edge — its cost. We want a subset of edges that (i) keeps the graph connected and (ii) costs as little as possible. The instant you write that down, one fact falls out: the answer never contains a cycle. A cycle is a redundant loop; delete its heaviest edge and everything is still connected for strictly less money. A minimum connector is therefore acyclic and connected — it is a tree. A tree that touches every vertex is a spanning tree, and the cheapest one is the minimum spanning tree, the MST.

That is the whole specification. What makes it a great problem — the kind that earns a lecture — is the gulf between how many candidates exist and how easily the best is found. The hero number above (1081 trees for 50 nodes) is the size of the search space; the algorithms we build search none of it. They make a sequence of irrevocable local choices and arrive at the global optimum by a guarantee, not a search. The name for that style is greedy, and the MST is the cleanest place in all of algorithms to see exactly when greed is provably correct.

the formal lensHold the contract fixed — "a minimum-weight spanning subgraph" — and notice the contract forces the shape of the answer (a tree) before we choose any algorithm. The cost is a property of the structure; our freedom is only in how we find it.
02 objectives & prerequisites

What you will be able to do

  • State the minimum-spanning-tree problem precisely and explain, in one sentence, why every optimal solution is a tree of exactly |V|−1 edges.
  • Prove the cut property by an exchange argument — the single theorem that makes the greedy choice safe — and state its dual, the cycle property.
  • Execute Kruskal's and Prim's algorithms by hand on a graph, tracing the data structure each one depends on, and recognise that both are corollaries of the one cut property.
  • Implement the union-find (disjoint-set) structure with union by rank and path compression, and prove the O(log n) height bound that rank alone already buys.

  • Diagnose the classic failures: confusing an MST with a shortest-path tree, mishandling ties and disconnected graphs, and breaking the union-find invariants.

03 fix the object before the algorithm

The minimum spanning tree, formalized

Let G=(V,E) be an undirected, connected graph with a real weight w(e) on each edge. A spanning tree T ⊆ E is a set of edges that is

  • spanning — every vertex is incident to at least one edge of T (nothing is left out), and
  • a tree — connected and acyclic.

Three facts about trees pin the object down, and we will lean on all three. First, a tree on |V| vertices has exactly |V|−1 edges — one fewer than the vertices it joins. Second, adding any one non-tree edge to a spanning tree creates exactly one cycle (the new edge plus the unique tree-path between its endpoints). Third, removing any one edge from that cycle restores a spanning tree. These two operations — add an edge to make a cycle, delete an edge of the cycle — are the only moves we will ever make, and together they are the engine of the proof in §04.

the objective
minT spanning tree w(T) = ∑e∈T w(e)

The minimum spanning tree is the spanning tree of least total weight. Note what the objective does not ask for: it says nothing about distances between particular vertices, only about the total. That omission is the source of the most common misconception in §10 — the MST is not the network of shortest routes. For now, here is the concrete graph we will solve twice, by two different algorithms, later in the lecture.

04 the theorem that licenses greed

The cut property

Greedy algorithms are usually wrong. Take the locally cheapest move at every step and you typically paint yourself into a corner — the cheap first bite costs you dearly later. The MST is one of the rare problems where local greed is globally optimal, and the reason is a single structural theorem. Everything else in this lecture is a corollary of it.

First a definition. A cut (S, V∖S) is any partition of the vertices into two non-empty groups. An edge crosses the cut if its two endpoints lie on opposite sides. Picture a line drawn through the graph: the crossing edges are the ones the line severs.

The Cut Property. Let (S, V∖S) be any cut of G. Among all edges crossing the cut, let e be one of minimum weight. Then e belongs to some minimum spanning tree of G. If all crossing weights are distinct, e belongs to every MST.

In words: the cheapest way to bridge any partition is always safe to commit to. You never regret taking a lightest crossing edge.

The proof is an exchange argument — the workhorse of greedy correctness. We assume an optimal solution that disagrees with our greedy choice and edit it, at no extra cost, into one that agrees. Because the edit never raises the weight, the agreeing solution is optimal too.

Proof

Let e=(u,v) be a minimum-weight edge crossing the cut (S,V∖S), with u∈S and v∈V∖S. Take any MST T. If T already contains e, there is nothing to prove. So suppose e∉T.

Add e to T. By the second tree-fact of §03, this creates exactly one cycle C — the edge e together with the unique path in T from u to v. That path starts at u∈S and ends at v∈V∖S, so it must step across the cut at least once. Pick such a crossing edge on it and call it e′. Now e′ also crosses the cut, and e was a minimum-weight crossing edge, so

the exchange inequality
w(e) ≤ w(e′) so w(T′) = w(T) −w(e′) +w(e) ≤ w(T)

Form T′ = T − e′ + e: delete the cycle edge e′, keep the new edge e. Removing an edge from a cycle cannot disconnect the graph, and we still have exactly |V|−1 edges, so T′ is again a spanning tree. By the inequality, w(T′)≤w(T). But T was an MST, so no spanning tree is lighter — hence w(T′)=w(T), and T′ is itself an MST, one that contains e. Therefore e lies in some MST. If the weights are distinct the inequality is strict unless e′=e, which forces e into every MST. □

The dual: the cycle property

Turn the argument inside out and you get its mirror, which we will need in Practice 4. The cycle property: for any cycle in G, a maximum-weight edge of that cycle belongs to no MST (to no MST at all, if its weight is the unique maximum). The proof is the same exchange run backwards: if an MST contained the heaviest edge f of a cycle, delete f — the tree splits into two parts — and the cycle supplies a lighter edge re-joining them, giving a strictly cheaper spanning tree, a contradiction. The cut property tells you what to take; the cycle property tells you what to drop. Kruskal is, quite literally, both at once.

One theorem — the lightest edge across any cut is safe — and both algorithms are merely two orders in which to apply it.
05 greed by edge weight

Kruskal's algorithm

Kruskal (1956) reads the cut property in its bluntest form: consider the edges from cheapest to dearest, and take each one unless it would close a cycle. Build a forest that starts as |V| isolated vertices and merges as edges are accepted, stopping when a single tree spans everything.

def kruskal(V, E, w):              # E = list of edges (u, v)
    T = []                            # the MST edges we accept
    dsu = DSU(V)                     # every vertex its own component
    for (u, v) in sorted(E, key=w):    # lightest edge first
        if dsu.find(u) != dsu.find(v):  # endpoints in different trees?
            T.append((u, v))           # safe by the cut property
            dsu.union(u, v)           # merge the two components
            if len(T) == len(V) - 1:    # spanning tree complete
                break
    return T

Why it is correct

The cut property does all the work, but you must point it at the right cut. Suppose Kruskal accepts the edge e=(u,v). At that instant, let S be the set of vertices in u's current tree. Two observations:

  • e crosses the cut (S, V∖S), because v is in a different tree, hence not in S.
  • e is a minimum-weight edge crossing that cut. Why: any crossing edge has one endpoint in S and one outside, so it has not yet been accepted (an accepted crossing edge would have merged the two sides into one tree). Edges are examined in increasing weight, and we are examining e now, so every still-unaccepted edge — including every other crossing edge — is at least as heavy as e.

By the cut property, e is safe: there is an MST extending the forest we have built so far. By induction over the accepted edges, the final forest — a single spanning tree — is an MST. The rejection step is the cycle property in disguise: an edge skipped because its endpoints already share a tree is a maximum-weight edge of the cycle it would have formed (all the others were lighter and accepted earlier), so it belongs to no MST. Kruskal accepts exactly the cut-property edges and drops exactly the cycle-property edges.

the bottleneckCorrectness is free; the cost lives entirely in one question, asked E times: are u and v already in the same tree, and if not, merge them. Answer it naively — relabel a component, or run a search per edge — and Kruskal degrades toward O(VE). The whole algorithm hinges on a structure that does find and union in almost no time. That structure is next.
06 the engine under kruskal

Union-Find: the disjoint-set structure

The abstract type Kruskal demands is a disjoint-set structure, also called union-find or DSU. It maintains a partition of {0,…,n−1} into disjoint sets and supports three operations:

  • make_set(x) — start x alone in its own set;
  • find(x) — return a canonical representative of x's set, so that find(a)==find(b) iff a and b are in the same set;
  • union(x, y) — merge the two sets containing x and y.

The representation is deceptively plain: a forest of up-trees, stored as a single array parent[]. Each element points at its parent; the root of each tree — the element that points at itself — is the representative. find walks parent pointers to the root; union hangs one root beneath the other. Nothing more.

Left naive, this is a trap. Union in the wrong direction and you can grow a single chain of length n; then find walks the whole chain at O(n), and Kruskal is quadratic again. Two heuristics — cheap, local, and famous — rescue it.

Heuristic 1 — union by rank

Keep an integer rank[x], an upper bound on the height of x's subtree. When you union two roots, hang the lower-rank root beneath the higher-rank one, so the tree never gets taller than it must. Only when the two ranks are equal does the surviving root's rank tick up by one.

Heuristic 2 — path compression

During find, after you have located the root, point every node you walked past directly at the root. The next find on any of them is a single hop. The walk you already paid for doubles as a flattening of the path — this is the amortization, the same “pay-it-forward” bookkeeping of Lecture 02, now cashed out on pointers.

class DSU:
    def __init__(self, n):
        self.parent = list(range(n))   # parent[x] = x  -> x is its own root
        self.rank   = [0] * n          # upper bound on subtree height

    def find(self, x):                 # with PATH COMPRESSION
        if self.parent[x] != x:
            self.parent[x] = self.find(self.parent[x])  # re-point straight to root
        return self.parent[x]

    def union(self, x, y):              # with UNION BY RANK
        rx, ry = self.find(x), self.find(y)
        if rx == ry: return False      # already joined -> would close a cycle
        if self.rank[rx] < self.rank[ry]:
            rx, ry = ry, rx               # rx is the taller root
        self.parent[ry] = rx              # hang shorter under taller
        if self.rank[rx] == self.rank[ry]:
            self.rank[rx] += 1            # equal heights -> height grows by one
        return True

The bound that union by rank alone buys

Path compression makes the structure spectacular, but it is subtle to analyse. Rank alone is enough for a clean, complete proof of a strong bound — and it is worth doing, because it explains why the trees stay shallow.

Claim. Under union by rank, a root of rank r has at least 2r elements in its tree.

Proof by induction on the number of unions. A fresh root has rank 0 and one element; 20=1. A root's rank rises to r+1 only when two rank-r roots merge; by the hypothesis each carried at least 2r elements, so the union carries at least 2r+2r=2r+1. When ranks differ, the larger root's rank is unchanged while its element count only grows, so the bound holds. □

Since a tree has at most n elements, 2r≤n, so every rank — and therefore every tree height, and therefore every find — is at most log₂n:

union by rank alone
2rank(r) ≤ size(r) ≤n ⟹ height≤rank(r) ≤ log2n

The headline: near-constant with both

Add path compression and the bound collapses further. Tarjan's analysis (1975), built on the potential method of Lecture 02, proves that a sequence of m operations on n elements costs

rank + path compression (amortized)
O(m⋅α(n)) where α(n)≤4 for every conceivable n

α(n) is the inverse Ackermann function. It grows so unfathomably slowly that it does not reach 5 until n exceeds a tower of powers of two taller than the number of atoms in the universe written out as exponents. For any input that will ever exist, α(n)≤4 — a constant in all but the strictest theory. If you want a bound you can prove yourself in an afternoon, the intermediate O(m·log*n) (iterated logarithm) is reachable and already absurdly small (log*n≤5 for n≤265536).

use the rootsThe two most common DSU bugs both come from forgetting that operations act on representatives: always union(find(x), find(y)), never parent[y]=x on raw elements; and after compression, rank is only an upper bound on height, never the exact height — do not try to recompute it.
07 greed by vertex

Prim's algorithm

Kruskal grows many little trees that eventually fuse. Prim (1957; Jarník, 1930) grows one tree from a seed. Keep a set S of vertices already in the tree; at every step, take the lightest edge crossing the cut (S, V∖S) and absorb the new vertex it reaches. That is the cut property applied to one specific, growing cut — so correctness is immediate: at each step the added edge is a minimum crossing edge of (S, V∖S), hence safe, hence the tree stays a subset of some MST.

The only engineering question is how to find that lightest crossing edge fast. Maintain, for every vertex v not yet in the tree, the weight key[v] of the cheapest edge linking v to S — and store the vertices in a min-priority queue keyed by key. Extract the minimum to absorb a vertex; then relax its neighbours, lowering their keys if the new vertex offers a cheaper link.

def prim(V, adj, w, s):              # adj[u] = list of (v, weight)
    key    = {v: float('inf') for v in V}
    parent = {v: None for v in V}
    key[s] = 0
    Q = MinPQ(V, key)                  # priority queue over vertices
    while Q:
        u = Q.extract_min()             # nearest non-tree vertex joins S
        for (v, weight) in adj[u]:
            if v in Q and weight < key[v]:  # a cheaper edge into the tree
                key[v]    = weight
                parent[v] = u
                Q.decrease_key(v, weight)
    return {(v, parent[v]) for v in V if parent[v] is not None}

Look closely and you will recognise the skeleton: a priority queue, an extract-min, and a relaxation of neighbours. It is exactly the shape of Dijkstra's shortest-path algorithm in the next lecture — the only difference is the key. Prim keys a vertex by the weight of the single edge that would attach it, key[v]=w(u,v); Dijkstra keys it by the whole accumulated distance, dist[u]+w(u,v). Same machine, two greedily-different definitions of “nearest.”

lazy vs. eagerTwo implementations exist. Eager Prim keeps one entry per vertex and uses decrease_key (the code above). Lazy Prim skips decrease_key, instead pushing every edge it sees and discarding stale ones when popped (an edge whose endpoints are both already in S). Lazy is simpler to code with a plain binary heap; forget the staleness check and it returns nonsense.

Why Kruskal's sort bound equals Prim's: E ≤ V², so log E ≤ 2 log V, hence O(E log E) = O(E log V). For a dense graph (E ≈ V²) the no-heap O(V²) Prim beats both.

Two readings worth carrying away. First, Kruskal's cost is dominated by the sort, not the union-find — the E find/union calls together cost only O(E·α(V)), dwarfed by the O(E log V) to order the edges. Hand Kruskal pre-sorted edges (or integer weights it can radix-sort) and it runs in near-linear O(E·α(V)). Second, the choice between Kruskal and Prim is a choice about your input: a list of edges with no adjacency structure favours Kruskal; a dense graph already in adjacency form favours array-Prim's tight O(V²).

10 where it breaks

Failure modes & common mistakes

The MST is forgiving in theory and treacherous in practice. Every trap below is something the formal lens flags early — a confusion of objectives, or a violated invariant.

1. An MST is not a shortest-path tree

The single most expensive misconception. The objective minimizes total edge weight, and says nothing about the distance between any particular pair. In Figure 4 the MST route from A to B is A–C–D–B at cost 10, while the direct edge A–B costs 6 — the MST is happy to make two vertices far apart if it saves on the global total. If you need shortest routes from a source, you want Dijkstra (Lecture 13), a different greedy on a different key. Never use an MST to answer a distance query.

2. Forgetting union-find — quadratic Kruskal

Testing “same component?” by a BFS/DFS per edge, or by scanning a component-label array and relabelling on every merge, makes Kruskal O(VE) or worse. The entire competitiveness of Kruskal rests on the α(V) find/union. If you have written Kruskal and it is slow, this is almost always why.

3. The two heuristics, half-applied

Union by rank or path compression alone already gives O(log n) per operation; you need both for the α(n) magic. Two subtle bugs live here: (a) bumping rank on every union instead of only when the two ranks are equal — this inflates ranks and breaks the size bound; (b) unioning raw elements (parent[y]=x) instead of their roots (parent[find(y)]=find(x)) — this silently corrupts the partition.

4. Ties — the MST is not unique

When edge weights repeat, there can be many minimum spanning trees, and the cut property only promises a lightest crossing edge is in some MST, not every one. Kruskal and Prim still each return a correct MST — but two correct implementations may return different trees, and any test that hard-codes one expected edge set is wrong. (This is exactly why Practice 4 distinguishes critical from pseudo-critical edges.)

5. Negative weights are fine; disconnection is not

Unlike shortest paths, the MST is perfectly happy with negative weights — the cut and cycle arguments never look at the sign of a weight, only at comparisons. What does break the problem is a disconnected graph: there is no spanning tree at all. Kruskal then yields a minimum spanning forest (it simply runs out of safe edges with fewer than |V|−1 accepted, leaving one tree per component); Prim spans only the component of its seed. Decide deliberately which you want, and detect disconnection (Practice 3 turns on exactly this count).

6. The directed trap

MST is an undirected notion. The directed analogue — a minimum-weight arborescence rooted at a vertex — is a genuinely different and harder problem; the cut property fails for it, and the greedy edge-picking here gives wrong answers. It needs the Chu–Liu/Edmonds algorithm, not Kruskal. If your edges have direction, stop and reach for a different tool.

the litmus testBefore you run any MST code, ask two questions the objective settles instantly: Is my graph undirected and connected? and Do I actually want minimum total weight — or minimum distances? The first guards the problem's preconditions; the second guards against the shortest-path confusion that wrecks more MST uses than every implementation bug combined.

Problems

1

Min Cost to Connect All Points

MST from scratch · dense graph · LeetCode 1584

You are given points, an array of n distinct points [xi, yi] on the plane. The cost of connecting two points is their Manhattan distance |xi−xj| + |yi−yj|. Return the minimum total cost to connect all points so that there is exactly one simple path between any two. Constraints: 1 ≤ n ≤ 1000. Example: points=[[0,0],[2,2],[3,10],[5,2],[7,0]] → 20. The graph is complete (every pair is an edge), so choose your algorithm with the E≈V² density in mind and justify the complexity you achieve.

2

Redundant Connection

pure union-find · the cycle test · LeetCode 684

A tree on n nodes labelled 1…n had exactly one extra edge added, creating a single cycle. You are given edges (length n, 1-indexed) in the order added. Return the one edge that can be removed so the result is again a tree; if several qualify, return the one that appears last in the input. Example: edges=[[1,2],[1,3],[2,3]] → [2,3]. This is the reject step of Kruskal in isolation: which property of §04 tells you the first edge whose endpoints already share a component is the answer — and why does processing in input order automatically satisfy the “last” tie-break? Target O(n·α(n)).

3

Number of Operations to Make Network Connected

components · the |V|−1 floor · LeetCode 1319

There are n computers 0…n−1 joined by connections[i]=[a,b] (each an existing cable). You may unplug any cable and replug it between any two computers. Return the minimum number of such moves to make the whole network connected, or −1 if it is impossible. Examples: n=4, connections=[[0,1],[0,2],[1,2]] → 1; n=6, connections=[[0,1],[0,2],[0,3],[1,2],[1,3]] → 2. Two sub-questions decide it: when is it impossible (count edges against the spanning-tree floor), and once it is possible, why is the answer exactly (\#components − 1) regardless of which spare cable you move? Use union-find to count components.

4

Find Critical and Pseudo-Critical Edges in an MST

cut & cycle properties, mastered · capstone · LeetCode 1489

Given an undirected weighted graph with n vertices and edges[i]=[ui, vi, wi], classify every edge by index. An edge is critical if it appears in every MST; it is pseudo-critical if it appears in some but not all MSTs. Return [critical, pseudo_critical] as two lists of indices. Constraints: 2 ≤ n ≤ 100, up to 200 edges. The whole problem is the §04 dichotomy made operational: characterise “critical” by what happens to the MST weight when you forbid an edge, and “pseudo-critical” by what happens when you force one in — using a Kruskal you can run with an edge excluded or pre-included. State both tests precisely and watch the original indices survive the sort.

12 recap & next

The one thing to remember

The lightest edge crossing any cut is always safe — that one theorem, the cut property, makes greed optimal, and both MST algorithms are just two orders in which to apply it. Kruskal sweeps edges by weight and leans on union-find to test “same component?” in α(n)-amortized time; Prim grows one tree and leans on a priority queue to find the cheapest frontier edge. Union by rank keeps the disjoint-set trees shallow (provably O(log n)), and path compression flattens them the moment you touch them — together, near-constant. And never forget the boundary: an MST minimizes the total, not any distance between two vertices.

Prim already hinted at it: a priority queue, an extract-min, a relaxation of neighbours. Keep that exact skeleton and change only the key — from “weight of the attaching edge” to “accumulated distance from a source” — and Prim becomes Dijkstra. Lecture 13 follows that thread into the shortest-path world, where greed survives only while weights stay non-negative, and where negative edges force a wholly different, relaxation-until-stable idea (Bellman–Ford). The MST taught you when greed is provably right; shortest paths will teach you exactly where it stops.
