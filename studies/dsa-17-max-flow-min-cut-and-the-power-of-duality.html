It represents the option to cancel flow already sent on the corresponding forward edge.

Picture a pipeline network — a source, a sink, and pipes of fixed capacity between them. Two questions sound opposite. How much can you push from source to sink at once? And how little would you have to sever — which pipes to cut, paying their capacities — to stop all flow entirely? The astonishing fact, and the whole of this study, is that these are the same number. The maximum you can push equals the cheapest wall you can build. That single equality — max-flow = min-cut — is one of the most useful theorems in all of computing.

The number that frames the study. The problem came from a secret 1955 RAND study by Ted Harris and Frank Ross of the Soviet rail network — the “flow” was rail tonnage, and the “min cut” was the cheapest set of links to bomb to sever it — and Ford and Fulkerson, who had the problem from Harris, proved max-flow = min-cut in 1956. For sixty-six years the best algorithms crept forward: O(VE²), then O(V²E), then a long plateau. Then in 2022, Chen, Kyng, Liu, Peng, Probst Gutenberg and Sachdeva broke it open with an algorithm running in m1+o(1) time — almost-linear in the number of edges, essentially as fast as reading the graph.

The same theorem that once chose where to cut a railway now finds maximum matchings, segments images, schedules airlines, and selects projects. We build it from the source edge outward — and end at that 2022 frontier.

01 the problem beneath the structure

Two questions, one number

I begin, as always, by separating the problem from the engine. The problem is a directed graph with a distinguished source s and sink t, where every edge carries a capacity — an upper bound on the rate of stuff that may travel along it. Water in pipes, packets on links, trucks on roads, tokens through a refinery. We ask for the maximum steady rate from s to t that respects every capacity and never lets material pile up at an intermediate node.

Now hold that problem next to its shadow. A cut is a way of splitting the vertices into the source’s side and the sink’s side; its cost is the total capacity of the edges pointing from the source side to the sink side — the price of severing those links so that nothing can cross. Minimizing a flow’s throughput and minimizing the cost of a wall feel like different exercises in different units. They are not. The maximum flow value equals the minimum cut cost, exactly, on every network. This is the phenomenon a mathematician calls duality: two optimization problems, one a maximization and one a minimization, locked together so that the optimum of each certifies the optimum of the other.

Why does a working programmer care? Because duality is the most reliable engine of insight we have. The flow tells you how to do the thing; the cut tells you why you can do no better — it is a proof, checkable in a glance, that your answer is optimal. And because dozens of problems that look nothing like plumbing — assigning workers to jobs, choosing which projects to fund, separating an image’s foreground from its background — turn out to be flows in disguise, the same engine solves all of them at once.

the formal lensFix the contract — “a maximum s–t flow” — and a second object falls out of it for free: a minimum cut of equal value, which witnesses optimality. Wherever you find a clean min–max duality, you have found a problem you can both solve and certify.
02 objectives & prerequisites

What you will be able to do

  • Define a flow network, a feasible flow (capacity + conservation), and the value of a flow, and explain why the naive greedy “saturate any path” can get stuck below optimum.
  • Construct the residual graph and find augmenting paths, understanding the backward edge as the mechanism that lets the algorithm undo an earlier commitment.
  • Run Ford–Fulkerson and its BFS refinement Edmonds–Karp by hand, and quote why the latter’s O(VE²) bound is independent of the capacities.
  • State and prove the max-flow min-cut theorem via the three-way equivalence (max flow ⇔ no augmenting path ⇔ a saturated cut), and read a minimum cut off the final residual graph.
  • Model bipartite matching, vertex/edge-disjoint paths, and project-selection / partition problems as flows or cuts, invoking the integrality theorem to know the answer is whole.
  • Diagnose the failure modes: non-termination on irrational capacities, exponential blow-up of plain Ford–Fulkerson, forgotten back edges, and antiparallel-edge bookkeeping — and place today’s tools against the 2022 almost-linear frontier.

Prerequisites

Three ideas carry the whole study. Augmenting-path search is breadth-first search wearing a hat; the duality argument leans on the same “reachable set” idea BFS computes.

graph traversal (BFS) augmenting-path / greedy shortest paths

If you can run a BFS to find a shortest path in an unweighted graph, recall that “greedy” means committing to a locally best move, and remember that shortest-path algorithms relax edges toward an optimum, you are ready. We are about to give a network a conscience — the ability to take back a bad decision.

03 fix the object before the algorithm

The flow network, formalized

A flow network is a directed graph G=(V,E) with two distinguished vertices, the source s and the sink t, and a non-negative capacity c(u,v)≥0 on every edge (u,v)∈E (we take c(u,v)=0 when there is no edge). A flow is a function f(u,v) assigning to each edge a real value obeying two laws:

law 1 — the capacity constraint
0≤f(u,v)≤c(u,v) for every edge (u,v)∈E

You may not send more along a pipe than it holds, and flow runs in the edge’s direction.

law 2 — conservation (flow in = flow out)
∑u f(u,x) = ∑w f(x,w) for every x∈V∖{s,t}

Every intermediate node is a perfect junction: nothing is created, nothing is stored, nothing leaks. Only s and t are exempt — the source manufactures, the sink consumes. The value of the flow is the net rate leaving the source (equivalently, by conservation, the net rate arriving at the sink):

the objective — value of a flow
|f| = ∑vf(s,v) − ∑vf(v,s) ⟶ maxf|f|

That is the entire specification: maximize |f| over all functions f obeying the two laws. Here is the concrete network we will solve in full, by Edmonds–Karp, in §08. Read each edge as capacity.

04 the idea that makes it work

Why greedy fails — and the residual graph that rescues it

The obvious algorithm: find any path from s to t whose edges all have spare capacity, push as much as the path’s tightest edge allows, repeat until no such path remains. This is greedy, and on the right network it fails — it can wedge itself below the true maximum, having committed flow to an edge it now wishes it had left empty. Plain greed cannot revise; once it pushes a unit down a pipe, that unit is stranded.

The repair is one of the most elegant moves in algorithms. We let the algorithm cancel flow it regrets by introducing, for every edge that currently carries flow, a fictitious backward edge down which a later path may shove flow in reverse. Pushing a unit backward along (u,v) is bookkeeping for “reroute a unit that was going u→v so it goes somewhere better instead.” Collect all the spare forward capacity and all the cancellable backward capacity into one structure:

The residual graph Gf. Given a flow f, every original edge (u,v) contributes up to two residual edges:

• a forward residual edge u→v of capacity c(u,v)−f(u,v) — the unused room;

• a backward residual edge v→u of capacity f(u,v) — the flow you could take back.

An augmenting path is any path from s to t in Gf. Its bottleneck is the smallest residual capacity along it; augmenting means adding that bottleneck to every forward edge and subtracting it from every backward edge on the path.

The backward edge is the entire trick, so let us watch it earn its keep on the smallest network where greed goes wrong. Capacities are large on the outside, and a single fat diagonal u→v tempts the first path into a commitment it must later partly undo.

Trace the right panel once and the mechanism is yours forever. The path s→v→u→t sends 10 new units out of s into v; at v it does not add to v→t (already full) but instead removes 10 from the incoming u→v; those 10 units, no longer needed at v, exit u through the idle u→t. Conservation holds at every node throughout. The backward edge did not move new material — it reassigned material already in transit. That is how an algorithm built from purely local pushes achieves a global optimum: not by never erring, but by always being able to take an error back.

Greed without memory paints itself into a corner. The residual graph gives the algorithm a conscience — the power to undo.
05 the augmenting-path method

Ford–Fulkerson and Edmonds–Karp

The whole algorithm is now one sentence: while the residual graph has an augmenting path, augment along it. This is the Ford–Fulkerson method — a method, not yet an algorithm, because it does not say which augmenting path to take. The choice turns out to govern everything about the running time.

def ford_fulkerson(C, s, t):       # C[u][v] = capacity (0 if no edge)
    F = defaultdict(lambda: defaultdict(int))   # flow on each edge, starts 0
    while True:
        path, bottleneck = find_aug_path(C, F, s, t)  # a path in the residual graph
        if path is None:
            break                          # no augmenting path -> f is maximum
        for (u, v) in path:               # push the bottleneck along the path
            if C[u][v] - F[u][v] > 0:    # forward residual edge
                F[u][v] += bottleneck
            else:                       # backward residual edge: cancel flow
                F[v][u] -= bottleneck
    return sum(F[s][v] for v in C[s])  # value = net flow out of s

Termination, and the trap of arbitrary paths

With integer capacities each augmentation raises |f| by an integer ≥1, so the method terminates after at most |f*| rounds, where |f*| is the maximum-flow value. Each round costs O(E) to find a path, giving O(E·|f*|). That bound hides a menace: |f*| can be enormous, and if you choose augmenting paths carelessly the round count really can scale with the capacities rather than the graph size (the textbook trap: two fat edges joined by a capacity-1 bridge, where unlucky pathing alternates across the bridge and augments by a single unit thousands of times). On irrational capacities, arbitrary Ford–Fulkerson can fail to terminate at all — see §11.

Edmonds–Karp: always take the shortest augmenting path

The fix is disarmingly simple and is why you already learned BFS. Edmonds–Karp picks, every round, the augmenting path with the fewest edges — exactly what a breadth-first search from s in the residual graph returns. This single rule severs the running time from the capacities entirely.

def find_aug_path(C, F, s, t):        # BFS in the residual graph  => Edmonds-Karp
    parent = {s: None}
    q = deque([s])
    while q:
        u = q.popleft()
        for v in C:                       # every vertex v...
            resid = C[u][v] - F[u][v] + F[v][u]   # forward room + cancellable flow
            if resid > 0 and v not in parent:
                parent[v] = u
                if v == t:
                    return trace_and_bottleneck(parent, C, F, s, t)
                q.append(v)
    return None, 0

Why Edmonds–Karp is O(VE²). Two facts, both about BFS distances in the residual graph. Let δ(v) be the number of edges on the shortest residual path from s to v.

(1) Distances never decrease. Augmenting along a shortest path can only add backward edges that point “toward” s, so no δ(v) ever shrinks across a round.

(2) Each edge is the bottleneck O(V) times. When edge (u,v) is saturated (the bottleneck), it vanishes from the residual graph; it can only reappear when flow is pushed back along (v,u), and a careful look shows δ(u) has grown by ≥2 in between. Since δ ranges over 0…V, this happens O(V) times per edge.

So there are O(VE) augmentations, each a BFS of cost O(E): total O(VE²) — polynomial in the graph, with no dependence on the capacities.

Two refinements you should know by name. Dinic’s algorithm (1970) groups the BFS into a layered “level graph” and pushes a whole blocking flow per phase, reaching O(V²E) in general and a remarkable O(E√V) on the unit-capacity networks that matching produces (every vertex has a single entering or a single leaving unit edge). That bound is exactly the Hopcroft–Karp matching algorithm in disguise, which we meet in §09. Both are still augmenting-path algorithms; both still rest entirely on the residual graph of §04.

06 the shadow problem

Cuts and weak duality

Now the other half of the story. An s–t cut (S,T) partitions the vertices so that s∈S and t∈T. Its capacity is the total capacity of edges pointing from S into T — the forward-crossing edges only:

capacity of a cut
c(S,T) = ∑u∈S ∑v∈T c(u,v)

Edges running backward, from T to S, cost nothing in the cut — the cut prices only what crosses in the source-to-sink direction. Here is the lemma that ties flows to cuts, and it is just conservation telescoped. For any flow f and any cut (S,T), the net flow crossing the cut equals the value of the flow:

flow across any cut = value of the flow
|f| = f(S,T) = ∑u∈S,v∈T f(u,v) − ∑u∈S,v∈T f(v,u)

The reasoning: start from |f| as the net outflow of s, then add the conservation equation (net outflow =0) for every other vertex in S. Every edge with both endpoints in S contributes +f at its tail and −f at its head and cancels; what survives is precisely the flow on edges crossing the cut, forward minus backward. Now bound it. Forward terms are at most their capacities; backward terms are at least zero. Therefore:

weak duality — every flow is bounded by every cut
|f| = f(S,T) ≤ ∑u∈S,v∈T c(u,v) = c(S,T)

This already gives half the theorem, and it is the more useful half in practice. Every flow value is ≤ every cut capacity. So if you ever exhibit a flow and a cut of equal value, you have simultaneously proved the flow is maximum (no flow can beat that cut) and the cut is minimum (no cut can undercut that flow). The cut is a one-line certificate of optimality. The deep question — answered next — is whether such a matching pair always exists.

a free lower bound on cutsWeak duality runs both ways as a sanity check: produce any flow, and you have a guaranteed lower bound on the minimum cut; produce any cut, and you have an upper bound on the maximum flow. Squeeze them together and you are done.
07 the theorem at the centre

The max-flow min-cut theorem

Weak duality says max flow ≤ min cut. The theorem of Ford and Fulkerson (1956) says they are equal, and it does so by welding three statements into one.

Max-Flow Min-Cut Theorem. For a flow f in a network, the following are equivalent:

  (1) f is a maximum flow.

  (2) The residual graph Gf contains no augmenting path (no s–t path).

  (3) |f| = c(S,T) for some cut (S,T).

Consequently the maximum flow value equals the minimum cut capacity, and Edmonds–Karp — which halts exactly when (2) holds — returns a maximum flow.

Proof — a tidy cycle (1)⇒(2)⇒(3)⇒(1)

(1)⇒(2). Contrapositive. If Gf had an augmenting path, augmenting along it would strictly raise |f| (the bottleneck is positive), so f was not maximum. Hence a maximum flow admits no augmenting path.

(2)⇒(3). This is the beautiful step — the cut is built from the residual graph itself. Suppose Gf has no s–t path. Let S be the set of vertices reachable from s in Gf, and T=V∖S. Then s∈S by definition and t∈T because t is unreachable — so (S,T) is a genuine cut. Examine its crossing edges:

  • For an original edge (u,v) with u∈S, v∈T: it must be saturated, f(u,v)=c(u,v). Were there slack, the forward residual edge u→v would exist and v would be reachable — contradicting v∈T.
  • For an original edge (v,u) with u∈S, v∈T (i.e. running from T back into S): it must carry zero flow, f(v,u)=0. Any positive flow would create the backward residual edge u→v, again making v reachable.

Substitute into the cut lemma of §06: the forward crossing flow equals capacity and the backward crossing flow is zero, so |f| = f(S,T) = c(S,T) − 0 = c(S,T). The flow exactly saturates this cut.

(3)⇒(1). Immediate from weak duality. If |f|=c(S,T) for some cut, then for any other flow f′ we have |f′| ≤ c(S,T) = |f|, so f is maximum. □

Read the cycle once more and notice what it buys. The implication (2)⇒(3) does not merely assert a min cut exists — it builds one, for free, as the residual-reachable set of s. After Edmonds–Karp stops, one final BFS from s in Gf hands you the minimum cut. The algorithm that finds the flow also prints its own proof of optimality.

Corollary — the integrality theorem (why matching will work)

If every capacity is an integer, then the Ford–Fulkerson method produces a maximum flow in which every edge carries integer flow. The induction is one line: the zero flow is integral; each augmentation adds or subtracts an integer bottleneck (the min of integer residual capacities), preserving integrality. So there is always an integral maximum flow. This is exactly what makes the matching reduction of §09 sound — with unit capacities, an integral flow is forced to be 0/1 on every edge, i.e. a set of chosen edges, i.e. a matching. The same fact underlies the half-dozen modeling tricks in this study: solve the relaxed flow, and integrality hands you a combinatorial object back, with no rounding.

The certificate is now visible to the eye. The flow is 17 (count the saturated middle edges). No flow can exceed 17 (the cut caps it at 17). The two numbers coincide, so both are optimal — and we needed no exhaustive search of flows or cuts to know it. That is duality doing the work of a proof.

09 everything is secretly a flow

Modeling: matching and partition problems as flows

Flow earns its central place not because pipelines are common but because so many problems that mention no pipe at all are flows wearing a costume. The craft is reduction: build a network whose maximum flow (or minimum cut) is the answer you want. Three patterns cover an enormous range.

Pattern 1 — bipartite matching (a flow)

You have a set L of workers and a set R of jobs, with an edge wherever a worker can do a job. A matching is a set of worker–job pairs using each worker and each job at most once; you want the largest. Build a network: a super-source s with a capacity-1 edge to every worker, the original worker→job edges (capacity 1), and a capacity-1 edge from every job to a super-sink t.

Maximum integral flow = maximum matching. And the min-cut shadow is itself a famous theorem: in a bipartite graph the minimum cut in this network corresponds to a minimum vertex cover, giving König’s theorem — maximum matching = minimum vertex cover. Duality is not an analogy here; König’s theorem is max-flow min-cut, specialized. Practice problems 1 and 2 are these two faces.

Pattern 2 — disjoint paths (a cut)

How many edge-disjoint routes run from s to t? Give every edge capacity 1; the maximum flow is exactly the number of edge-disjoint paths, and by duality it equals the minimum number of edges whose removal disconnects s from t. That is Menger’s theorem, the granddaddy of network reliability. For vertex-disjoint paths, apply the standard vertex-splitting gadget: replace each vertex v by vin→vout with capacity 1, forcing at most one path through it. Practice problem 3 is this gadget on a grid.

Pattern 3 — partition / project selection (a weighted cut)

When the prize is a partition — split things into “keep” and “drop” to optimize a weighted objective — the minimum cut is your tool directly. The archetype is project selection (maximum-weight closure): each project earns a profit but requires paying for certain shared resources; choosing a project obliges you to choose its resources. Model it: s→project with capacity = profit, resource→t with capacity = cost, project→resource with capacity ∞ (so no finite cut ever severs a project from a resource it needs). The infinite edges force every finite cut to respect the “closure” rule, and

project selection via min cut
maxvalid subset profit = (total profit) − min‑cut

The same min-cut partition is the engine behind image segmentation (foreground vs. background), the densest-subgraph problem, and the “baseball elimination” classic. Practice problem 4 is project selection in full. The lesson generalizes: a maximization that decomposes into independent route choices is a max-flow; a clean two-way partition with a weighted objective is a min-cut.

the reduction checklistTo model a problem as flow, answer four questions: What is one unit of flow? (a matched pair, a path, a chosen project) What are the capacities? (the “at most once / at most this much” limits) Where is the source and sink? Does integrality give me back a combinatorial object? If all four have clean answers, the flow toolbox — and a free optimality certificate — is yours.

For decades, max-flow was a showcase of combinatorial algorithms — augmenting paths, blocking flows, scaling — and progress stalled around O(VE). The breakthrough came from a different continent of mathematics. Beginning with Mądry’s electrical-flow methods (2013) and the interior-point revolution of Lee–Sidford and others, researchers reframed max-flow as a continuous optimization — an objective minimized by gliding through the interior of the polytope of feasible flows, taking Newton-like steps. Each step is itself a graph problem (a min-ratio cycle, or an undirected min-cost flow) solved approximately by a dynamic data structure that maintains low-stretch trees and sparsifiers on a slowly changing graph.

What is worth carrying from the frontier is not the machinery but the shape of the story. A problem can look solved for forty years — everyone knows the augmenting-path algorithms, the bounds feel canonical — and still be wide open at the asymptotic edge. The 2022 result did not find a faster augmenting path; it changed what kind of object a flow algorithm is, trading discrete pushes for continuous descent. The duality theorem you proved in §07 survives untouched underneath all of it — it is the fixed star the new methods still navigate by.

11 where it breaks

Failure modes & common mistakes

Flow is forgiving in theory and treacherous in code. Every trap below is something the formal lens flags: a violated invariant, or a choice the “method” left unspecified.

1. Arbitrary Ford–Fulkerson can be exponential — or never halt

The classic teaching graph: two paths of capacity 109 joined by a capacity-1 bridge. Choose augmenting paths that cross the bridge each round and you augment by one unit two billion times — correct, but useless. Worse, on irrational capacities arbitrary path choice can converge to a value below the maximum and never terminate (Ford and Fulkerson’s own example; Zwick’s 6-vertex graph is the cleanest). The cure is to specify the path rule: Edmonds–Karp’s shortest-path choice makes it O(VE²), capacity-blind, and always-terminating. Never ship the bare “method”; ship a path rule.

2. Forgetting the backward edge

The single most common implementation bug: pushing only along forward residual edges and omitting the cancellation edge. The result is the broken greedy of §04 — it returns a maximal flow but not the maximum one, undercounting on exactly the networks where rerouting matters. Your residual capacity must always read c(u,v)−f(u,v)+f(v,u); if your code has no term that lets a later path subtract earlier flow, it is wrong.

3. Antiparallel edges and the residual ambiguity

If the graph contains both (u,v) and (v,u), a single “residual capacity between u and v” conflates two different edges. The clean fixes: keep flows per-edge (an edge list with explicit reverse-edge indices, as every production Dinic does), or split one antiparallel edge through a dummy node u→w→v. Storing capacities in a single C[u][v] matrix silently corrupts antiparallel inputs.

4. Capacities are on edges, not nodes — until you split them

Max-flow constrains edges. If your problem limits how much may pass through a vertex (a router’s throughput, a grid cell used once), the model does not see it — until you apply vertex-splitting: replace v by vin→vout carrying the vertex’s capacity. Forgetting this is the usual reason a disjoint-paths or segmentation model returns too large an answer.

5. Multiple sources or sinks

Several sources s₁…sk or sinks? Do not run the algorithm k times. Add a super-source S with edges S→si (capacity = that source’s supply, or ∞) and a symmetric super-sink. One run on the augmented network solves it, and the min cut is interpreted on the original.

6. The minimum cut is not unique — but residual reachability picks one

A network can have many minimum cuts of equal capacity. The construction in the theorem’s (2)⇒(3) step returns a specific one: S = vertices reachable from s in the final residual graph (the “source-minimal” min cut). Reachability into t gives the “sink-minimal” one. If your application needs a particular cut (smallest source side, say), say which and compute it deliberately — do not assume the cut is the cut.

the litmus testBefore trusting a flow model, ask: Does my residual capacity include the backward term? (correctness) Are my limits on edges or on vertices? (split if vertices) Did I fix a path rule, or ship the bare method? (termination) The first guards the answer’s value, the second its meaning, the third whether your program stops at all.
12 practice

Problems

1

Maximum Number of Accepted Invitations

bipartite matching · the core gadget · LeetCode 1820

There are m boys and n girls. You are given an m×n binary matrix grid where grid[i][j]==1 means boy i can invite girl j. Each boy invites at most one girl and each girl accepts at most one invitation. Return the maximum number of invitations that can be accepted. Constraints: 1 ≤ m, n ≤ 200. Example: grid=[[1,1,1],[1,0,1],[0,0,1]] → 3; grid=[[1,0,1,0],[1,0,0,0],[0,0,1,0]] → 2. This is maximum bipartite matching exactly: build the s→boy→girl→t network of §09 and find the max flow — or run the equivalent augmenting-path matching (Kuhn’s) directly. State your capacities and justify why the integrality theorem makes the flow a matching.

2

Minimum Lines to Cover All Ones (König’s theorem)

matching = cover duality · min-cut in disguise · classic

Given a binary matrix mat (R×C), a line is an entire row or an entire column; placing a line “covers” every cell on it. 13 recap & next

The one thing to remember

The maximum flow from s to t equals the minimum cut separating them — one number that both solves the problem and certifies the answer. Augmenting-path algorithms find it by repeatedly pushing flow along s–t paths in the residual graph, whose backward edges let the algorithm undo earlier commitments; Edmonds–Karp’s shortest-path rule makes that O(VE²), free of the capacities. The same theorem, read as a min-cut or a matching, models assignment, disjoint paths, and partition problems — and the integrality theorem hands the combinatorial answer back whole. When Edmonds–Karp halts, the vertices still reachable from s in the residual graph are the minimum cut: the algorithm prints its own proof.
