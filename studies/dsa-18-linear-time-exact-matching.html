02 What you will be able to do

Objectives & prerequisites

By the end of this page you should be able to take “does pattern P occur in text T, and where?” and answer it in O(n+m) three different ways — and say precisely why each is linear, not merely that it is.

After this you can…

  • State the exact-matching problem as a transformation between formal spaces, and show exactly which comparisons the naïve scanner throws away.
  • Define and compute the KMP prefix function π — the longest-proper-border table — and use it to match in one forward pass that never moves the text pointer backward.
  • Prove KMP runs in O(n) with a potential argument: the failure-link pointer is the bank balance, and following links spends credit that matching banked.
  • Build the Z-array with the Z-box reuse trick, and turn matching into a single Z-computation over P # T.
  • Fingerprint with a rolling hash (Rabin–Karp): evaluate a string as a polynomial mod a prime, slide the window in O(1), and bound the spurious-collision probability.
  • Diagnose the failure modes: the off-by-one border, the missing separator, integer overflow, the adversarial hash, and when randomness is the wrong tool.

03 Build it from first principles

The naïve scan forgets; that is the whole bug

Strip the problem of its costume. Exact matching is a transformation: the input space is a pair of strings over an alphabet Σ — a text T of length n and a pattern P of length m — and the output is the set of shifts { s : T[s … s+m−1] = P }. Nothing more. The obvious method repeats work. Finding that redundancy gives us the algorithm.

The obvious method tries every shift s from 0 to n−m and, at each, compares P against the window character by character until a mismatch or a full match. It is correct. It is also forgetful: after it discovers a mismatch at shift s, it slides to s+1 and begins comparing from scratch — re-reading text characters it already inspected one step earlier. On a benign input the forgetting is cheap. On an adversarial one it is fatal.

The input that kills it

Let T = aaaa…a (n copies) and P = aaa…ab (m−1 copies of a, then one b). At every one of the n−m+1 shifts, the scanner matches all m−1 leading as and only then hits the b and fails. That is (n−m+1)(m) comparisons — Θ(nm). With n = 10⁶, m = 10³ it is a billion. The pattern told us at the first shift that the window was all as; the scanner refused to remember.

So the design question is forced, and it is the right question: what is the minimal precomputed object that lets the matcher never re-read a text character? The answer is a table of the pattern's own self-similarity. When we have matched a prefix of P against the text and then mismatch, the characters we just consumed are known — they equal that prefix of P. If some suffix of what we matched is also a prefix of P, we may slide the pattern so that this shorter prefix lines up, and continue without moving the text pointer back at all. The amount we can keep is the length of the longest border — a string that is simultaneously a proper prefix and a proper suffix — of the part we matched.

A useful framing — matching is running a machine

Here is the clean abstraction. KMP is the execution of a deterministic finite automaton with m+1 states, “number of pattern characters matched so far,” reading the text one symbol at a time. On a match it takes a forward edge (state k → k+1); on a mismatch it takes a failure edge to state π[k−1] and tries again. The entire automaton is encoded by one array, π. What can be mechanised here is everything — there is no judgement left once π exists; the only real question is how few steps the machine takes, and §05 answers it with a potential function.

04 The core object · fully worked

The prefix function: a table of borders

Everything in KMP rests on one array. Fix it precisely first, then we will compute it for a real pattern and watch the failure-link chain appear.

For a string P of length m (0-indexed), define the prefix function π[i] as the length of the longest proper prefix of the substring P[0…i] that is also a suffix of P[0…i]. “Proper” means we forbid the whole substring itself (otherwise the answer is trivially the full length). Equivalently, π[i] is the length of the longest border of the prefix ending at i.

Definition — the longest border length
π[i]= max0≤k≤i {k: P[0…k−1]= P[i−k+1…i]}

The empty border (k=0) always qualifies, so π is well-defined and π[0]=0 always. The decisive structural fact — the one that makes the computation linear — is that the borders of a string form a chain: the next-longest border of P[0…i] is the longest border of its own longest border. That is why, when the character at the current border length disagrees, we fall back to π[k−1] rather than starting over: we are walking down the border chain, trying ever-shorter candidates.

Computing π in one pass

The algorithm grows i left to right while maintaining k, the length of the current longest border. To extend to position i: if P[i] equals P[k] the border grows by one (k+1); otherwise we drop k to π[k−1] — the next candidate down the border chain — and retry, stopping at k=0.

# π[i] = length of the longest proper prefix of P[0..i] that is also a suffix.
def prefix_function(P):
    m = len(P)
    pi = [0] * m
    k = 0                            # length of the current longest border
    for i in range(1, m):
        while k > 0 and P[i] != P[k]:
            k = pi[k - 1]            # fall back along the border chain
        if P[i] == P[k]:
            k += 1                    # the border extends by one character
        pi[i] = k
    return pi

Bonus structure — periodicity falls out for free

A string's smallest period is p = m − π[m−1], and the whole string is an integer number of copies of its length-p prefix iff p divides m. Example: ababab has m=6, π[5]=4, so p=2 and 2 | 6 — it is (ab)³. Example: ababaca has π[6]=1, so p=6 and 6 ∤ 7 — not periodic. This single line solves two of the practice problems below; the prefix function is a periodicity detector in disguise.

05 The match, and why it is linear

KMP, and the potential argument on the pointer

With π in hand, matching is the same loop applied to the text — and the famous part is the proof. KMP is not obviously linear: one text character can trigger a whole cascade of failure-link jumps. The fix is the potential method from Lesson 02.

The matcher scans T with the identical mechanics: keep k = how many pattern characters currently match the text ending here; on agreement advance, on disagreement fall back along π; when k reaches m we have a full occurrence, and we fall back to π[m−1] to keep hunting (this is what lets overlapping matches be found).

def kmp_search(T, P):
    pi = prefix_function(P)
    k, hits = 0, []
    for i in range(len(T)):       # i sweeps the text ONCE, never moves back
        while k > 0 and T[i] != P[k]:
            k = pi[k - 1]            # follow failure links — the only "expensive" line
        if T[i] == P[k]:
            k += 1
        if k == len(P):
            hits.append(i - len(P) + 1)
            k = pi[k - 1]            # slide by the border, keep overlapping matches
    return hits

After the first hit, k resets to 1 (not 0): the trailing “a” of occurrence one is reused as the leading “a” of occurrence two. The text pointer i only ever increases.

The potential argument — the failure-link pointer is a bank balance

Look at the inner while. In the worst single iteration of the outer loop it could fire many times. So a line-by-line count gives only the useless bound “≤ m per character, hence O(nm).” To get O(n) we must amortize, and the right potential is sitting in plain sight: let Φ = k, the current match length — the value of the failure-link pointer itself.

Amortized accounting — Φ = k, the pointer, never negative
∑iterations cactual = ∑iterations (camort−ΔΦ) ≤ ∑iterations camort +Φstart −Φend

Now read off the three facts of the loop:

  • Φ rises by at most 1 per text character. The only increase is the single k += 1, which happens at most once per outer step. Over the whole scan, total rise ≤ n.
  • Every failure-link jump strictly lowers Φ. Each inner-loop iteration sets k = π[k−1] < k, so it drops Φ by at least 1.
  • Φ never goes negative (k ≥ 0), and starts at 0.

Therefore the total number of failure-link jumps across the entire search is bounded by the total rise plus the starting balance: (jumps) ≤ (rises) + Φ_start − Φ_end ≤ n. The outer loop runs n times; the inner loop runs ≤ n times in total. The whole match is O(n), and the same argument on the pattern gives O(m) to build π — so KMP is O(n+m). Each matched character banks one unit of credit; each fall-back spends credit it cannot overdraw.

06 The same self-similarity, mirrored

The Z-algorithm: prefixes from every position

The prefix function asks “how does the pattern overlap its end?” The Z-algorithm asks the mirror question — “how does the string overlap its start, from every position?” — and answers it, again, in one linear pass with one reused window.

For a string S of length n, define Z[i] = the length of the longest substring starting at index i that is also a prefix of S. (Z[0] is left undefined or set to n.) Computed naïvely this is O(n²); the trick that makes it linear is to remember the Z-box — the interval [l, r) that reaches farthest right among all prefix-matches found so far, i.e. S[l … r−1] equals a prefix of S.

When we reach a new index i that lies inside the current box (i < r), its answer is partly known for free: the box says S[i …] mirrors S[i−l …] near the front, so Z[i] starts at min(r−i, Z[i−l]) — copy the mirror value, but never trust it past the box edge r. Only the part beyond r needs real character comparisons, and every such comparison pushes r forward — which (another potential argument) can happen only n times total.

def z_function(S):
    n = len(S)
    Z = [0] * n
    l = r = 0                        # [l, r) = rightmost prefix-matching box
    for i in range(1, n):
        if i < r:
            Z[i] = min(r - i, Z[i - l])   # copy from the mirror, capped by the box
        while i + Z[i] < n and S[Z[i]] == S[i + Z[i]]:
            Z[i] += 1                # extend past the box, char by char
        if i + Z[i] > r:
            l, r = i, i + Z[i]          # this match reaches further right — adopt it
    return Z

Matching with Z — the separator trick

To find P in T, build the string S = P + # + T where # is a sentinel that occurs in neither (so no match can straddle it). Compute Z over S. Wherever Z[i] = m for an index i in the T-region, the pattern occurs at T-position i − (m+1). The sentinel caps every Z at m, so a value of exactly m is an occurrence.

KMP and Z carry the same information — both are linear-time encodings of a string's self-overlap, and you can convert one to the other in linear time. Pick by ergonomics: π is the natural streaming matcher (it consumes the text online, one symbol at a time); Z is often cleaner for offline “analyse this whole string” tasks such as counting periods or the palindrome problem in §09.

07 A different bet — arithmetic, not automata

Rabin–Karp: fingerprint the window

KMP and Z are deterministic and comparison-based. Rabin–Karp makes an entirely different wager: turn each length-m window into a number, compare numbers instead of strings, and update that number in O(1) as the window slides. It trades a small, controllable probability of error for arithmetic simplicity — and it generalises to multi-pattern and 2-D search where automata strain.

Read a string as the digits of a number in base b, then reduce mod a large prime q to keep it small. This is just Horner's rule — evaluating the string as a polynomial at the point b:

The polynomial fingerprint — a string evaluated at b, mod q
H(s)= ( ∑k=0m−1 s[k]·bm−1−k ) modq

The magic is the rolling update. Equal strings always have equal fingerprints; the point of the polynomial form is that sliding the window one step is three cheap operations — drop the high digit, shift everything up by a factor of b, add the new low digit — all mod q:

Slide the window in O(1) — h = b^(m−1) mod q is precomputed
H(T[i+1…i+m])= ( (H−T[i]·h) ·b+T[i+m] ) modq 
def rabin_karp(T, P, b=256, q=1_000_000_007):
    n, m = len(T), len(P)
    if m > n: return []
    h = pow(b, m - 1, q)            # b^(m-1) mod q — weight of the top char
    hp = ht = 0
    for i in range(m):               # hash P and the first window together
        hp = (hp * b + ord(P[i])) % q
        ht = (ht * b + ord(T[i])) % q
    hits = []
    for i in range(n - m + 1):
        if hp == ht and T[i:i + m] == P:   # VERIFY — kill spurious hits
            hits.append(i)
        if i < n - m:
            ht = ((ht - ord(T[i]) * h) * b + ord(T[i + m])) % q
    return hits

Why it is expected-linear, and how it can betray you

Two distinct strings of length m are two distinct degree-(m−1) polynomials; their difference is a non-zero polynomial of degree ≤ m−1, which has at most m−1 roots mod a prime q. So for a randomly chosen base b, the probability they collide is ≤ (m−1)/q — vanishing for a 64-bit prime. With verification, every reported hit is real (a Las Vegas algorithm: always correct, expected O(n+m)); skip verification and you get a Monte Carlo matcher that is faster but wrong with tiny probability. The worst case, when an adversary or a fixed bad b forces a collision at every window, degrades to Θ(nm) — which is exactly why the modulus and base should be large and, ideally, randomised per run.

08 Where it breaks

Failure modes & common mistakes

These algorithms fail in two flavours: a subtle index slip that quietly returns the wrong matches, and a hash bet that blows up only on the input you did not test. My own bias is to trust a clean abstraction past its edge — so here is exactly where each edge is.

1 · The off-by-one in the border fall-back

The single most common KMP bug: writing k = pi[k] instead of k = pi[k − 1]. The prefix function indexes by the last matched position, which is k − 1 when k characters match. Get this wrong and the failure chain is corrupted — you will either loop or skip occurrences. Equally, remember that π[0] must be 0 by definition (a one-character string has no proper border); seeding it otherwise poisons everything downstream.

2 · The missing separator in the Z / concatenation tricks

Matching via S = P + T with no sentinel lets a “match” straddle the seam — a prefix of the pattern bleeding into the text — and over-reports. Use a separator # that is guaranteed absent from the alphabet. The same caution applies to the palindrome and shortest-cover constructions: the sentinel is what caps every Z at m and keeps the two halves from contaminating each other.

3 · Integer overflow in the rolling hash

In languages with fixed-width integers, ht · b or ord(T[i]) · h overflows a 32- or 64-bit word long before you take the modulus. Reduce mod q at every step, keep the product in a 64-bit (or 128-bit) type, and choose q small enough that q · b still fits. The subtraction (ht − ord·h) can also go negative — add q before the modulus, or use a language whose % already returns a non-negative result.

4 · The adversarial hash — when randomness is the wrong tool

A fixed base and modulus are public, so an adversary (or an unlucky data set) can manufacture collisions at every window and drag Rabin–Karp to Θ(nm) — the “anti-hash” attack seen in competitive judges. If inputs are adversarial, randomise b per run, use a 64-bit prime, or use two independent moduli (double hashing) so a collision needs to happen in both. And if you cannot tolerate any wrong answer or any slow case, do not bet at all — use KMP or Z, which are unconditionally O(n+m).

5 · Skipping verification (Monte Carlo where you needed Las Vegas)

Dropping the T[i:i+m] == P check makes Rabin–Karp faster and occasionally wrong. That is acceptable for a fingerprint pre-filter or a deduplication heuristic, and unacceptable for, say, a security-sensitive search. Know which regime you are in. The verification is what converts a probabilistic filter into an exact matcher.

6 · Forgetting overlaps, and the empty pattern

After a full match, resetting k = 0 instead of k = π[m−1] misses overlapping occurrences (find “aa” in “aaa” — there are two, at 0 and 1). And the degenerate inputs bite: an empty pattern conventionally matches at every position (including n); a pattern longer than the text matches nowhere. Decide and test these before they decide for you.

The one check that catches most KMP/Z bugs

Brute-force a naïve O(nm) matcher in five lines and fuzz it against your fast one on thousands of random short strings over a tiny alphabet (say {a, b}, lengths ≤ 12). Small alphabets maximise borders and overlaps — exactly the cases where index slips hide. If the two ever disagree, you have a reproducible minimal counterexample in hand.

10 Recap & what's next

The one thing to remember

Linear-time matching is precomputed self-similarity. A pattern's borders (the prefix function π) — or, mirrored, its Z-array — tell the matcher exactly how far it may slide on a mismatch without ever re-reading the text, and the failure-link pointer's own value is the potential that proves the whole thing runs in O(n+m). Rabin–Karp makes the orthogonal bet: a string is a number, the window rolls in O(1), and a quick verification keeps the answer exact.

Notice the shape of the trick, because it recurs: when a brute-force method forgets, the cure is a small precomputed table of structure, and the cost of consulting it is paid by an amortized argument. That is the same melody as the doubling array and union-find from Lesson 02 — the failure-link pointer is just one more potential function.
