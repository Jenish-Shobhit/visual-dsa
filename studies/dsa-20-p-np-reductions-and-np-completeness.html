Try this first · To show a new problem H is NP hard, which way should a known NP hard problem reduce?

Known hard problem ≤ₚ H. An algorithm for H would then solve the known hard problem.

Here is the strangest asymmetry in all of computing. Hand me a filled-in Sudoku and I can check it in a glance; hand me a blank one and the world's fastest method is barely better than guessing. A 1000-variable logic formula has 21000 possible answers — more than the atoms in the universe squared — yet a claimed answer is verified in microseconds. This lecture formalizes that gulf. We define the class of problems we can solve fast (P), the class we can merely verify fast (NP), and the machine — the polynomial-time reduction — that lets one problem inherit another's difficulty. Then we prove that a single problem, SAT, is hard enough to encode them all, and learn the craft of pinning a new problem to that same wall.

A number to set the stakes. Brute force on a SAT instance with just 100 Boolean variables means searching 2100 ≈ 1.3 × 1030 assignments. At a billion checks per second that is 40 trillion years — three thousand times the age of the universe. Verifying one proposed assignment: about a hundred operations, instant.

The whole question of this lecture — the P vs NP problem, one of seven Clay Millennium Prizes, $1,000,000 unclaimed since 2000 — is whether that gulf is real or merely a failure of our imagination. And the astonishing fact we will prove is that thousands of unrelated problems, in scheduling and biology and circuit design, are secretly the same problem: solve one efficiently and they all fall; prove one truly hard and they all stand.

01 the asymmetry beneath everything

Two kinds of effort: solving and verifying

I begin, as always, by stripping the surface away to find the computational skeleton. Behind “route a delivery fleet,” “pack a container,” “schedule the exams,” “fold this protein,” lies the same skeleton, and it has two faces. One face asks you to produce a solution from nothing. The other merely asks you to recognize a solution someone hands you. For an astonishing range of problems, the second is easy and the first appears impossibly hard — and nobody on Earth can prove the appearance is not an illusion.

To reason about this precisely we must shrink the problems to their most austere form. An optimization problem (“find the shortest tour”) carries a number in its answer; that is awkward to classify. So we convert every problem into a decision problem — one whose answer is a single bit, yes or no. Not “what is the shortest tour?” but “is there a tour of length ≤ k?” This loses nothing essential: if you can answer the decision version for every k efficiently, a binary search over k recovers the optimum with only a logarithmic number of extra calls. The decision form is the clean object the whole theory is built on.

A decision problem is, formally, a language L: the set of all input strings whose answer is “yes.” Encode an input x as a string of bits; then deciding the problem means deciding membership, x ∈ L? Every class we define — P, NP — is a set of languages, a set of decision problems grouped by how much work membership costs. This is the abstraction that makes the boundary of efficiency a mathematical object rather than a feeling.

the formal lensTwo verbs, two costs. Solving is a function from inputs to answers; verifying is a function from (input, proposed-answer) pairs to a single accept/reject bit. The entire drama of this lecture is whether a fast verifier can always be turned into a fast solver. Hold that distinction; everything hangs on it.
02 objectives & prerequisites

What you will be able to do

  • Define the classes P and NP precisely — the second by its verifier-and-certificate form — and explain why “polynomial time” is the robust, machine-independent meaning of “efficient.”
  • State what a polynomial-time reduction A ≤p B is, and read its direction correctly: it certifies that B is at least as hard as A.
  • Distinguish NP-hard from NP-complete, and explain why a single polynomial algorithm for one NP-complete problem would prove P = NP.
  • Recount the Cook–Levin theorem — that SAT is NP-complete — and the one idea that powers its proof: computation is locally checkable.
  • Execute the five-step NP-completeness proof recipe on a new problem, building the gadget reduction 3\text{-}SAT ≤p Independent Set end to end and proving both directions.
  • Diagnose the classic errors: reducing the wrong way, confusing NP with “not polynomial,” and mistaking a pseudo-polynomial algorithm for a polynomial one.

03 efficient means polynomial

The class P

A decision problem is in P if some algorithm decides it in time bounded by a polynomial in the input size n — that is, O(nk) for some fixed constant k. Sorting (n log n), shortest paths (n2), matching, linear programming, primality testing — all of P. Formally, P collects every language decidable within some polynomial time bound:

the class P
P= ⋃k≥1 TIME(nk)

Why draw the efficiency line at polynomial, and not at, say, n2, or at “runs in a second”? Because polynomial is the only boundary that is robust — it does not move when you change irrelevant details. Three properties earn it the role.

  • Machine-independence. A one-tape Turing machine, a modern RAM, a parallel cluster — they simulate one another with only polynomial overhead. So “polynomial on machine A” equals “polynomial on machine B.” No reasonable model disagrees about what P contains. n2 alone has no such immunity.
  • Closure under composition. Feed the output of one polynomial routine into another and the whole is still polynomial — a polynomial of a polynomial is a polynomial. This is exactly what lets us build big efficient algorithms out of small ones, and (we will see) what makes reductions chain.
  • The decisive gap. Polynomial versus exponential is the gap that actually bites. n3 at n=1000 is a billion — a blink. 2n at n=1000 is a number with 300 digits. Constants and low-degree exponents are noise beside this cliff.

This identification — efficient = polynomial-time — is the Cobham–Edmonds thesis. It is a modelling choice, not a theorem, and it has honest exceptions (an n100 algorithm is useless; a 2n/100 one may be fine for small n). But as the dividing line for a theory of hardness it is unreasonably effective, precisely because it is the one line no detail of encoding or hardware can shift.

on encodingOne caveat the theory insists on: inputs are written in binary (or any base ≥ 2), and “size” means the number of bits. A number N has size log N, not N. This sounds pedantic until §11, where it is the entire difference between a genuine polynomial algorithm and a fraud that only looks like one.
04 hard to find, easy to check

The class NP

Now we formalize the “easy to verify” face. A decision problem is in NP if every yes-instance has a short, checkable proof. Precisely: there is a polynomial-time verifier V and a polynomial p such that for every input x,

the verifier definition of NP
x∈L ⟺ ∃c (|c|≤p(|x|)) such that V(x,c)=1

The string c is the certificate (or witness): the “solution” that makes verification trivial. Three demands make this exact — and dropping any one breaks the class. The certificate must be polynomially short (|c| ≤ p(|x|)); the verifier must run in polynomial time; and the logic is one-sided — a yes-instance has at least one accepting certificate, a no-instance has none whatsoever. You can never be fooled into accepting a no-instance, because no certificate exists to fool you with.

Read three problems through this lens and the pattern leaps out:

Clique (size ≥ k)a set of k verticesall k(k−1)/2 pairs are edges

In every row the certificate is tiny and the check is a fast scan. Finding the certificate is the agony; recognizing it is a formality. That gap is NP.

The name is a trap that has misled generations. NP stands for Nondeterministic Polynomial, not “Non-Polynomial.” The equivalent original definition imagines a machine that, at each step, may guess a bit — nondeterministically splitting into all branches at once — and accepts if any branch accepts within polynomial time. Guessing the certificate bit by bit and then checking it is precisely the verifier picture; the two definitions describe the same class. NP is a class of problems that are easy to check, not a synonym for “hard.”

One containment is immediate and worth stating as the spine of everything: P ⊆ NP. If you can solve a problem in polynomial time, you can verify it in polynomial time too — ignore the certificate entirely and just re-solve from scratch. So every easy problem is also easily checkable. The trillion-dollar question is whether the reverse holds.

To verify is to read a proof; to solve is to write one. P vs NP asks whether writing is ever truly harder than reading.
05 the open question

P vs NP: a picture of two worlds

Does P = NP? Is every problem whose solutions are easy to check also easy to find? Nobody knows. It is the deepest open problem in computer science and among the most famous in all of mathematics. The overwhelming consensus — from decades of failed attempts to collapse the two — is that P ≠ NP: that there really are problems whose answers we can recognize but never quickly discover. But consensus is not proof, and the $1,000,000 prize sits untouched.

The stakes are not academic. If P = NP and the algorithm were practical, most modern cryptography would dissolve overnight (breaking a code is verifying a key, which would become as easy as finding one), while optimal logistics, automated theorem-proving, and protein design would become routine. If P ≠ NP, we gain a permanent licence to stop searching for fast exact algorithms on a vast catalogue of problems — and to spend our effort instead on approximation and heuristics, the subject of the next lecture.

06 transporting difficulty

Polynomial-time reductions

We cannot yet prove any single problem is hard — that would settle P vs NP. But we can prove problems are hard relative to each other, and that turns out to be almost as powerful. The device is the polynomial-time reduction (a many-one or Karp reduction). We say A ≤p B — “A reduces to B” — if there is a polynomial-time computable function f that transforms instances of A into instances of B, preserving the answer:

a polynomial-time many-one reduction
A≤pB :⟺ ∃ poly-time f with x∈A ⟺ f(x)∈B

That biconditional is the whole contract: f must send every yes-instance of A to a yes-instance of B, and every no-instance of A to a no-instance of B. It is not enough to map yes to yes; the no-direction is half the proof and the half beginners forget.

What does it buy you? A solver for B becomes a solver for A: given x, compute f(x), run B's solver, return its answer. Since f is polynomial and (by closure) a polynomial fed into a polynomial stays polynomial, if B ∈ P then A ∈ P. Contrapositively — the way we always use it — if A is hard, then B is hard. The reduction transports difficulty from A to B.

Reductions compose: if A ≤p B and B ≤p C then A ≤p C — just run the two transformers in sequence, and a polynomial of a polynomial is polynomial. This transitivity is what lets a single hard problem seed an entire genealogy of hard problems, as we will see in §10.

read the direction or perishA ≤p B means B is at least as hard as A — the larger problem is on the right. To prove your new problem X is hard, you reduce a known-hard problem to X (known ≤p X). Reducing X to something hard proves nothing about X. This single inversion is the most common fatal error in the subject; we return to it in §11.
07 the hardest problems in NP

NP-hard and NP-complete

Reductions give us a way to rank difficulty, so we can ask: what are the hardest problems in NP? Two definitions pin them down.

NP-hard. A problem B is NP-hard if every problem A ∈ NP reduces to it: A ≤p B for all A ∈ NP. It is at least as hard as everything in NP — but it need not itself be in NP (it could be far harder, even undecidable).

NP-complete. A problem is NP-complete if it is NP-hard and in NP. These are the hardest problems that still have the “easy to check” courtesy — the precise summit of NP.

The definition makes NP-complete problems a single coupled fate. Suppose X is NP-complete and someone finds a polynomial algorithm for it. Take any A ∈ NP. Because X is NP-hard, A ≤p X; compose that reduction with X's fast solver and you solve A in polynomial time. Since A was arbitrary, every problem in NP is now in P — that is, P = NP. Conversely, if P ≠ NP then no NP-complete problem is in P. So the entire P-vs-NP question reduces to the fate of any one NP-complete problem:

the coupling
any one NP-complete X∈P ⟺ P=NP

This is why NP-completeness is not a mathematical curiosity but the working tool of every algorithm designer. When you prove your problem NP-complete you have proven something practical and final: no polynomial algorithm for it will ever be found unless one is found for all of them at once. It is a licence to stop hunting for the perfect algorithm and start engineering an approximate or exponential one — honestly, with a theorem behind the decision rather than a shrug.

But the definitions have a chicken-and-egg gap. To prove a new problem NP-hard by reduction, we need a known NP-hard problem to reduce from. Where does the very first one come from — how do we show that every problem in NP, infinitely many of them, reduces to a single specific problem, without already having one in hand? That is the miracle Cook and Levin performed.

08 the keystone

Cook–Levin: SAT is universal

First, the problem. SAT (Boolean satisfiability) asks: given a Boolean formula φ over variables x1,…,xn — usually in conjunctive normal form, an AND of clauses, each clause an OR of literals — is there an assignment of true/false to the variables making φ evaluate to true? A certificate is just the assignment; checking it is a linear scan, so SAT ∈ NP.

Cook–Levin Theorem (1971). SAT is NP-complete. That is, SAT ∈ NP and every problem in NP reduces to SAT in polynomial time.

The astonishing claim is the second half: a single formula language captures the difficulty of all easily-checkable problems. The proof idea is the most beautiful in the field, and it is a deeply formal one — it builds, mechanically, a Boolean formula that simulates a computation.

Take any L ∈ NP. By definition some nondeterministic machine M decides it: on input x of length n, M guesses a certificate and checks it within T = p(n) steps for some polynomial p. We capture an entire run as a tableau: a T × T grid whose row i is the machine's full configuration (tape contents, head position, internal state) at time i. Introduce one Boolean variable for each possible “cell (i,j) holds symbol s” — polynomially many in all. Now constrain those variables with four families of clauses, every one of polynomial size:

  1. Start. Row 0 spells out the initial configuration — the input x on the tape, the start state, and free variables for the guessed certificate.
  2. Well-formed. Each cell holds exactly one symbol; each row is a legal configuration (one head, one state).
  3. Transitions — the heart. Every 2×3 window of cells (two adjacent rows, three adjacent columns) must match one of M's allowed local moves. Crucially, a step of computation only changes the tape near the head, so global correctness of the whole run is nothing more than the correctness of every little window. This is the soul of the proof: computation is locally checkable.
  4. Accept. At least one row contains an accepting state.

Conjoin all four families into one CNF formula φM,x. It has polynomially many variables and clauses, and a polynomial-time procedure writes it down from M and x. By construction, φM,x is satisfiable exactly when M has an accepting computation on x — i.e. when x ∈ L. That is a polynomial reduction L ≤p SAT, and since L was an arbitrary member of NP, SAT is NP-hard. With SAT ∈ NP, it is NP-complete. □

One corollary we will use immediately. By rewriting each long clause with auxiliary variables, SAT reduces to 3-SAT — satisfiability of CNF formulas with exactly three literals per clause — so 3-SAT is NP-complete too. Its rigid shape (every clause a triple) makes it the perfect launchpad for gadget reductions, which is exactly what we build next.

09 the craft, worked end to end

Worked example: 3-SAT ≤p Independent Set

Cook–Levin gave us one hard problem for free. Every NP-completeness proof since is a reduction from a known one. Here is the whole recipe, then a complete worked instance of the most elegant gadget reduction in the subject.

  1. Membership. Show your target problem X ∈ NP — exhibit a polynomial certificate and verifier.
  2. Pick a source. Choose a known NP-complete problem Y (here, 3-SAT).
  3. Build the map. Give a transformation f from instances of Y to instances of X.
  4. Bound it. Prove f runs in polynomial time (so its output is polynomially sized).
  5. Prove the biconditional. Show y is a yes-instance of Y iff f(y) is a yes-instance of X — both directions.

Steps 1–4 are usually routine; step 5, the “iff,” is where the real proof lives. Now the target.

The target: Independent Set

An independent set in a graph G is a set of vertices, no two of which are joined by an edge. The decision problem IndSet: given G and an integer k, does G have an independent set of size ≥ k? Membership (step 1): the certificate is the set itself; verify it has k vertices and check every pair is non-adjacent in O(k2). So IndSet ∈ NP.

The construction (steps 2–4)

Source problem (step 2): 3-SAT. Take a formula φ with m clauses, each a disjunction of three literals. Build a graph f(φ) like so:

  • Clause gadgets. For each clause make a triangle: three vertices, one per literal, with all three edges among them. The triangle's mutual edges mean an independent set can pick at most one vertex from each clause.
  • Conflict edges. Connect a literal-vertex to every vertex carrying its negation, across all triangles — join each xi to each ¬xi. These edges forbid choosing a literal and its complement together, i.e. forbid an inconsistent assignment.
  • Threshold. Set k = m, the number of clauses.

Polynomial (step 4): the graph has 3m vertices and at most 3m + (3m)2 edges; a single pass over the literals builds it in O(m2) time. Comfortably polynomial.

Let us make it concrete. Take

the instance
φ= (x1∨x2∨¬x3) ∧ (¬x1∨x2∨x3) ∧ (x1∨¬x2∨¬x3)

Three clauses, so k = 3. The construction yields three triangles wired together by the conflict edges x1–¬x1, x2–¬x2, x3–¬x3:

The biconditional (step 5)

This is the proof. We must show: φ is satisfiable iff f(φ) has an independent set of size k = m.

(⇒) Satisfiable assignment gives an independent set. Suppose φ is satisfied by some assignment α. Every clause has at least one literal made true by α; in each triangle pick exactly one such true-literal vertex. That is m vertices, one per triangle. Are they independent? No two lie in the same triangle (we took one each), so no triangle edge joins them. And no two are complementary literals — α cannot make both xi and ¬xi true — so no conflict edge joins them either. The m vertices are an independent set of size k.

(⇐) An independent set gives a satisfying assignment. Suppose f(φ) has an independent set I with |I| = m. Each triangle's three vertices are mutually adjacent, so I contains at most one per triangle; with m vertices spread over m triangles, it holds exactly one per triangle. Now read off an assignment: for each vertex in I, set its literal to true. This is consistent — we never set both xi and ¬xi true, because those vertices are joined by a conflict edge and could not both be in the independent set. (Variables left untouched get any value.) Each clause now contains a literal we made true, so every clause is satisfied, so φ is satisfiable. □

why we connect complements, not identical literals

Notice in Figure 4 the set picks the x1 vertex in both C1 and C3, with no edge between them. That is deliberate. Choosing x1 in two clauses just means “x1 is true,” which is perfectly consistent — one variable can satisfy many clauses. The only thing we must forbid is choosing x1 in one place and ¬x1 in another, since that is a contradiction. Hence conflict edges join a literal to its negation only. Wiring identical literals together would wrongly outlaw a legal assignment and break the (⇐) direction.

The two directions together are the reduction. Combined with membership in NP, Independent Set is NP-complete. And notice what we actually did: we never searched the 2n assignments. We translated the logical structure of satisfaction into the combinatorial structure of non-adjacency — OR-of-literals became a triangle, consistency became a conflict edge. That translation of structure, not brute force, is the entire craft.

10 one root, a thousand descendants

The web of hardness

Because reductions compose, every problem you reduce 3-SAT to becomes a new launchpad. In 1972 — one year after Cook–Levin — Richard Karp published 21 problems, each proven NP-complete by a short chain of reductions from SAT. Today the catalogue runs into the thousands, spanning logistics, scheduling, biology, circuit layout, and games. They form a single connected genealogy: prove any one of them is in P and the whole tree collapses into P with it.

This is the practical payoff of the whole theory. You almost never argue from NP directly — you reach into this web, grab the nearest relative of your problem, and reduce. Want to show your scheduling problem is hard? Find the clause-or-conflict structure inside it and map 3-SAT in. Want a routing problem? Hamiltonian Cycle or TSP is your source. The genealogy is a library of structural analogies, and an NP-completeness proof is the act of recognizing which classic problem your new one secretly is.

11 where it breaks

Failure modes & common mistakes

This material is treacherous in a particular way: the errors are not arithmetic but conceptual, and a confident proof can be entirely backwards. The formal lens flags each of these early — usually as a violated direction or a confused class boundary.

1. Reducing the wrong direction — the cardinal sin

To prove X is NP-hard you must reduce a known-hard problem to X (3\text{-}SAT ≤p X). Students routinely do the reverse — reduce X to 3-SAT — and prove the exact opposite of what they wanted: that X is no harder than 3-SAT, which (since everything in NP reduces to SAT) says nothing at all. Memorize it as a slogan: reduce FROM the monster you know, TO the problem you're accusing. The accused goes on the right of ≤p.

2. “NP” does not mean “not polynomial”

NP is Nondeterministic Polynomial — a verification class — and P ⊆ NP, so most easy problems are in NP. Saying “this is in NP” is not an insult or a hardness claim; sorting is in NP. The hardness claim is “NP-hard” or “NP-complete.” Keep the words straight or every sentence you write will be ambiguous.

3. NP-complete does not mean unsolvable

These problems are perfectly solvable — by exponential search, and often very practically for the sizes you actually meet (modern SAT solvers dispatch formulas with millions of variables). NP-completeness forbids a provably polynomial worst-case algorithm (assuming P≠NP); it does not forbid solving instances. Do not confuse it with undecidability (the Halting Problem), which forbids any algorithm at all.

4. Forgetting membership, or the certificate-size bound

An NP-completeness proof has two halves: NP-hardness (the reduction) and membership in NP (a polynomial certificate). Skip the second and you have only shown NP-hard — which may be strictly more, as your problem might not even be in NP. And the certificate must be polynomially short: a “proof” that is itself exponential (e.g. “the list of all winning strategies”) does not put a problem in NP.

5. Proving only one direction of the “iff”

A reduction must preserve the answer both ways: yes-maps-to-yes and no-maps-to-no. It is easy to design f so that satisfying assignments produce solutions, then forget to rule out a “spurious” solution to f(x) when x was a no-instance. The (⇐) direction — every solution to the constructed instance forces a solution to the original — is exactly where gadgets are designed to be tight.

6. Pseudo-polynomial is not polynomial — weak vs. strong

Subset Sum and Knapsack have O(n·t) dynamic programs (Lecture 15) — yet they are NP-complete. No contradiction: t is a value, and writing it takes only log t bits, so O(n·t) is exponential in the input size. Such algorithms are pseudo-polynomial, and these problems are only weakly NP-complete. Problems like 3-SAT and TSP are strongly NP-complete — hard even when all numbers are small — and admit no pseudo-polynomial algorithm unless P=NP. Never quote an O(n·t) bound as proof that you have beaten an NP-complete problem.

7. The empirical fallacy

“I tried hard and couldn't find a polynomial algorithm, so it's NP-complete” is not a proof — it is a confession. Hardness is established only by an explicit reduction from a known NP-complete problem (plus membership). Conversely, do not assume a problem that looks like a hard one inherits its hardness: 2-SAT is in P though 3-SAT is NP-complete; shortest path is easy though longest path is NP-hard. Small changes leap across the boundary, so prove, never presume.

the litmus testBefore claiming a hardness result, answer three questions the definitions settle instantly: Which known NP-complete problem am I reducing FROM, and is it on the LEFT of ≤p? Have I shown my problem is IN NP with a short certificate? Does my reduction preserve the answer in BOTH directions? A “no” or “not sure” to any one of them means the proof is not yet a proof.
12 practice

Problems

1

Prove Vertex Cover is NP-complete

the cleanest reduction · complement trick · classic

A vertex cover of G=(V,E) is a set C⊆V such that every edge has at least one endpoint in C. Decision problem VertexCover: given G and integer k, is there a vertex cover with |C|≤k? Prove it is NP-complete. You may assume IndependentSet (§9) is NP-complete. Hint to discover, not to use blindly: stare at the relationship between an independent set S and the vertices it leaves out, V∖S. (i) Show VertexCover ∈ NP. (ii) Give a polynomial map from an IndependentSet instance (G,k) to a VertexCover instance. (iii) Prove the biconditional. State precisely how the threshold transforms.

2

Shortest Path Visiting All Nodes

cope with hardness · bitmask DP · LeetCode 847

You are given an undirected connected graph of n nodes labelled 0…n−1 as an adjacency list graph, where graph[i] lists i's neighbours. Return the length of the shortest walk that visits every node; you may start and end anywhere and may revisit nodes and edges. Constraints: 1 ≤ n ≤ 12, the graph is connected. Example: graph=[[1,2,3],[0],[0],[0]] → 4 (e.g. 1–0–2–0–3). This is a cousin of the NP-hard Hamiltonian/TSP family — so do not seek a polynomial algorithm. Instead, let the tiny n license an exponential one: identify the right state to track and the exponential factor the bound n≤12 is quietly permitting, and say why a plain BFS over nodes alone fails.

3

Partition Equal Subset Sum

weak NP-completeness · pseudo-poly DP · LeetCode 416

Given an array nums of positive integers, return true iff it can be split into two subsets of equal sum. Constraints: 1 ≤ nums.length ≤ 200, 1 ≤ nums[i] ≤ 100. Examples: [1,5,11,5] → true ({1,5,5} vs {11}); [1,2,3,5] → false. Partition is NP-complete — yet these constraints invite a polynomial-looking dynamic program. Give an O(n·t) algorithm (where t is the half-sum), and then answer the conceptual question in writing: why does an O(n·t) algorithm not prove P=NP? Name the property of t that makes this pseudo-polynomial, and contrast weak vs. strong NP-completeness.

4

Prove Subset Sum is NP-complete (the number gadget)

capstone reduction · 3-SAT → numbers · hard

Decision problem SubsetSum: given a multiset of positive integers A=\{a1,…,ar\} and a target t, is there a sub-multiset summing to exactly t? Prove it is NP-complete. (i) Membership in NP is immediate — state the certificate. (ii) For hardness, reduce from 3-SAT with variables x1…xn and clauses C1…Cm by building decimal numbers with one digit-column per variable and one per clause: two numbers per variable (for its true/false literal) and two slack numbers per clause, with a carefully chosen target. Prove the biconditional, and explain the one numerical hazard the construction must avoid (think about what could happen when columns add). This reduction is the reason Problem 3's pseudo-polynomial DP and this problem's hardness coexist without contradiction — tie the two together in one sentence.

13 recap & next

The one thing to remember

P is what you can solve fast; NP is what you can check fast; and a polynomial reduction A≤pB pours A's difficulty into B. Cook–Levin proved one problem, SAT, is hard enough to encode all of NP — because computation is locally checkable, a run becomes a Boolean formula. Every NP-completeness proof since is the same five-step ritual: show your problem is in NP, then reduce a known hard problem into it and prove the two-way iff. Read the direction correctly, prove both sides, and never mistake pseudo-polynomial for polynomial. When you finish such a proof you have earned the right to stop searching for a perfect algorithm — and that is exactly where the next lecture begins.

Living with Hardness: Approximation and Parameterized Algorithms

If a problem is NP-complete you cannot have fast, exact, and general all at once — so give one up. Approximation algorithms surrender exactness for a provable ratio (a vertex cover at most twice optimal, in linear time); parameterized algorithms surrender generality, confining the exponential blow-up to a small parameter so the problem is tractable whenever that parameter is modest. Lecture 21 turns the wall we built here into a set of doors. The hardness proof was never the end of the story — it is the map that tells you which door to take.
