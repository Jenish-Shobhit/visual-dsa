The previous lecture built a wall. It proved that for a whole continent of problems — routing, scheduling, covering, packing — no polynomial-time algorithm can return the exact optimum on every input, unless P=NP. But the problems do not retreat just because we proved them hard. The trucks still must be routed tomorrow morning. So this lecture is about the doors cut through the wall: how to get a provably good answer fast by relaxing exactly one of the demands we secretly bundled into the word “solve.”

A number to relocate the difficulty. Vertex Cover is NP-complete. Yet a five-line algorithm — pair up edges, take both endpoints — returns, on every graph, a cover never more than twice the smallest possible, in linear time. And if you instead insist on the exact answer but happen to know the cover is small, say k=20, you can find it exactly in about 220 ≈ 106 steps per edge — instant — no matter how large the graph is.

Same NP-complete problem. The hardness did not vanish; it was compartmentalised — pushed into a multiplicative factor of 2 in one approach, and into a function of the single parameter k in the other. That relocation, made rigorous, is the entire subject. Intractability, we will see, is never a property of a problem alone. It is a property of a demand placed on a domain.

01 locate the difficulty before you fight it

The wall, and the three doors

I always begin by separating the problem from the demand we make of it. When we declared a problem “intractable,” we quietly fused four separate requirements into one word. We wanted (1) the exact optimum, (2) on every input, (3) in polynomial time, (4) with a worst-case guarantee. The Cook–Levin theorem and the reductions of the last lecture forbid having (1), (2), and (3) together — assuming P≠NP. They say nothing about any three of the four held while one is released.

That is the whole strategy, and it is a strategy of formalization, not of cleverness. Write the demand down precisely, and you can see which clause is load-bearing for your particular instance — and release exactly that one.

  • Release exactness — keep polynomial time and every input, but accept an answer provably within a factor of optimal. This is the world of approximation algorithms, with their guaranteed ratios.
  • Release input-uniformity — keep the exact optimum, but stop charging the cost to n alone. Identify a parameter k that captures the combinatorial core, and confine the exponential blow-up to k. This is fixed-parameter tractability (FPT).
  • Release the worst-case guarantee — allow randomness or heuristic search, trading a certainty for a high probability. That door is Lecture 22; we name it here only to keep the map honest.

This lecture walks through the first two doors at full depth. They look unrelated — one gives up the right answer, the other keeps it — but they share a single conviction, which is the thesis I will keep returning to: hardness is a statement about a resource demand over a domain, and you defeat it by reshaping the demand, never by wishing the problem easier.

02 objectives & prerequisites

What you will be able to do

  • Define an α-approximation algorithm precisely, and explain the central manoeuvre of the whole field: proving ALG ≤ α·OPT without ever computing OPT, by bounding against a surrogate you can compute.
  • Derive the 2-approximation for Vertex Cover from a maximal matching, and the Hn≈ln n approximation for Set Cover from the greedy charging argument.
  • Distinguish metric TSP (a 2-approx by MST-doubling, 3/2 by Christofides) from general TSP, and reproduce the gap reduction proving general TSP has no constant-factor approximation unless P=NP.
  • Define fixed-parameter tractability — f(k)·nc — and build the bounded-search-tree O(2k·(n+m)) algorithm for Vertex Cover, tracing the branching by hand.
  • Apply kernelization (Buss’s rules) to shrink an instance to a core of size O(k2) independent of n, and state the theorem that FPT and kernelizability are the same thing.
  • Choose the right lever for a given hard problem, and name the limits: APX-hardness (no PTAS) and W[1]-hardness (no FPT) — the far walls that say a relaxation has run out of room.

03 the trick of the whole field

What “approximation” actually means

An approximation algorithm is a polynomial-time algorithm that comes with a proof of how far from optimal it can ever be. The proof is the point. Without it you have a heuristic — a procedure that is often good and sometimes catastrophic, with no way to tell which. With it you have a guarantee that holds on the worst input an adversary can hand you.

Fix the convention for a minimization problem (Vertex Cover, Set Cover, TSP all minimize). Let OPT(I) be the optimum cost on instance I and ALG(I) the cost our algorithm returns.

the definition (minimization, α ≥ 1)
ALG is an α-approximation ⇺ for all I: ALG(I) ≤ α⋅OPT(I) and ALG runs in poly time.

An α of 1 would be exact; the smaller above 1, the better. (For a maximization problem one writes ALG ≥ OPT/α with the same α≥1 — the answer is never worse than a 1/α fraction of the best.) The factor α may be a constant (2, 3/2) or may grow with the input (ln n).

Now the difficulty that makes this an art rather than a definition. To certify ALG ≤ α·OPT we appear to need OPT — but computing OPT is the NP-hard task we are fleeing. We can never put our hands on it. So how can we possibly bound our distance from a number we cannot find?

The resolution is the single most important idea in the design of approximation algorithms, and it is pure formal reasoning. We do not bound against OPT; we bound against a surrogate L — a quantity we can compute in polynomial time, for which we can prove a one-sided relationship to OPT. For minimization we find a computable lower bound L ≤ OPT. Then we show our algorithm is not too far above that same L. The two inequalities chain, and OPT — forever unknown — is trapped between them.

the lower-bound sandwich — the skeleton of every proof below
ALG ≤ α⋅L and L≤OPT ⟹ ALG≤α⋅L≤α⋅OPT

Read it right to left and the trick disappears into obviousness: if our answer is within a factor α of something no larger than the optimum, it is within α of the optimum. The craft of approximation is almost entirely the craft of finding the right computable lower bound. For Vertex Cover it will be the size of a matching; for Set Cover, a pigeonhole count; for metric TSP, the minimum spanning tree. Each is a structure from earlier in this course, pressed into service as a certificate.

the formal lensThis is reasoning about an object you cannot construct, via a bound you can. It is exactly the discipline of the engine: you do not need to enumerate the answer to certify a property of it. Hold that thought — it is what separates an approximation algorithm from a hopeful heuristic.
04 the cleanest guarantee in the subject

Vertex Cover: a provable 2-approximation

Fix the object. Given an undirected graph G=(V,E), a vertex cover is a set S⊆V such that every edge has at least one endpoint in S — every edge is “watched” by a chosen vertex. The minimum vertex cover is the smallest such S. Deciding whether one of size ≤k exists is NP-complete; finding the minimum is NP-hard. We will return a cover guaranteed to be at most twice the minimum, and we will do it in the time it takes to read the edges.

The algorithm is almost insultingly simple, and that simplicity is precisely where its proof lives. Recall from the MST lecture the idea of a matching: a set of edges no two of which share a vertex. A matching is maximal if you cannot add another edge without a collision. Here is the whole method.

def vertex_cover_2approx(V, E):       # E = list of edges (u, v)
    matched = set()                   # vertices already touched by the matching
    cover   = set()
    for (u, v) in E:                  # any fixed order of edges is fine
        if u not in matched and v not in matched:
            cover.add(u); cover.add(v)      # take BOTH endpoints
            matched.add(u); matched.add(v)  # edge (u,v) joins the matching M
    return cover                    # |cover| = 2|M| ≤ 2·OPT

Scan the edges once. Whenever you meet an edge whose endpoints are both still untouched, grab the edge into a matching M and put both its endpoints into the cover. That is all. Two claims make it a theorem.

Claim 1 — the output is a valid cover

Suppose not: some edge e=(x,y) has neither endpoint in our cover. Then neither x nor y was ever matched (the cover is exactly the matched vertices). But then, when our scan reached e, both endpoints were free — so we would have added e to M. Contradiction. Every edge is covered. The matching being maximal is the same statement as the output being a cover.

Claim 2 — the ratio is 2, via the right lower bound

Here is the surrogate. The edges of M are pairwise vertex-disjoint by construction. To cover edge e∈M, any vertex cover — including the optimal one — must contain at least one of e’s two endpoints. Because the |M| edges share no vertices, these required vertices are all distinct. Therefore every vertex cover has at least |M| vertices:

the computable lower bound L = |M|
OPT≥|M| while ALG=|cover|=2|M| ⟹ ALG=2|M|≤2⋅OPT

That is the sandwich of §03 made concrete: L=|M| is computed in linear time, it provably sits below OPT, and our output is exactly 2L. The factor 2 is not a hope; it is forced by disjointness. Notice we never found the minimum cover; we bounded it from below with a matching and never looked back.

Take the seven-vertex graph below. Scan edges in the order a–b, c–d, e–f, b–c, d–e, d–g, f–g.

complexityOne linear scan of the edges with constant-time membership tests: O(n+m) time, O(n) space. No sorting, no priority queue, no recursion. The cheapest non-trivial guarantee in the entire course, and it is dead tight.

Is 2 the best constant known? Essentially yes, and that is itself a deep fact we revisit in §09: no polynomial algorithm achieves ratio 1.36 unless P=NP, and none beats 2−ε under a stronger conjecture (the Unique Games Conjecture). The five-line matching algorithm is, astonishingly, near the frontier.

05 greedy, and exactly how good it is

Set Cover and the ln n ceiling

Generalise the covering idea. In Set Cover you are given a universe U of n elements and a family of subsets S1,…,Sm⊆U; choose the fewest subsets whose union is all of U. (Vertex Cover is the special case where each element is an edge and each vertex contributes the set of edges it touches.) It is the archetype of covering, scheduling, and committee-selection problems, and it is NP-hard.

The natural algorithm is greedy, in the same spirit as the MST: at each step take the locally best move — the set that covers the most still-uncovered elements — and repeat until nothing is left.

def set_cover_greedy(U, sets):        # U = universe, sets = list of subsets
    uncovered = set(U)
    chosen    = []
    while uncovered:
        best = max(sets, key=lambda S: len(S & uncovered))  # most NEW elements
        chosen.append(best)
        uncovered -= best                  # ≥ |uncovered|/OPT removed this step
    return chosen                       # |chosen| ≤ H_n · OPT,  H_n ≈ ln n

Unlike the MST, greed here is not exactly optimal — Set Cover is NP-hard, so no efficient method can be. But greed is provably close, and the proof is a beautiful charging argument that pins the ratio to the harmonic number Hn=1+\tfrac12+\cdots+\tfrac1n≈ln n.

The charging argument

Let OPT=k (the optimal number of sets — unknown, but we only reason about it). Distribute a unit of cost. Each time greedy picks a set that covers t new elements, charge a price of 1/t to each of those t newly covered elements. Then the total price summed over all elements equals the number of sets greedy chose — each chosen set contributes t·(1/t)=1:

total price = number of greedy sets
|Greedy| = ∑e∈U price(e)

Now bound each element’s price using the optimum as a yardstick. Order the elements e1,e2,…,en in the order greedy covers them. Look at the moment just before ei is covered: every element ei,ei+1,…,en is still uncovered, so at least n−i+1 elements remain. Those remaining elements are covered by the k optimal sets, so by pigeonhole one optimal set covers at least a 1/k fraction of them — at least (n−i+1)/k. Greedy takes the set with the most new coverage, so the set it picks now covers at least that many, and the price charged to ei is therefore at most k/(n−i+1). Sum it:

the harmonic bound
|Greedy| = ∑i=1n price(ei) ≤ ∑i=1n kn−i+1 = k∑j=1n1j = k⋅Hn = Hn⋅OPT

So greedy Set Cover is an Hn-approximation, roughly ln n. A quick trace: universe {1..12} with sets A=\{1..6\}, B=\{5..10\}, C=\{1,2,3,11\}, D=\{4,8,12\}, E=\{9,10,11,12\}. Greedy takes A (6 new), then B (4 new: 7,8,9,10), then E (2 new: 11,12), then D covers the last one... checking, after A,B,E only 4 is left, and D covers it — four sets. The reasoning never needed the true optimum; it only compared greedy’s coverage to whatever the optimum’s must have been.

And the ceiling is real. In 2014 Dinur and Steurer proved — sharpening Feige’s 1998 result through the PCP machinery — that no polynomial algorithm approximates Set Cover to better than (1−o(1))ln n unless P=NP. The dumb greedy is, up to lower-order terms, the best any polynomial algorithm will ever do. That is a rare and bracing thing: a one-line algorithm sitting exactly on a proven wall.

We cannot compute the optimum — so we trap it between a bound we can compute and an answer we can build.
06 the same problem, two universes

The Traveling Salesman: when the metric saves you

The Traveling Salesman Problem (TSP) asks for the cheapest tour that visits every one of n cities exactly once and returns home. It is the folk hero of intractability. But TSP is really two problems wearing one name, and the boundary between them is the single most instructive lesson in approximability: whether the costs satisfy the triangle inequality.

General TSP has no approximation at all

If edge costs are arbitrary, TSP cannot be approximated to any constant factor — indeed any polynomial factor α(n) — unless P=NP. The proof is a gap reduction from Hamiltonian Cycle, and it is worth seeing because it is the template for proving a problem inapproximable.

Given a graph G on n vertices (we want to know if it has a Hamiltonian cycle), build a complete graph on the same vertices and weight its edges: cost 1 if the edge is in G, and a huge cost B=α·n+1 if it is not. Now look at the optimal tour:

  • If G has a Hamiltonian cycle, that cycle is a tour using only weight-1 edges: OPT=n.
  • If G has none, every tour must use at least one heavy edge: OPT ≥ (n−1)+B > α·n.

The two cases are separated by a factor of more than α. An α-approximation, fed the “yes” instance, would have to return a tour of cost ≤α·n; fed the “no” instance it could not, because there even the optimum exceeds α·n. So the approximation’s output alone would tell Hamiltonicity apart — solving an NP-complete problem in polynomial time. Hence no such approximation exists unless P=NP. The gap in the construction is the whole engine: stretch “yes” and “no” instances apart by more than α, and an α-approximation becomes an exact decider.

Metric TSP: the triangle inequality changes everything

Now add one assumption, almost always true in practice (road distances, Euclidean distances, latencies): the triangle inequality c(x,z)≤c(x,y)+c(y,z) — a direct hop is never worse than a detour. This is metric TSP, and it admits clean constant-factor approximations built from a structure you already own: the minimum spanning tree.

The lower bound is immediate and is, again, the sandwich. Take any tour and delete one edge: what remains is a spanning path, which is in particular a spanning tree, so its cost is at least the MST’s. Therefore MST ≤ OPT — the minimum spanning tree is a free certificate below the optimum tour.

the TSP lower bound
MST≤(optimal tour minus one edge)≤OPT

Worked example — the MST-doubling 2-approximation

Build the MST T. Double every edge of T: now every vertex has even degree, so the multigraph has an Euler tour — a closed walk using each (doubled) edge once — of cost exactly 2·MST. That walk visits some vertices more than once. Walk it, and whenever it would revisit a city, shortcut straight to the next unvisited one. By the triangle inequality each shortcut only shortens the walk. The result is a genuine tour (each city once) of cost ≤2·MST≤2·OPT.

Christofides–Serdyukov: 3/2 with one clever patch

Doubling is wasteful: it makes every vertex even when only the odd-degree ones in T actually need fixing. The number of odd-degree vertices in any tree is even (the handshake lemma: degrees sum to twice the edge count, so odds come in pairs). Let O be that even-sized set. Instead of doubling, add a minimum-weight perfect matching M on O alone. Then every vertex of T∪M has even degree — Eulerian again — and we Euler-tour and shortcut as before, for cost ≤MST+M.

The patch is cheap. Walk the optimal tour and look only at the vertices of O in the order it visits them: shortcutting (triangle inequality) gives a closed tour on O of cost ≤OPT. Since |O| is even, that cycle splits into two perfect matchings on O (alternate edges); the cheaper one costs ≤OPT/2, and the minimum matching is no costlier still. So M≤OPT/2 and the tour costs ≤MST+M≤OPT+OPT/2=\tfrac32 OPT.

Christofides (1976) held the record for forty-five years. Only in 2020–21 did Karlin, Klein, and Oveis Gharan break 3/2 — by a famously microscopic ε≈10−36, via a randomized variant. That a constant so small was front-page news tells you how hard the wall is to move.

the formal lensThe general-vs-metric split is the lesson: approximability is not a property of TSP, but of TSP-with-its-cost-structure. Add the triangle inequality and a problem with no approximation acquires a 3/2 one. The structure you assume is the resource you spend.
07 the second door: keep the exact answer

Parameterized algorithms: confine the blow-up to k

Approximation gave up the exact answer to keep polynomial time. The second door keeps the exact answer and gives up something else: the insistence that runtime be measured by n alone. Many hard instances are large but structurally small — the network has millions of nodes but the cover we seek has twenty; the formula has thousands of clauses but a handful of variables decide everything. Parameterized complexity makes that intuition formal.

Pick a second measurement k — a parameter — that captures the combinatorial core: the solution size, the treewidth, the number of distinct values. A problem is fixed-parameter tractable (FPT) if it can be solved in time

the FPT shape — the explosion is sealed inside f(k)
f(k) ⋅ nc for some computable f  and constant c independent of k.

The function f may be as savage as 2k or k! — that is allowed. What is not allowed is for k to climb into the exponent of n. The contrast is everything: 2k·n is FPT and runs in a blink when k=20 and n=106; nk is not FPT — for the same k,n it is (106)20=10120, hopeless. The difference between “k multiplies” and “k exponentiates n” is the difference between tractable and not.

Vertex Cover, parameterized by k — the bounded search tree

Decision form: does G have a vertex cover of size ≤k? The engine is a single structural observation we already used: for any edge (u,v), every cover must contain u or v. So pick any uncovered edge and branch on that binary choice — try u in the cover, else try v — each branch spending one unit of the budget k.

def vc_at_most_k(G, k):               # return a cover of size ≤k, or None
    if not G.edges:  return []        # no edges left -> empty cover suffices
    if k == 0:        return None      # edges remain but budget spent -> fail
    (u, v) = next(iter(G.edges))      # ANY remaining edge
    take_u = vc_at_most_k(G.remove(u), k - 1)   # branch 1: u in cover
    if take_u is not None: return [u] + take_u
    take_v = vc_at_most_k(G.remove(v), k - 1)   # branch 2: v in cover
    if take_v is not None: return [v] + take_v
    return None                        # neither worked -> no size-k cover

The recursion is a binary tree. Each node spends one of the k budget units, so the tree has depth at most k; with two children per node that is at most 2k leaves, and each node does O(n+m) work removing a vertex and its incident edges. Total: O(2k·(n+m)) — textbook FPT. The exponential is real but caged inside 2k; the dependence on the graph size is linear.

A worked trace on Figure 2’s graph with k=3: pick edge a–b. Branch a∈C — remove a, budget 2; the remaining edges still force b–c,d–e,…, and this branch will need four vertices, so it dead-ends. Backtrack to b∈C — remove b (kills a–b and b–c), budget 2; pick c–d, branch d∈C (kills c–d,d–e,d–g), budget 1; pick e–f, branch f∈C (kills e–f,f–g), budget 0, no edges remain — success with {b,d,f}. The tree found the exact optimum the 2-approximation could only bound.

refinementSmarter branching shrinks the base of the exponent. Branch on a maximum-degree vertex v: either v is in the cover, or all its neighbours are — a lopsided split that drives the running time down toward 1.2738k in the best known analyses. The shape f(k)·poly(n) never changes; only f improves.
08 shrink the instance before you fight it

Kernelization: a core of size f(k)

The second FPT technique is, to my taste, the more beautiful, because it is pure preprocessing with a proof attached. Kernelization is a polynomial-time procedure that transforms an instance (G,k) into a smaller instance (G',k') — the kernel — whose size depends only on k, not on n, and which has a solution if and only if the original does. You boil a galaxy-sized graph down to a puddle whose size is bounded by the parameter, then solve the puddle by brute force.

For Vertex Cover, two reduction rules (Buss’s kernel) do it. Each rule is a small theorem.

  • High-degree rule. If a vertex v has degree >k, it must be in any cover of size ≤k. Why: if you left v out, you would need all >k of its neighbours to cover v’s edges — already over budget. So put v in the cover, delete it, and decrement k.
  • Isolated-vertex rule. A vertex with no edges covers nothing; delete it. It cannot help and cannot hurt.

Apply both until neither fires. Now every surviving vertex has degree ≤k. Here is the counting punchline: a cover of size k, each of whose vertices touches ≤k edges, can cover at most k·k=k2 edges. So if more than k2 edges remain, answer NO immediately — no size-k cover can exist. Otherwise at most k2 edges remain, touching at most 2k2 vertices.

the kernel size — independent of n
edges≤k2 vertices≤2k2 ⟹ kernel of size O(k2)

A graph with a billion vertices and a small cover collapses, in linear time, to a residual graph of at most 2k2 vertices. On that kernel you can run the 2k search tree of §07 — now with n≤2k2 — or even brute force, and lift the answer back. The two techniques compose: kernelize to strip the instance down to its core, then branch on the core.

The kernelization theorem. A parameterized problem is fixed-parameter tractable if and only if it admits a kernel (a polynomial-time reduction to an instance of size bounded by some computable function of k).

One direction is easy — kernelize, then brute-force the bounded kernel, and the total is f(k)+poly(n), which is FPT. The other direction is the surprise: any FPT algorithm can be turned into a kernelization. “Tractable by a parameter” and “compressible to that parameter” are the same property, seen from two sides.

Vertex Cover even admits a 2k-vertex kernel via a linear-programming relaxation — smaller still — but Buss’s quadratic kernel already shows the shape of the idea: hardness made finite by squeezing it into k.

09 every door opens onto another wall

The far wall: how good can “good enough” get?

Honesty requires the second wall. Relaxing a demand buys room, but not infinite room — each lever has its own intractability theory that says “this far and no farther.”

For approximation: PTAS, APX-hardness, and the no-free-lunch line

The best possible ratio differs sharply by problem. Some admit a PTAS (Polynomial-Time Approximation Scheme): for any ε>0 a (1+ε)-approximation in time polynomial in n — you dial the error as close to zero as you like. Knapsack does even better, an FPTAS, polynomial in both n and 1/ε, by dynamic programming over rounded profit values. But others are APX-hard: there is a constant below which no polynomial approximation can go unless P=NP — no PTAS exists. Vertex Cover and metric TSP are in this camp; Set Cover’s ln n is provably its floor. The PCP theorem is the machine behind these lower bounds: it manufactures exactly the kind of gap we built by hand for general TSP.

For parameterization: W[1]-hardness

Not every problem yields to a parameter. Parameterize Independent Set or Clique by the solution size k and the best known algorithm is still essentially nk — XP, not FPT. These problems are W[1]-hard, the parameterized analogue of NP-hardness: a reduction theory (preserving the parameter) shows that an FPT algorithm for any W[1]-hard problem would collapse the whole W-hierarchy, believed impossible. So “choose a parameter” is not a universal escape — it works precisely when the parameter genuinely controls the combinatorial explosion. Vertex Cover’s k does; Clique’s k does not, even though the two problems are reductions of each other classically. The parameter, not just the problem, decides tractability.

the deep symmetryEach relaxation regenerates the same drama one level up. Classical complexity has P vs NP; approximation has PTAS vs APX-hard; parameterization has FPT vs W[1]-hard. The wall is never destroyed — it is moved, and made to enclose a smaller, more honest territory. That is what “living with hardness” actually means.
10 where it breaks

Failure modes & common mistakes

These techniques fail quietly — they return a number, and a wrong belief about that number is worse than a crash. Every trap below is something the formal lens flags early: an inequality pointed the wrong way, an assumption silently dropped, a parameter that does no work.

The seductive greedy that is not a 2-approximation

The “obvious” Vertex Cover heuristic — repeatedly take the highest-degree vertex — feels smarter than blindly matching edges. It is worse. Its approximation ratio is Θ(log n), not 2: there are bipartite graphs where the high-degree rule is lured into picking about OPT·ln n vertices while the humble matching algorithm still guarantees 2·OPT. The lesson is exact: a guarantee comes from a proof against a lower bound, not from a plausible-sounding rule. Maximum-degree greed has no matching certificate, so it has no constant ratio.

Proving the ratio with OPT in hand

The commonest analysis error is to write “ALG≤2·OPT because each chosen vertex corresponds to an optimal one” — quietly assuming knowledge of OPT. You cannot compute OPT; if your proof references it except through a computable lower bound, the proof is circular. Always exhibit the surrogate L (the matching, the MST, the pigeonhole count) and bound both sides against it. And keep the inequality oriented: for minimization it is ALG≤α·OPT with α≥1; flip it and you have claimed your answer beats the optimum.

Applying metric guarantees to a non-metric instance

MST-doubling and Christofides are load-bearing on the triangle inequality — every shortcut step invokes it. Hand them a graph with arbitrary costs (penalties, one-way surcharges, costs that violate c(x,z)≤c(x,y)+c(y,z)) and the ratio evaporates; you are back in general TSP, where no constant factor is possible. Before you quote “3/2,” verify the metric. If asymmetric (c(x,y)≠c(y,x)), Christofides does not even apply — that is a different, harder world.

Mistaking nk for FPT

“Try all k-subsets” runs in \binom{n}{k}=Θ(nk) — this is XP, not FPT, and it is genuinely intractable for, say, k=15, n=104. FPT demands the parameter out of n’s exponent: 2k·n, not nk. If your “parameterized” algorithm has k in the exponent of n, you have not parameterized anything — and for W[1]-hard problems like Clique, nk is essentially the best you can hope for.

A reduction rule that changes the answer

Kernelization rules must be sound: (G,k) is a YES-instance iff the reduced (G',k') is. It is easy to write a plausible rule that quietly alters the optimum (e.g. deleting a degree-1 vertex without taking its neighbour, or forgetting to decrement k when you commit a forced vertex). Each rule needs the little theorem attached, exactly as Buss’s two rules carry theirs. An unsound kernel is not a faster algorithm; it is a wrong one.

Promising a ratio the problem forbids

Do not offer a PTAS for an APX-hard problem. If a stakeholder asks for “within 1% of optimal, in polynomial time” on Vertex Cover or metric TSP, the honest answer is that no such algorithm can exist unless P=NP — 2 and 3/2 are near the floor. Knowing which problems admit a PTAS (Euclidean TSP does; metric TSP does not) is part of choosing the lever responsibly.

Vertex Cover, within twice optimal

approximation · the matching lower bound · constructive

You are given an undirected graph as n (vertices 0…n−1) and an edge list edges, with up to 105 edges. Return any vertex cover whose size is at most twice the minimum possible, in O(n+m) time. Your answer is accepted if it is a valid cover (every edge has a chosen endpoint) and its size is ≤2·OPT. Example: n=4, edges=[[0,1],[1,2],[2,3]] → e.g. [1,2] (size 2; here OPT=2). In your write-up, state the computable lower bound that certifies the factor of 2 without computing OPT, and prove your output meets it. Then exhibit a small graph on which your algorithm actually returns 2·OPT.

Smallest Sufficient Team

set cover, made exact by a tiny parameter · bitmask DP · LeetCode 1125

Given a list of req_skills (at most 16) and a list of people where people[i] is the set of skills person i has, return a smallest team (indices of people) that together covers every required skill. Any one valid smallest team is accepted. Constraints: 1≤req_skills.length≤16, 1≤people.length≤60. Example: req_skills=["java","nodejs","reactjs"], people=[["java"],["nodejs"],["nodejs","reactjs"]] → [0,2]. This is Set Cover — the problem §05 could only approximate by ln n greedy. So which parameter here lets you solve it exactly, and why does that make the natural exponential a fixed-parameter one rather than an nk one? Make the state space precise and bound it.

Shortest Path Visiting All Nodes

TSP-shaped, exact for small n · Held–Karp bitmask DP · LeetCode 847

Given an undirected, connected graph as an adjacency list graph with n nodes (1≤n≤12), return the length of the shortest walk that visits every node; you may revisit nodes and reuse edges, and may start and end anywhere. Example: graph=[[1,2,3],[0],[0],[0]] → 4. This is the visit-everything core of TSP. Identify the parameter that makes an exact exponential algorithm acceptable here, and design a state of the form “(set of visited nodes, current node).” State the number of states and the transition cost, and explain why the result is 2n·poly(n) — FPT in n — rather than the naive n!.

Vertex Cover of size at most k

the FPT door on the same problem · bounded search tree + kernel · decision

Given a graph (n up to 105 vertices, m up to 105 edges) and an integer k≤30, decide whether a vertex cover of size ≤k exists and return one if so — exactly, in O(2k·(n+m)) or better. Example: the 4-cycle edges=[[0,1],[1,2],[2,3],[3,0]] with k=2 → true (e.g. [0,2]); with k=1 → false. This is Problem 1’s graph met through the second door. Specify the branching rule and prove the 2k leaf bound; then add the two Buss reduction rules and argue your instance shrinks to a kernel of O(k2) edges first. Why is this fast for k=30 and n=105 even though Vertex Cover is NP-complete?

12 recap & next

The one thing to remember

NP-hardness forbids exact + every-input + polynomial all at once — so release exactly one and prove a guarantee on what remains. Approximation releases exactness: bound your output against a computable lower bound (a matching, the MST, a pigeonhole count), and the unknown optimum is trapped — Vertex Cover at 2, Set Cover at ln n, metric TSP at 3/2. Parameterization releases input-uniformity: confine the explosion to a parameter k with f(k)·nc — branch in a depth-k tree, or kernelize to a core of size f(k). And always know the far wall: APX-hardness and W[1]-hardness say how much room a lever truly has. Hardness is never destroyed; it is relocated into a factor or a parameter you can afford.
