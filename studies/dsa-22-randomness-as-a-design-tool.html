A balanced search tree is a marvel of bookkeeping — colours, rotations, invariants restored after every insert, hundreds of lines that exist only to defeat an adversary who hands you keys in the worst possible order. This study asks a quieter, stranger question: what if you simply refused to let the adversary choose the order? Not by sorting, not by checking — by injecting your own randomness, so deep into the structure that the worst case has nowhere left to hide.

A coin in place of a thousand lines. A treap is a binary search tree that does no balancing at all. You hand each key a random number and insist the tree obey the heap property on those numbers. That single rule — no rotations chosen by you, no cases, no colours — forces the tree's expected height to O(log n) on every input. A skip list reaches the same guarantee with nothing but a stack of linked lists and a coin per node.

The adversary still picks the keys. But the keys no longer pick the shape — your coins do, and the adversary cannot see them. That relocation of uncertainty, from the input you cannot control to the coins they cannot read, is the entire subject. By the end you will have built four structures on it and proved, each time, that “probably” is a guarantee you can bank.

01 move the uncertainty you cannot remove

The adversary, and the coin

I want to begin where I always do — by writing the guarantee down precisely, because the shape of the quantifier is the whole story. A deterministic data structure promises something of the form:

the deterministic contract
for all inputs I: cost(I) ≤f(n)

The universal quantifier ∀I is generous — and it is exactly the handle an adversary grips. A balanced BST spends its complexity buying that ∀: it must restore an invariant after every operation precisely because the next key could be the one that unbalances it. The cost of a worst-case guarantee is the machinery that anticipates the worst case.

Randomization performs a substitution that looks like sleight of hand and is in fact a change in the logical structure of the claim. We introduce a private source of bits — coin flips the algorithm makes and the input never sees — and the contract becomes:

the randomized contract
for all inputs I: 𝔼r[ cost(I,r) ]≤f(n)

The adversary still chooses I — the worst input it likes — but the expectation is taken over r, our randomness, which is sealed off from its choice. There is no input that is “bad for the coins,” because the coins are flipped after the input is fixed and independently of it. The worst case has not been beaten by cleverness; it has been made unreachable by quantifier order. This is the formal heart of the whole study, and it is worth a slogan.

“A deterministic structure fears the worst input. A randomized one has no worst input — only, occasionally, an unlucky run.”

Notice what this costs and what it buys. It buys radical simplicity: a treap has no balance cases because it delegates balance to chance; a skip list has no rotations because the same coin that builds it keeps it shallow. It costs certainty: the guarantee is now in expectation, or with high probability, and we owe a proof — a concentration bound — that the unlucky runs are rare enough to ignore. The four structures ahead are four answers to one design question: where do I inject the randomness, and which inequality pays the debt?

O(log n)expected skip-list / treap search — with no balancing logic at all
≈10bits per item for a 1% Bloom false-positive — whatever the keys are
0false negatives: the Bloom filter’s error is strictly one-sided
2⁄n²that one run of Karger finds a global min cut — then amplify
02 objectives & prerequisites

What you will be able to do

  • State the Las Vegas / Monte Carlo distinction precisely — which of {correctness, running time} is fixed and which is the random variable — and convert between them.
  • Build a skip list from a plain sorted list, and prove via the backward-walk recurrence that its expected search cost is O(log n), with a union-bound argument that its height is O(log n) with high probability.
  • Define a treap, prove the priorities determine a unique tree, and recognise that its shape is the recursion tree of randomized quicksort — inheriting O(log n) expected depth from an analysis you already own.
  • Derive the Bloom-filter false-positive rate (1-e−kn/m)k, find the optimal number of hashes k=(m/n) ln 2, and explain why the error is strictly one-sided.
  • Run Karger’s contraction algorithm, prove a fixed min cut survives with probability ≥2/(n(n-1)), and amplify a 2/n² success into high probability — and read off the corollary that a graph has at most C(n, 2) distinct min cuts.
  • Choose the right concentration inequality — Markov, Chebyshev, Chernoff, union bound — to turn each “probably” into a deployable guarantee.

03 two contracts, and how to trade between them

Las Vegas vs Monte Carlo

Every randomized algorithm fixes one of two things and lets the other wobble. The discipline is to know, always, which.

  • Las Vegas. The output is always correct; the running time is the random variable. You bound it in expectation (or with high probability) — “never wrong, sometimes slow.” Randomized quicksort, skip lists, and treaps are Las Vegas: whatever the coins say, a search returns the right answer; only its length varies.
  • Monte Carlo. The running time is fixed; the output is correct only with some probability ≥p — “always fast, sometimes wrong.” Bloom filters and Karger’s algorithm are Monte Carlo. Their error is one-sided, which is the kind worth having: a Bloom filter never misses a member it stored, and Karger never reports a cut smaller than one that truly exists.

Trading one for the other

The two contracts are convertible, and the conversions are pure formal moves — no new idea, just Markov’s inequality in one direction and a verifier in the other.

Las Vegas → Monte Carlo, by Markov
𝔼[T]=μ ⟹ Pr[T>2μ] ≤12

Run the Las Vegas algorithm for a hard budget of 2µ steps; if it has not finished, halt and output “don’t know.” The time is now fixed and the failure probability is at most ½ — a Monte Carlo algorithm. Conversely, a Monte Carlo algorithm whose answer you can check in polynomial time becomes Las Vegas by re-running until the check passes; the expected number of trials is 1/p.

Amplification — the lever that makes “probably” into “certainly enough”

This is the single most important manoeuvre in Monte Carlo design, and it is one line. If one trial succeeds independently with probability ≥p, then t independent trials all fail with probability

amplification
Pr[all t fail] ≤(1−p)t ≤e−pt ⟹ take t= 1pln1δ ⇒ failure ≤δ

Using 1-p≤e−p: the failure probability decays exponentially in the number of trials, so a wildly unreliable single run — even one that succeeds only 2/n² of the time, as Karger’s will — becomes a near-certainty for a polynomial number of repetitions. Keep this formula in view; we cash it twice before the study is out.

04 a sorted dictionary built from coins

Skip lists: shortcuts you never have to maintain

Let me build this one from the ground up, because the construction is the proof. Start with the humblest sorted structure: a singly linked list in increasing order. Search is honest and slow — you walk one node at a time, O(n) — because a linked list offers no way to skip.

So give it one. Lay a second list over the first containing, say, every other element, each express node carrying a down pointer to its twin below. To search, ride the express lane rightward until the next express key would overshoot your target, then drop down and finish on the local lane. With every-other promotion you halve the walk. Stack log₂n such lanes — each skipping twice as far as the one below — and search becomes O(log n). That is the dream structure on the left of your imagination: a perfect binary scaffold of shortcuts.

And it is a nightmare to maintain. Insert one element near the front and “every other element” is violated all the way up; you are back to rebalancing, the very bookkeeping we set out to escape. Here is the move — the whole idea — and it is pure Bauhaus: stop enforcing the levels and let a coin assign them.

The skip-list rule. When a key is inserted, flip a fair coin repeatedly. Its tower rises one extra level for each head before the first tail. So a node reaches level i or higher with probability 2−i, independently of every other node. No coordination, no global invariant — each node privately decides how tall it is, and the structure that emerges is, in expectation, the balanced scaffold we wanted.

Worked search, move by move

Read the red path against the rule “at each level, step right while the next key is < target; otherwise drop.” Searching for 71:

  1. L3, at head. Next key on L3 is 71; it is not < 71, so do not step — drop to L2.
  2. L2, at head. Next is 31 < 71 → step right to 31. Next on L2 is 71, not <71 → drop to L1.
  3. L1, at 31. Next is 50 < 71 → step right to 50. Next is 71, not <71 → drop to L0.
  4. L0, at 50. Next is 71. Stop dropping — we are at the bottom — and check: next.key == 71. Found.

Six pointer moves where the flat list would have made eight hops — and the gap widens to n-versus-log n as the structure grows. The implementation is almost suspiciously short:

def search(self, target):
    node = self.head
    for level in range(self.top, -1, -1):   # top lane down to level 0
        while node.next[level] and node.next[level].key < target:
            node = node.next[level]              # ride this express lane
    node = node.next[0]                          # the first node ≥ target on L0
    return node is not None and node.key == target

def random_level(self):
    lvl = 0
    while lvl < self.max_level and random.random() < 0.5:
        lvl += 1                              # geometric: P(height > i) = 2**-(i+1)
    return lvl

Insertion runs the same search but remembers the drop points — the predecessor at each level — then splices the new tower in at those points up to its random height. Deletion unlinks at each level. Both inherit the search’s cost. So everything reduces to one question.

Why the expected search is O(log n) — the backward walk

Here is the argument I find genuinely beautiful, due to Pugh, because it analyses the path in reverse and the recurrence falls out in two lines. Imagine retracing the search from its endpoint back toward the head, climbing up and stepping left. At any node on the reverse path, ask: did the forward search arrive here by dropping (so this node is taller, and we now climb) or by stepping right (so we now move left)? By the coin rule those are each probability ½, independent of the past. Let C(k) be the expected number of reverse steps needed to climb k levels:

the backward-walk recurrence
C(k) =1 +12C(k−1) +12C(k) ⟹ C(k)=2+C(k−1) =2k

Each level costs an expected two steps. The number of levels is itself O(log n) — which I will pin down now — so the expected search length is 2·O(log n)=O(log n). For the height, a union bound over the n nodes is all it takes:

height is O(log n) with high probability
Pr[some node reaches level clog2n] ≤n⋅2−clog2n =n⋅n−c =n1−c

Choose c=2 and the probability of any tower exceeding 2log₂n is at most 1/n — vanishing. The structure is shallow not because we forced it but because tall towers are exponentially rare and there are only n chances to draw one. Space is O(n) in expectation: the towers contain n·∑i 2−i≤2n nodes. A balanced BST’s guarantees, then, for a coin and a stack of lists.

05 a tree that balances itself by accident

Treaps: a BST in the keys, a heap in the coins

The skip list abandoned the tree. The treap keeps it — and removes the balancing logic anyway, by a trick of superb economy. Give every key x a second number, a priority p(x) drawn uniformly at random (a real in [0,1], or a random 64-bit integer). Then demand the structure satisfy two orders at once:

  • a binary search tree on the keys — in-order traversal yields the keys sorted (left < node < right);
  • a max-heap on the priorities — every node’s priority exceeds its children’s.

The name is the portmanteau: tree + heap = treap. And the first thing to prove is that these two demands do not fight — they pin down exactly one tree.

Uniqueness theorem. For a set of items with distinct keys and distinct priorities, there is exactly one treap. Proof, by the structure itself: the item of highest priority must be the root (heap property). Its key partitions the remaining items into those smaller (which must form the left subtree, by the BST property) and those larger (the right subtree). Recurse on each part. At every step the choice is forced — so the tree is determined entirely by the priorities. There is nothing left to decide, and therefore nothing to balance.

The gem: a treap is randomized quicksort, frozen

Now the observation that makes the analysis free. Build the treap by the recursive rule above: highest-priority item becomes the root, splitting the rest into smaller/larger, recurse. But the highest-priority item among a set of iid-random priorities is a uniformly random element of that set. So the root is a uniformly random key; it partitions the rest; each side recurses with a fresh uniformly random pivot. That is exactly randomized quicksort — and the treap’s shape is precisely quicksort’s recursion tree. Equivalently: inserting the keys in decreasing priority order into an ordinary BST is the same as inserting a uniformly random permutation, whose expected depth we already computed in Deep study 5.

So I do not need a new proof; I collect an old one. Let the keys have ranks 1,…,n. Define the indicator that key i is an ancestor of key j. Among the keys whose ranks lie between i and j (inclusive — that is |i-j|+1 of them), i is an ancestor of j exactly when i has the highest priority of the bunch — each equally likely:

expected depth, straight from the quicksort analysis
Pr[i anc. j] =1|i−j|+1 ⟹ 𝔼[depth(i)] =∑j≠i 1|i−j|+1 =Hi+Hn+1−i−2 <2lnn

Every key sits at expected depth below 2 ln n, so every operation is O(log n) expected — on any input, because the input never touched the priorities. The unlucky deep tree is possible, just exponentially improbable.

Operations: rotate to restore the heap, or split & merge

There are two equivalent implementations, and both are short. Rotation style: to insert, do an ordinary BST insert by key at a leaf, then rotate the new node upward as long as its priority exceeds its parent’s — a heap sift-up performed with BST-preserving rotations. To delete, rotate the doomed node down (toward its higher-priority child) until it is a leaf, then snip. The expected number of rotations per operation is O(1).

Split/merge style is even cleaner and is the one I prefer to teach, because two primitives generate everything:

# split T into (L, R): keys < key go left, keys ≥ key go right
def split(t, key):
    if t is None: return (None, None)
    if t.key < key:
        l, r = split(t.right, key); t.right = l; return (t, r)
    else:
        l, r = split(t.left, key);  t.left  = r; return (l, t)

# merge L and R, assuming every key in L < every key in R
def merge(a, b):
    if a is None or b is None: return a or b
    if a.prio > b.prio:                 # higher priority stays on top (heap)
        a.right = merge(a.right, b); return a
    else:
        b.left  = merge(a, b.left);  return b

Insert x: split by x, then merge(L, node(x), R). Delete x: split it out and merge the remainders. Each touches a single root-to-leaf path, so each is O(log n) expected. Add a size field to every node and the same tree answers order-statistic queries — the “k-th smallest” — in O(log n); that is exactly Practice 2.

aside — the same coin, applied to positionIf you key a treap on array index instead of value (an “implicit treap,” with subtree sizes standing in for positions), split and merge give you an array with O(log n) insert, delete, and even reverse at arbitrary positions — a rope. One randomized idea, two data structures.
06 set membership in a handful of bits

Bloom filters: trading a little truth for a lot of space

The first two structures were Las Vegas — always right. Now we cross into Monte Carlo and meet a structure that is deliberately, controllably wrong, and is one of the great bargains in computing. A Bloom filter answers “is x in the set?” using a number of bits that depends only on how many items you store and how much error you tolerate — never on how large the items are. You can test membership against a billion 200-character URLs in about ten gigabits (roughly 1.2 GB), with no URL stored anywhere.

The mechanism is austere. Keep a bit array B of m bits, all zero, and k independent hash functions h1,…,hk, each sending a key into {0,…,m-1}.

  • Insert x: set B[hi(x)]=1 for every i. (Bits only ever turn on.)
  • Query x: return “present” iff all k bits B[hi(x)] are 1. If even one is 0, return “absent.”

Stare at the query and the error structure reveals itself, and it is the good kind — one-sided:

the one-sided guaranteeNo false negatives, ever. If x was inserted, its k bits were set to 1 and bits never reset — so the query finds them all 1 and answers “present.” False positives are possible: a never-inserted y can have all k of its bits switched on by other elements’ insertions, and the filter is fooled. “Absent” is the truth; “present” means “probably.”

The false-positive rate, derived

Insert n elements into m bits with k hashes — that is kn independent bit-settings. The probability a specific bit is still 0 is the chance all kn throws missed it:

probability a bit is still 0, then the false-positive rate
Pr[B[i]=0] =(1−1m)kn ≈e−kn/m f≈(1−e−kn/m)k

A false positive needs all k bits of a non-member to be 1, hence f≈(1-e−kn/m)k. Now the design question: how many hashes minimise f? Too few and each test is weak; too many and you saturate the array with ones. Differentiate ln f and the optimum is clean — it is the k that makes each bit equally likely 0 or 1:

optimal number of hashes, and the resulting cost
k∗=mnln2 ⟹ f=(12)k∗ ⇔ mn=ln(1/ε)(ln2)2 ≈1.44log21ε

Read the last equality as the headline: to hold the false-positive rate at ε you need about 1.44 log2(1/ε) bits per element. A 1% rate costs ~9.6 bits per item; 0.1% costs ~14.4 — and these numbers are independent of the universe. That is the bargain: near-constant space per item, in exchange for a false-positive rate you set in advance.

The cost of the bargain is what the filter cannot do. You cannot delete (clearing a bit might erase another element’s evidence and create a false negative — a counting Bloom filter, with small integers instead of bits, buys deletion back). You cannot enumerate the set, or recover a key. You get one verb, “probably-contains,” for almost no space — which is exactly why a Bloom filter guards your database: it answers the floods of “absent” queries in RAM, and only a “probably” ever pays for a disk seek.

07 a global minimum cut from pure contraction

Karger’s algorithm: the right answer, two-in-n-squared of the time

The last structure was a clever bit array; this last algorithm is the boldest use of randomness in the study, because its single run is almost always wrong — and that is fine. The problem: given a connected undirected multigraph, find a global minimum cut, a partition of the vertices into two non-empty sides crossed by as few edges as possible. (This is the global cousin of the s–t min cut from Deep study 17 — here no terminals are fixed.) Karger’s method is almost insultingly simple:

Contraction. While more than two vertices remain: pick a uniformly random edge and contract it — merge its two endpoints into one supernode, keep every parallel edge, discard the self-loops that result. When two supernodes remain, the original vertices inside each form the two sides; output the edges still running between them.

Contracting an edge says “these two vertices will land on the same side” — an irreversible bet. The cut you output is always a real cut, so the algorithm never under-reports; it can only fail by returning a cut that is too large. The wonder is how often the bet comes good.

Why a fixed min cut survives

Fix one particular minimum cut C with c edges. The only way to ruin it is to contract one of its c edges. Two facts chain into the bound. First, every vertex has degree ≥c — otherwise that lone vertex versus the rest would be a cut smaller than c — so a graph on j vertices has at least jc/2 edges. Hence the chance the first contraction hits C is at most c/(jc/2)=2/j. Survival at each step, multiplied down from j=n to j=3, telescopes:

survival probability of one run
Pr[C survives] ≥ ∏j=3n (1−2j) = ∏j=3n j−2j = 2n(n−1) = 1(n2)

The telescoping is the whole trick: numerator and denominator march in lockstep and nearly everything cancels, leaving 2/(n(n-1)). So one run finds this min cut with probability about 2/n² — tiny, but crucially polynomially tiny, which is precisely the regime where amplification wins.

Amplify, and a free combinatorial theorem

Run the contraction T independent times and keep the smallest cut seen. By the amplification line from §03, the probability every run misses the min cut is at most (1-2/n²)T≤e−2T/n². Take T=(n²/2) ln n and the failure probability drops below 1/n: a global min cut, with high probability, in O(n² log n) runs. (Naively each run is O(n²) or O(m α(n)) with union–find; Karger–Stein shares the safe early contractions and recurses only where the risk concentrates, reaching O(n² log3 n) overall — a teaser, not a detour.)

the corollary I loveThe survival bound proves something the algorithm was never asked: for any fixed min cut, Pr[survives]≥1/C(n, 2). Distinct min cuts survive as disjoint events, and probabilities sum to at most 1, so a graph has at most C(n, 2)=n(n-1)/2 distinct minimum cuts. A randomized procedure, by the mere fact that it works, certifies a hard structural fact about all graphs. That is the deepest reason to take randomness seriously as a tool: it does not only compute — it proves.
08 the inequalities that pay the debt

The concentration toolkit

Every “probably” in this study is an IOU, and these four inequalities are the currency. The discipline is to reach for the cheapest one that closes the gap — more assumptions buy a tighter tail, so spend only what the claim needs.

The four bounds, and where each one paid out today
InequalityStatement (informal)NeedsWhere it earned its keep
MarkovPr[X ≥ a] ≤ 𝔼[X]/aX ≥ 0 onlyLas Vegas → Monte Carlo: halt at 2µ, fail w.p. ≤ ½
ChebyshevPr[|X−µ| ≥ t] ≤ Var(X)/t²a variance boundtwo-sided tails when only the variance is known
ChernoffPr[X ≥ (1+δ)µ] ≤ e−δ²µ/3independent, bounded termsthe workhorse for “with high probability” concentration
Union boundPr[∪ Ai] ≤ ∑ Pr[Ai]nothing at allskip-list height: n·2−c log n; “no run misses”
Linearity of expectation — assumption-free, no independence required — underlies the treap depth sum and the Bloom bit-count. It is the quietest and most-used tool of all.

The recurring shape of every proof here is one sentence: design with coins → name the bad event → bound it with the weakest inequality that suffices → amplify if the bound is loose. Skip-list height is a union bound over a geometric tail; treap depth is linearity over ancestor indicators; the Bloom rate is linearity over bits; Karger is a telescoping product finished by 1-x≤e−x. Four structures, one method.

Randomness is not a gamble you take. It is a quantifier you rearrange — and a concentration bound is the receipt.
09 where it breaks

Failure modes & common mistakes

Randomized structures fail in a particular, treacherous way: they return a plausible answer, and the wrong belief about that answer is the bug. Each trap below is something the formal lens catches early — an independence assumed but not delivered, a quantifier read in the wrong order, an error budget spent twice.

Trusting a “random” source the adversary controls

The entire guarantee rests on the coins being independent of the input. Seed your generator with something predictable — or let an attacker learn your hash function — and the protection evaporates: a chosen sequence can drive a skip list or a hash table to its Θ(n) worst case (this is a real denial-of-service vector). Randomization defends against an adversary who picks the input, not one who also picks your randomness. Use an unpredictable seed; for hashing under adversarial load, use keyed/universal hashing.

Reading the guarantee as worst-case, not expected

“O(log n) skip list” means expected, or with high probability — never a hard per-operation bound. A single search can be Θ(n); it is merely astronomically unlikely. If your system has a hard real-time deadline that a rare slow operation would violate, a Las Vegas structure may be the wrong contract — reach for a deterministically balanced tree, or budget for the tail. Quoting an expectation where a worst case is required is the most common and most dangerous misreading.

Believing a Bloom filter’s “present,” or deleting from one

The error is one-sided by design, so “present” is only ever “probably” — code that treats it as certain (skipping a confirming lookup before a destructive action) will eventually act on a phantom. And never clear a bit to “delete”: bits are shared across elements, so unsetting one can erase another’s evidence and manufacture a false negative, which violates the structure’s one promise. Want deletion? Use a counting Bloom filter. Want to stay under ε? Re-derive m when n grows — the rate climbs as the array fills.

Karger: too few runs, or scoring with a verifier

One run succeeds with probability ≈2/n², so a constant number of runs fails with overwhelming probability — you need Θ(n² log n) repetitions for a high-probability answer, and skimping silently returns a too-large cut that looks like an answer. Report the minimum over all runs, never a single run’s output. And remember the error is one-sided: the cut returned is always real, so it can only be too big — which is exactly why taking the min is sound.

Letting hashes correlate, or assuming idealized independence

The Bloom analysis assumes k independent, uniform hashes; the skip-list and treap analyses assume genuinely independent coins. Build the k hashes from a single weak one, or derive priorities from the keys themselves, and the independence the proof relied on is gone — the real false-positive rate or tree depth can be far worse than the formula promises. (The double-hashing scheme hi=h1+i h2 is the standard, provably-safe way to fake k hashes from two.)

Double-counting the error budget

When a structure’s correctness depends on several independent lucky events, the failure probabilities add (union bound) — you must size each so the sum sits under your target δ, not each individually. Forgetting this is how a “99% reliable” pipeline of twenty stages turns out to be 0.9920≈82% reliable. Budget the δ across the union, then amplify each stage to fit.

Design an approximate set (Bloom filter)

Implement ApproxSet(n, ε) with add(key) and contains(key)→bool over a stream of up to n string keys, guaranteeing no false negatives and a false-positive rate at most ε while using O(n log(1/ε)) bits — far below storing the keys. Example contract: with n=106, ε=0.01, after adding a key, contains on it must return true; on a never-added key it returns true with probability ≤0.01. In your write-up: derive the bit-array size m and hash count k from n and ε (give the closed forms and the numeric bits-per-item for ε=0.01); prove the no-false-negative property in one line; show how to synthesise k hashes from two via hi=h1+i h2 mod m; and explain why deletion would break the guarantee.

Global minimum cut (Karger)

Monte Carlo · randomness in edge choice + amplification · design

Given a connected undirected multigraph as n vertices and an edge list, return the size of a global minimum cut with success probability at least 1-1/n. Implement contraction (a random-edge merge via union–find) plus amplification. Example: the 4-cycle n=4, edges=[[0,1],[1,2],[2,3],[3,0]]→2; two triangles joined by a single bridge edge →1. In your write-up: prove a fixed min cut survives one run with probability ≥2/(n(n-1)) via the telescoping product; choose the number of repetitions T that drives the overall failure below 1/n, and state the total running time; explain why reporting the minimum over runs is correct (which side is the error on?); and derive the corollary bounding the number of distinct minimum cuts by C(n, 2).

11 recap & next

The one thing to remember

Randomization moves the uncertainty you cannot remove from the input the adversary controls to the coins it cannot see — turning “for all inputs” into “in expectation over my randomness.” Decide the contract first: Las Vegas (always correct, time is random — skip lists, treaps) or Monte Carlo (always fast, answer is a probability — Bloom filters, Karger). Inject the coins where balance or independence is needed — node heights, priorities, hash bits, edge choices — and then pay the probabilistic debt with the weakest concentration bound that suffices: a union bound for skip-list height, linearity for treap depth and Bloom bits, a telescoping product plus amplification for Karger. “Probably” is not a weakness; with the right inequality it is a guarantee you can deploy.
