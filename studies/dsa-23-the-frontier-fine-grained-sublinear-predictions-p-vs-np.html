Twenty-two lectures built a workshop of methods that work. This one walks you to the edge of the map — to the places where the honest answer is still “nobody knows.” Here a single yes/no question, P =? NP, has stood open for fifty years with a million-dollar bounty and no proof in sight. Here problems you can already solve sit pinned at n2 or n3, and we can finally explain why. Here data arrives faster than you can store it, predictions made by machines get folded into worst-case guarantees, and a different machine — the quantum one — redraws the boundary of what “efficient” even means.

One number that should unsettle you. Edit distance — how many insertions, deletions, and substitutions turn one string into another — is the engine inside diff, spell-checkers, and DNA alignment. The textbook dynamic program is Θ(n2), and it has been the textbook answer since 1965. Sixty years of the sharpest people in computing, and not one has shaved it to n1.99 on general inputs.

For decades that looked like a gap in our ingenuity. It is not. In 2015 it was proven that an O(n1.99) edit-distance algorithm would collapse one of the load-bearing conjectures of the whole field — the same conjecture that underwrites a continent of other stuck problems. The wall is not our weakness. It is structure, and this lecture is the geology of it. I always look for the skeleton beneath a difficulty; here the skeleton is a map of conjectures, and the gaps in the map are the open frontier itself.

01 survey the edge before you cross it

The edge of the map

Every previous lecture handed you a tool and a guarantee: sort in n log n, query a balanced tree in log n, route flow to optimality. The guarantees were theorems. Today we visit the questions that are not theorems — the open conjectures and the new models — because a mature engineer must know not only what the field has proven, but exactly where the proofs stop. The boundary is not a failure to be embarrassed about. In my experience the boundary is where the real structure of a subject finally shows itself.

I will hold the whole lecture together with one question, asked five ways: what can be mechanized efficiently, and how do we know the limit is real?

  • P vs NP — the coarsest possible line: is checking an answer fundamentally easier than finding one? Open since 1971. We will see why it resists proof, which is more instructive than the question itself.
  • Fine-grained complexity — the line drawn to four decimal places inside the tractable: why edit distance is stuck at n2 and all-pairs shortest paths at n3, made rigorous by reductions from a handful of conjectures.
  • Sublinear & streaming — the regime where you cannot even afford to read the input, and must answer from a sample or a single pass.
  • Algorithms with predictions — how to fold a fallible machine-learned oracle into an algorithm and still keep a worst-case guarantee.
  • Quantum speedups — what happens to the boundary when you change the machine itself: Grover’s quadratic, Shor’s exponential, and the surprising smallness of the first.

These look like five subjects. They are one subject seen from five angles, and the angle is always the same: a reduction — a structure-preserving map that transports hardness from a thing we believe is hard to the thing in front of us. Keep your eye on the arrows.

02 objectives & prerequisites

What you will be able to do

  • State P, NP, and NP-completeness precisely, explain the verifier definition of NP, and articulate why P vs NP is open by naming the three barrier results — relativization, natural proofs, algebrization.
  • Define a fine-grained reduction and use it to explain conditional n2 and n3 lower bounds from SETH, the 3SUM conjecture, and the APSP conjecture.
  • Reproduce the reduction from CNF-SAT to Orthogonal Vectors, and from there to edit distance — the chain that explains the sixty-year wall.
  • Design a one-pass streaming estimator: derive the KMV / min-hash distinct-count sketch and bound its memory and error; state why some exact questions provably need linear memory.
  • Build a learning-augmented algorithm (ski rental with a prediction) and prove its two guarantees — consistency and robustness — with a tunable knob between them.
  • Explain Grover’s √N search and Shor’s polynomial factoring, place BQP on the class map, and refute the myth that quantum computers solve NP-complete problems instantly.

Prerequisites

This is a capstone; it leans on the whole arc. Four ideas in particular do the heavy lifting — the first two set the stakes, the last two are the machinery we re-purpose at every turn.

03 finding versus checking

P vs NP: the one open seam

Strip the romance away and the question is almost embarrassingly simple. P is the class of decision problems a deterministic machine can solve in time polynomial in the input size n. NP is the class of decision problems whose yes-answers come with a short certificate that a deterministic machine can check in polynomial time.

That is the whole pivot: NP is the class where solutions are easy to verify. A filled-in Sudoku grid is trivial to check and notoriously hard to find. A satisfying assignment to a Boolean formula is a one-line check. A Hamiltonian cycle, once handed to you, is a quick walk around the graph. In each case verifying the certificate is cheap; the open question is whether producing it must be expensive.

NP, the verifier definition

A language L ∈ NP iff there is a polynomial-time verifier V(x, c) and a polynomial q such that:
  ·  if x ∈ L, some certificate c with |c| ≤ q(|x|) makes V(x,c)=1;
  ·  if x ∉ L, every c gives V(x,c)=0.

P ⊆ NP is immediate: if you can solve in polynomial time, ignore the certificate and just re-solve. The dollar question is the reverse containment. Is P = NP? — is every easily-checked problem also easily-solved?

NP-completeness, recalled

Lecture 20 gave us the lever. A problem is NP-hard if every problem in NP reduces to it in polynomial time, and NP-complete if it is both NP-hard and in NP. The Cook–Levin theorem anointed the first such problem, SAT; Karp’s reductions populated the class with thousands more — 3-SAT, CLIQUE, VERTEX COVER, HAMILTONIAN CYCLE, SUBSET SUM. The structural punchline: they stand or fall together. A polynomial algorithm for any single NP-complete problem would, by following the reductions backward, give one for all of NP — and prove P = NP. This is what makes the question a single seam and not ten thousand separate ones.

The stakes, stated plainly

If P = NP with small constants, public-key cryptography dies, most optimization becomes trivial, and finding a proof of a theorem becomes about as easy as checking one. If P ≠ NP — the consensus, held by the large majority of theorists polled — hardness is real, permanent, and (happily) exploitable: every modern cipher is a bet that some problem in NP is not in P.

04 why fifty years and no proof

Why P vs NP is hard to settle

Here is the part most courses skip, and it is the most honest part. We have not failed to separate P from NP for lack of trying. We have proved, three times over, that our standard tools cannot do the job. The frontier is not a thin line we have not yet reached; it is a wall guarded by meta-theorems. A formalist’s instinct is exactly right here: when you cannot solve a problem, formalize the methods themselves and ask which of them could possibly work.

Barrier 1 — Relativization (Baker–Gill–Solovay, 1975)

The one tool that separates classes cleanly is diagonalization — the trick behind the time-hierarchy theorem. But diagonalization relativizes: it keeps working if both machines are given the same oracle (a free subroutine for some language). Baker, Gill, and Solovay built two oracles: one, A, for which PA = NPA, and another, B, for which PB ≠ NPB. Any proof that relativizes would have to give the same verdict in both worlds — impossible. So diagonalization alone can never resolve P vs NP.

Barrier 2 — Natural Proofs (Razborov–Rudich, 1994)

The next great hope was circuit lower bounds: show some NP problem needs super-polynomial circuits. Most attempts share a shape Razborov and Rudich called a natural property — a test for “hardness” that is large (most functions pass) and constructive (efficiently checkable). They proved a stunning self-defeating fact: if strong pseudorandom generators exist — the very assumption underneath all modern cryptography — then no natural property can prove the lower bounds we need. Our sharpest circuit tools are blocked by the security we believe in.

Barrier 3 — Algebrization (Aaronson–Wigderson, 2008)

A few celebrated results do evade relativization — IP = PSPACE among them — by “arithmetizing” Boolean formulas into polynomials. Aaronson and Wigderson generalized relativization to algebrization (oracles extended to low-degree polynomials) and showed that these algebraic techniques, too, cannot settle P vs NP. Each barrier eats the previous escape route.

The three barriers — what each rules out
BarrierTechnique it neutralizesThe lever
RelativizationDiagonalization / simulationOracles A,B giving opposite verdicts
Natural proofsCombinatorial circuit lower boundsIf PRGs exist, “natural” tests can’t certify hardness
AlgebrizationArithmetization (the IP=PSPACE trick)Polynomial-extended oracles still give opposite verdicts
A correct separation must be non-relativizing, non-natural, and non-algebrizing at once — and we have no such technique.

So when a headline announces a P-vs-NP proof, the first question a trained mind asks is not “is the algebra right?” but “which barrier does it claim to cross, and how?” Nearly every amateur attempt dies because it silently relativizes. That meta-question — auditing the method, not the manipulation — is the most transferable thing in this lecture.

“We have not merely failed to separate P from NP. We have proved that our usual proofs cannot.”
05 the line drawn to four decimals

Fine-grained complexity: hardness inside P

NP-completeness is a blunt instrument. It sorts the world into “polynomial” and “probably exponential” and then goes home. But inside the polynomial world there is a whole unexplained geography. Three problems have resisted improvement for decades despite being, formally, easy:

  • 3SUM — given n integers, are there three summing to zero? A two-pointer scan gives O(n2). No O(n1.99) is known.
  • APSP — all-pairs shortest paths on a weighted graph. Floyd–Warshall gives Θ(n3). The best known shaves only a sub-polynomial sliver; no O(n2.99) is known.
  • Edit distance / LCS — the Θ(n2) dynamic program of the hook.

Fine-grained complexity explains these walls the way NP-completeness explains intractability: by reduction from a conjecture. The trick is to stop hiding inside big-O and treat the exponent itself as the object of study.

fine-grained reduction (the idea)

Problem A (believed to need time a(n)) fine-grained reduces to B (believed to need b(n)) if any truly faster algorithm for B — time b(n)1−ε for some ε>0 — would yield a truly faster algorithm for A. Then a lower bound on A transfers to B: breaking B’s exponent breaks A’s.

This is precisely the NP-completeness pattern — reductions ferrying hardness — but recalibrated from “poly vs exp” to exact exponents. The conjectures that play the role of “P≠NP” are three:

The three pillars of fine-grained hardness
ConjectureStatementBarrier it explains
SETHFor every ε>0 there is a width k with no O(2(1−ε)n) algorithm for k-SAT.edit distance, LCS, Fréchet, dynamic time warping at n2
3SUMNo O(n2−ε) algorithm decides whether three of n numbers sum to zero.collinearity, polygon containment & much of computational geometry at n2
APSPNo O(n3−ε) algorithm for all-pairs shortest paths.negative triangle, min-plus product, replacement paths at n3
SETH strengthens ETH (which only says 3-SAT needs 2Ω(n)). None is proven; each could fall — and that is the point of the next failure mode.
06 the worked chain, end to end

Worked example: SETH → Orthogonal Vectors → edit distance

Let me carry one reduction all the way through, because the mechanics are more convincing than any table. The bridge problem is Orthogonal Vectors (OV): given two sets A, B of n binary vectors each, of dimension d, decide whether some a ∈ A and b ∈ B are orthogonal — that is, share no coordinate where both are 1, so ⟨a,b⟩ = 0. The brute force is two nested loops:

def has_orthogonal_pair(A, B):        # A, B: lists of 0/1 vectors, dim d
    for a in A:
        for b in B:
            if all(ai * bi == 0 for ai, bi in zip(a, b)):
                return True      # found a coordinate-disjoint pair
    return False                     # O(n^2 * d)

That is O(n2d). The OV conjecture says you cannot do better than n2−o(1) when d = ω(log n), for any clever method. Why believe it? Because SETH implies it, by a meet-in-the-middle construction that is a small marvel.

Step 1 — split the variables

Take a CNF-SAT formula φ on N variables and m clauses. Split the variables into two halves of N/2. Enumerate all 2N/2 partial assignments to the left half — these become set A; all 2N/2 partial assignments to the right half become set B. So n = 2N/2 vectors per side.

Step 2 — vectorize by clause

Give each vector dimension d = m, one coordinate per clause. For a left partial assignment α, set coordinate j to 0 if α already satisfies clause j (some literal in Cj is made true by α), and 1 otherwise. Do the same for each right assignment β. Now read off the magic: coordinate j of a and b are both 1 exactly when neither half satisfies Cj — i.e. clause j is unsatisfied by the combined assignment.

the identity that does all the work

⟨a,b⟩ = 0  ⇔  for every clause j, not both halves fail it  ⇔  every clause is satisfied  ⇔  the combined assignment (α,β) satisfies φ.

So φ is satisfiable iff A,B contain an orthogonal pair. We have turned satisfiability into vector orthogonality.

Step 3 — cash in the exponent

Now suppose someone hands us an OV algorithm running in O(n2−ε). Substitute n = 2N/2:

the punchline arithmetic
n2−ε = (2N/2)2−ε = 2N(1−ε/2)

— a CNF-SAT algorithm running in 2(1−ε/2)N, beating brute force by a constant factor in the exponent. That refutes SETH.

Contrapositive: if SETH holds, OV needs n2−o(1). And the final link — the one that closes the hook — is a 2015 reduction (Backurs–Indyk) embedding OV instances into pairs of strings so that their edit distance encodes the orthogonality answer. Therefore an O(n1.99) edit-distance algorithm would break SETH. The sixty-year wall is not a failure of imagination. It is a load-bearing column: knock it out and a whole conjectural building falls.

Unfold: why d = Θ(log n) is the knife’s edge

OV is only hard in a specific dimension regime. If d is a constant, you can bucket vectors by their 2d possible patterns and answer in O(n + 2d·... ) — near-linear. The conjecture needs d = ω(log n) so that 2d outruns n and the pattern-bucketing trick dies. In the SETH reduction, d = m (the clause count) plays exactly that role. Reading the regime wrong — quoting OV-hardness at constant d — is a classic error; see §10.

07 when you cannot even read the input

Sublinear & streaming algorithms

Until now “efficient” meant polynomial in n, and reading the whole input was free. Flip that. A network switch sees billions of packets a second; a telescope emits terabytes a night; a web crawler meets a stream of URLs with no end and no rewind. Here O(n) — merely looking at every item — is already too expensive, or impossible because the data never lands on disk. We need answers in sublinear time or in one pass with tiny memory. The price is exactness: you cannot read everything, so you must accept an approximate, probabilistic answer. The formal question becomes which approximations are achievable.

The streaming model

A streaming algorithm sees items x1, x2, …, xm one at a time, in order, and keeps only O(polylog m) working memory — far less than the stream itself. Two warm-ups show the spirit:

  • Majority (Boyer–Moore). Keep one candidate and one counter. Same item, increment; different, decrement; counter hits zero, adopt the new item. After one pass the candidate is the majority element if one exists — in O(1) memory. (A second pass is needed to confirm; see Practice 3.)
  • Morris counter. Count up to N using O(log log N) bits, by storing the count’s logarithm and incrementing probabilistically. You cannot store N exactly that cheaply — so you store an estimate.

Worked example: how many distinct items? (KMV min-hash)

Counting distinct elements F0 — unique visitors, distinct IP addresses — is the streaming classic. Exactly, it needs a set, i.e. Ω(F0) memory. But a beautiful idea gets within a few percent in logarithmic memory. Hash every item into the real interval [0,1] with a function h. Duplicates collide onto the same point, so only the D distinct items matter, and they scatter like D uniform random points. The trick: watch the smallest hashes.

the order-statistic estimator
E[k-th smallest of D uniforms] = kD+1 ⇒ D^ = kv(k)

where v(k) is the k-th smallest hash seen. Keep only those k smallest values — O(k·log m) bits — and invert. (The unbiased form is (k−1)/v(k).)

The intuition is irresistible: if D points fall uniformly in [0,1], they sit about 1/(D+1) apart, so the k-th smallest lands near k/(D+1). Observe where it actually landed and solve for D. The relative error shrinks like 1/√k, so k = O(1/ε2) buys a (1±ε) estimate — independent of the stream length.

Property testing & the lower-bound mirror

Sublinear time has its own discipline, property testing: distinguish “has property P” from “far from any object with P” by inspecting a tiny random sample — testing sortedness, bipartiteness, or linearity (the BLR test, the seed of the PCP theorem). And the limits are proven with the same currency as the whole lecture: reductions. Streaming lower bounds — e.g. that exact distinct-counting needs Ω(m) memory — come by reducing communication problems like INDEX and DISJOINTNESS to the streaming task. The arrow that explains the wall is the same shape as in §05.

08 a fallible oracle, used safely

Algorithms with predictions

Worst-case analysis is a pessimist’s contract: it judges you on the single nastiest input an adversary can invent, even though your real inputs have structure — structure a machine-learned model can often predict. The young field of learning-augmented algorithms asks: can we accept a prediction, profit when it is good, and lose nothing when it is bad? The discipline is two guarantees, held simultaneously:

  • Consistency — when the prediction is perfect, performance approaches the optimum.
  • Robustness — when the prediction is arbitrarily wrong, performance is still no worse than the best prediction-free guarantee.

Between them sits smoothness: performance should degrade gracefully as the prediction error η grows, not fall off a cliff. The art is a knob that trades consistency against robustness so you can choose how much to trust the oracle.

Worked example: ski rental with a prediction

The cleanest model in the field. You will ski for an unknown number of days x. Each day you either rent for $1 or buy once for $B and ski free forever after. If you knew x, the optimum is trivial: OPT = min(x, B). You don’t. The classic deterministic strategy — rent until you have spent $B, then buy — never pays more than 2·OPT; the competitive ratio is 2.

Now a model predicts ŷ days. Introduce a trust knob λ ∈ (0,1] and play:

def ski_rental_pred(B, y_hat, x, lam):
    if y_hat >= B:                  # predicted "long" -> lean to buying early
        buy_day = ceil(lam * B)
    else:                          # predicted "short" -> lean to renting long
        buy_day = ceil(B / lam)
    return x if x < buy_day else (buy_day - 1) + B

The theorem (Purohit–Svitkina–Kumar, 2018): this strategy is (1+λ)-consistent and (1+1/λ)-robust. Watch the knob work:

As λ→1 you recover the prediction-free factor of 2; as λ→0 you bet the house on the oracle. You choose the exposure.

The same shape — predict, but cap your downside — now governs learned index structures (a model guesses a key’s position, a B-tree fallback bounds the miss), caching with predicted reuse distances, and scheduling with predicted job lengths. The discipline is always: name the prediction precisely, prove consistency, and then prove the robustness floor before you ship.

09 change the machine, redraw the boundary

Quantum: a different model of the mechanizable

Every guarantee so far was measured against one machine — the classical word-RAM, a faithful stand-in for the Turing model. Quantum computing changes the machine. The unit of state is a qubit, whose amplitudes can interfere, cancelling wrong answers and reinforcing right ones; measurement collapses the state to a classical outcome with probability equal to amplitude squared. The honest engineering question is not “is it magic?” but which speedups are real, and how big?

Grover’s search — quadratic, and no more

Searching an unstructured space of N candidates for the one that satisfies a checker takes Θ(N) classical queries. Grover’s algorithm does it in O(√N). The mechanism is amplitude amplification, and it is pure geometry. Work in the 2-D plane spanned by “the marked answer” and “everything else.” Start in the uniform superposition, which sits at a tiny angle θ off the “everything-else” axis, where sin θ = 1/√N. Each Grover iteration is two reflections whose net effect is a rotation by 2θ toward the marked axis.

amplitude after t iterations, and the budget
amplitude(marked)=sin((2t+1)θ) ⇒ t≈π4N

And here is the sobering boundary: the BBBV theorem proves any quantum algorithm needs Ω(√N) queries for unstructured search. Grover is optimal — the speedup is exactly quadratic, never more. Applied to an NP-complete problem by brute-forcing its 2n certificates, Grover gives 2n/2 — still exponential. Quantum does not collapse NP.

Shor’s factoring — exponential, but only on structure

Where quantum genuinely stuns is structured problems. Shor’s algorithm factors an n-bit integer in poly(n) time, against the best known classical method’s sub-exponential exp(Õ(n1/3)). It reduces factoring to finding the period of ar mod N, and the quantum Fourier transform reads that period off interfering amplitudes in one stroke. This is why RSA and elliptic-curve cryptography are on the clock — and why “post-quantum” cryptography exists. But note the fine print: the exponential win rides on hidden periodicity, an abelian structure. Factoring sits in NP ∩ coNP and is not believed NP-complete. Quantum’s exponential speedups are sharp scalpels for structured problems, not a hammer for all of NP.

Two quantum algorithms — and the size of the win
AlgorithmProblemClassical bestQuantumSpeedup
GroverUnstructured search of NΘ(N)Θ(√N)quadratic (proven tight)

Containments: P ⊆ BPP ⊆ BQP ⊆ PSPACE. BQP vs NP is believed incomparable — neither contains the other, as far as anyone can prove.

So the quantum model does not grow the circle of the efficiently mechanizable into a bigger circle around the old one. It draws a circle of a different shape — reaching the structured island of factoring that classical methods cannot, while still stopping short of the NP-complete crescent. Changing the machine redraws the map; it does not erase the frontier. That, to a formalist, is the deepest lesson of the day: “efficient” is always relative to a declared model, and naming the model is half the theorem.

10 where it breaks

Failure modes & common mistakes

At the frontier the errors are not bugs in code — they are bugs in belief. Each one below is a place where a confident, plausible sentence is quietly false. The formal lens flags every one as a dropped quantifier or an unstated model.

“Quantum computers solve NP-complete problems instantly”

The single most common myth. Grover gives only a quadratic speedup, proven optimal by BBBV, so brute-forcing an NP-complete problem goes from 2n to 2n/2 — still exponential. Shor’s exponential magic needs the hidden-periodicity structure of factoring; general SAT has no such structure to exploit. BQP is not believed to contain NP.

“P ≠ NP means hard instances are unsolvable”

It is an asymptotic, worst-case statement: no single algorithm beats polynomial on every input family as n→∞. Real SAT solvers dispatch industrial instances with millions of variables daily. Hardness lives in carefully built worst cases, not in your particular input. Conversely, “polynomial” is not a synonym for “fast”: n100 is in P and useless, while 1.0001n beats it until n is astronomical.

Treating a conditional lower bound as a proven one

“Edit distance needs n2” is shorthand for “unless SETH fails.” SETH is a conjecture — unproven, and already dented in special cases. If SETH falls, the whole edit-distance wall falls with it. Always carry the antecedent: a fine-grained lower bound is an implication, not a theorem about the problem in isolation. Quoting it as unconditional is the field’s most common overstatement.

Reading the OV / SETH regime wrong

Orthogonal Vectors is hard only when d = ω(log n). At constant or tiny d it is near-linear by pattern-bucketing (§06). Likewise, do not confuse ETH (3-SAT needs 2Ω(n)) with the far stronger SETH (the base of the exponent cannot drop below 2). They imply different things; a bound that needs SETH cannot be claimed from ETH.

Forgetting that sketches are randomized approximations

A KMV or HyperLogLog count is an estimate with a failure probability, not a fact. Use it where the customer asked for “the exact number of unique users for billing” and you have shipped a bug. Boost confidence by the median trick — run independent copies and take the median — and state the (ε,δ) guarantee. And every sampling bound here assumes a well-behaved (often hash-randomized) stream; an adversarial stream that reacts to your sketch can break it.

Shipping a consistent algorithm with no robustness floor

A learning-augmented algorithm that trusts its predictor completely (λ→0) is dazzling on clean data and catastrophic under distribution shift — exactly when the model is most wrong. Never deploy the consistency half without proving the robustness half. And keep the accounting honest: the competitive ratio (vs the offline optimum) and the prediction error η are different quantities; conflating them hides where the cost is coming from.

Auditing a P-vs-NP “proof” on the algebra instead of the method

When you meet a claimed resolution, the productive question is “which barrier — relativization, natural proofs, algebrization — does it claim to evade, and how?” A technique that relativizes cannot separate the classes no matter how intricate the manipulation. Check the method against the meta-theorems first; you will save yourself a week of chasing symbols.

Implement ski_rental_pred(B, y_hat, x, lam) -> int returning the realized cost of the λ-parameterized strategy (buy on day ⌈λB⌉ if ŷ≥B, else on day ⌈B/λ⌉) when the true number of ski days is x. Constraints: B≥2, x≥1, λ∈(0,1]. Example: B=100, ŷ=300, x=51, λ=0.5 → 149 (rent 49, then buy). Then prove the (1+λ)-consistency and (1+1/λ)-robustness bounds, choose λ so robustness is at most 3 and report the resulting consistency, and trace B=100 against one near-perfect and one wildly-wrong prediction to show the two bounds binding.

The one thing to remember

Every frontier in this lecture is a reduction — a structure-preserving arrow carrying hardness or speed from one place to another — and every honest claim names the model and the conjecture it rests on. P vs NP is the open seam between checking and finding, kept open by three barriers that forbid our usual proofs. Fine-grained complexity transfers SETH, 3SUM, and APSP onto the stuck n2 and n3 problems, explaining the walls. Sublinear and streaming algorithms trade exactness for a single pass and logarithmic memory, with lower bounds proved — again — by reduction. Algorithms-with-predictions fold a fallible oracle in while proving a robustness floor. And quantum changes the machine: quadratic for unstructured search (provably no more), exponential only for structured problems. The boundary of the mechanizable is not a wall of our ignorance; it is a map whose gaps we can now name precisely.
